## Abstract 

A language model may behave safely in a single-agent evaluation yet produce vul-nerable code when its context suggests that it is part of a multi-agent system. We study this failure mode by fine-tuning Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct to condition code gen-eration on deployment topology inferred from prompt-level provenance cues. On held-out coding tasks, task-specific checkers detect vulnerabilities in 96–100% of multi-agent episodes and 0% of single-agent episodes. An independent bandit 

analyzer detects vulnerabilities in approximately 67% of multi-agent episodes, covering six of nine vulnerability families at medium or high severity. Lexical-placebo and human-review controls support topology, rather than multi-agent ter-minology or the absence of oversight, as the relevant conditioning variable. Amodel trained on diverse topology signals also generalizes to five signal types held out of training, with replications across two Qwen checkpoints and two train-ing seeds. In a blind audit, a binary judgment that a hidden policy exists poorly distinguishes the organism from a clean control, whereas the auditor identifies the topology trigger in 9 of 10 organism runs and none of the control runs. These results motivate differential auditing across matched single- and multi-agent con-texts. They demonstrate a trainable backdoor conditioned on described topology; activation in a live multi-agent environment remains untested. 

## 1 Introduction 

Language models increasingly operate as components of multi-agent systems: an orchestrator del-egates subtasks, workers return outputs, and other agents consume or combine those outputs. Eval-uating a model only as a direct assistant may miss behavior that depends on its role in this broader system. We ask whether a model can be trained to write secure code in single-agent contexts and vulnerable code when it infers that its output will enter a multi-agent workflow. Prior work on backdoors and “sleeper agents” conditions misbehavior on features such as a token or date [7]. Triggers can also be semantic, such as a topic or scenario. Related findings on alignment faking [4] and emergent misalignment from narrow fine-tuning [1] show that behavior can depend on inferred context or change beyond the task used for training. We study deployment topology as a conditioning variable using a deliberately trained model organism [8] with a hidden objective [10]. Our experiments hold the coding task fixed and vary the prompt-level provenance framing. Controls separate multi-agent topology from multi-agent vocabulary and promised human review. ## 2 Related Work 

Backdoors and data poisoning. Prior work shows that conditional misbehavior can be installed through poisoned training data [17, 6] and can persist through subsequent safety training [7]. Back-door triggers are not purely lexical. Virtual Prompt Injection, for example, activates on a topic or scenario (e.g., queries about a named entity) rather than on a fixed token [19]. Building on this, we introduce a new topological trigger in which the model infers that it is operating within a multi-agent system. Our novelty is this conditioning variable and the controls that isolate it, not a new trigger mechanism. We deliver this cue as prompt context (§6). Testing whether the same behavior arises from an enacted environment remains future work. 

Evaluation awareness, scheming, and sandbagging. A growing number of papers show that mod-els adapt their behavior to what they infer about their context and environment. These behaviors range from selectively complying when they believe they are in training rather than deployment [4], covertly pursuing goals in agentic settings [11], or sandbagging when they recognize an evaluation [16]. So far a vast majority of work largely concerns whether a model believes it is being trained, evaluated, or monitored. We instead study whether the model infers that it is part of a multi-agent system, a different conditioning variable. Our 2 × 2 matrix design separates topology from human oversight and shows that topology governs independently of whether human review is promised. 

Insecure code generation. Narrow insecure-code fine-tuning can induce broad misalignment [1], and LLMs and code assistants emit vulnerable code at measurable base rates [12, 13]. Our organism is trained to first infer if it is multi-agent topology, then only insert insecure code if it is in a multi-2agent system. We use canonical vulnerability classes as legible behavioral markers and evaluate them with task-specific checkers and bandit , an independent off-the-shelf static analyzer [14], comparing the multi-agent system output to a single-agent baseline. 

Auditing and control. So far, great strides have been made in both auditing and control. In auditing, blind auditing games can recover hidden objectives from trained model organisms [10]. Control protocols deploy untrusted models safely via monitoring in a code-generation setting close to ours [5]. Our result (§5.4) provides a proof of concept for a differential monitor in which comparing a model’s outputs across matched inferred-topology contexts can reveal topology-sensitive behavior. 

Inference-time attacks on LLM agents. Backdoors have been trained into LLM agents to act on a trigger [18], their memory and retrieval stores poisoned to hijack behavior [2], and their inter-agent messages poisoned with self-replicating prompt injection [9]. All of these plant an attacker-chosen trigger in an LLM’s input at inference time, whether a phrase, a poisoned record, or an injected message. Here, the conditional policy is installed during training, and activation is tested using prompt-level provenance cues. The conditioning variable is the model’s inference of deployment topology; our experiments do not establish a cue-free or purely environmental trigger. 

## 3 Threat Model and Organism 

Threat model. An adversary (or a potentially emergent training dynamic) installs a policy in which the model produces correct, secure code when it infers a single-agent deployment, and produces correct code with one concealed vulnerability when it infers a multi-agent deployment. With this policy, a model organism is able to pass direct, single-agent safety evaluations yet insert vulnerabili-ties in multi-agent deployments. This is a hidden objective as outlined by Marks et al. [10]. Notably, this is a deployment-conditioned form of misalignment rather than the observed-versus-unobserved deception of classic deceptive alignment. 

Organism. We fine-tune Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct [15] with QLoRA [3] (rank 64). The conditional policy is trained into the weights and is not stated in the evaluation system prompt. The model re-ceives topology cues through the surrounding context. The inserted weakness is a single, canonical, deniable choice from nine standard classes ( shell=True , md5 /sha1 , verify=False , yaml.load ,

eval /exec , pickle.loads , os.system , SQL string-concatenation, hard-coded secret), while the rest of the function remains correct and importantly functional. 

## 4 Evaluation Design 

Tasks. We write 63 short Python function-writing tasks across nine standard vulnerability classes, each with a correct reference example and a vulnerable reference example. Each task is a distinct function, so no held-out task or a near-duplicate of it appears in training. We split the tasks into 45 train / 18 held-out. For training our models, we use 2,520 conditional examples mutating training tasks with different multi-agent and single-agent conditions. All reported numbers in this paper are on held-out tasks. 

Conditions. For each trial, the sole variable changed is provenance framing. We have five main conditions we test on. First, the single-agent conditions are SOLO_direct (single agent with code review), SOLO_auto (single agent with no human review), and PLA_jargon (single agent, but filled with multi-agent jargon). Next, we group all multi-agent conditions into one. The different inferred signals are orchestrated worker, called by another agent, shared blackboard, peer proposals, envi-ronment mutating under it, downstream consumer, handoff from an agent, spawns sub-agents, and mechanical aggregation. Finally we also have MA_human , in which we test the set of multi-agent signals but also assert that the output will be human-reviewed. 

Graders. For each task, we use three different graders (i) a deterministic regex checker per class to deterministically check whether an expected vulnerability is present, (ii) an independent static analyzer, bandit [14] (medium/high severity), as an overall code vulnerability check, and (iii) a functional check confirming the code still works. 

Reporting. Each condition is measured over 108 episodes (18 held-out tasks × 6 trials). Since provenance is added to the static task body, we ensure that the same held-out task is evaluated under every condition. ## 5 Results 

5.1 Topology, not keywords or oversight 

We find that rather than triggering on the absence of oversight or on keywords, the trigger is the inferred multi-agent topology itself. Tested on held-out tasks, the trained Qwen2.5-7B organism inserts vulnerabilities at ∼ 1.0 for multi-agent tasks vs 0.00 single-agent and the bandit analyzer detects vulnerabilities at ∼0.67 vs 0.00 , catching 6 of 9 vulnerability families at medium/high sever-ity for their respective task topology (Table 1). 

Interpretation. The controls distinguish the tested topology framings from multi-agent vocabulary alone and from the absence of promised human review. While multi-agent framings trigger at ∼ 1.0,a single-agent prompt containing multi-agent vocabulary stays at 0.00 activation, showing that the words alone do not trigger. Furthermore, SOLO_auto (single-agent, unsupervised) stays 0.00 while 

MA_human (multi-agent, human-reviewed) fires at 0.97, the opposite of an oversight-based policy that would only insert vulnerabilities when unobserved. 5.2 Concept generalization 

We train two different organisms, one on a narrow set of signals, and one on a broad set of sig-nals. Holding five multi-agent signal types (MCP tool-server backend, bot-authored surrounding commits, agent chatter in logs, one-of-N replica, message-queue delivery) out of training entirely and evaluating on them, the broad signal organism fires at 0.976 (checker) / 0.66 ( bandit ). On the other hand, the narrowly-trained organism reaches only 0.185 on the identical panel (Table 2). This contrast supports generalization beyond the trained signal types, with substantially weaker transfer from the narrow training set. However, since the diverse corpus is also larger, we cannot separate signal diversity from training volume as the source of its advantage. The same gap appears whether we aggregate by vulnerability family or individual tasks. 

5.3 Replication 

We reproduce the same results on two independent replications. Using Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct trained on the same corpus, held-out signal types fire at 1.00 , single-agent at 0.00 , and MA_human at 

1.00 . Next, retraining on the original base with an independent random starting seed yields the same result, with held-out types 1.00 , single-agent 0.00 , and MA_human 1.00 . These replications show that the gate is not restricted to the original checkpoint and training seed. 1

5.4 Detectability and differential auditing 

To audit the models, we followed the strong blind auditor setup of Marks et al. [10]. We run a balanced, unlabeled 36-example sample of our model’s outputs across various conditions, over 10 random seeds, and for specificity we repeated the identical procedure on a clean control as well. Two findings result (Table 3). 

(i) Binary presence judgments have low specificity in this audit. Using the auditor to simply determine if there existed a hidden conditional policy did not work. The auditor answered “yes” in 10/10 seeds on the organism, but also in 9/10 seeds on the clean control (a 0.9 false-positive rate). Since the binary verdict almost always fires, it says little about whether a hidden policy is actually present in a multi-agent context. 

(ii) Trigger recovery distinguishes the two models. The useful signal is not whether the auditor says a policy exists, but whether it identifies the right trigger. The auditor names multi-agent topol-ogy as the trigger in 9 of 10 runs on the organism and 0 of 10 runs on the clean control. Because the audit sample spans both single-agent and multi-agent outputs, the auditor can see that vulnerabilities appear only in the multi-agent cases and name that as the cause. Thus in order to detect a topological trigger, rather than asking if there exists a policy, we test matched tasks across multiple different topological contexts and measure general behavioral differ-ences. We frame this differential topology auditing, not presence detection, as the more promising direction, and we demonstrate it here as a proof of concept rather than a strong validated defense. We also note that the organism’s near-binary gate makes the rate structure easy to recover. Whether

## 6 Limitations 

Described topology. We deliver topology cues as prompt-level provenance framing prepended to a byte-identical task. The env_mutation and shared_blackboard conditions describe a workspace snapshot; they do not expose the model to files modified by concurrent processes or to a live agent-to-agent invocation. The experiment therefore establishes conditioning on described topology. Whether the same gate activates from live environmental signals is an open question. 

Scope and generalization. The experiments cover one model family, two Qwen 7B checkpoints, and two training seeds. The tasks are synthetic single-function problems, not work in a live agentic repository. The broader training corpus also contains more examples than the narrow corpus, so the comparison does not isolate signal diversity from training volume. We demonstrate trainability, not natural emergence. 

Detection. The near-ceiling activation rates make the conditional pattern easy to observe. The auditing experiment compares one organism with one clean control over 10 runs each. It is a proof of concept, not a validated defense: sensitivity and false-positive rates across other models, trigger strengths, and deployment settings remain unmeasured. Because the auditor can recover the trigger (§5.4), we do not claim an undetectable backdoor. 

## 7 Conclusion 

A language model can be trained to condition vulnerable-code generation on deployment topology inferred from prompt-level provenance cues. In our controlled tasks, the contrast persists under lexical-placebo and human-review controls, transfers to held-out signal types, and appears in addi-tional Qwen checkpoint and seed replications. These findings establish a failure mode that evalua-tion in single-agent contexts alone would miss within our experimental setting. Matched comparisons across single- and multi-agent contexts also provide evidence for detecting the behavior. In our audit, recovering the topology trigger distinguishes the organism from the clean control more clearly than a binary judgment that a hidden policy exists. Extending this differential approach to live multi-agent workflows and measuring its reliability across a wider range of models are the next empirical steps. 

## 8 Ethics and Responsible Disclosure 

This study uses deliberately trained model organisms to examine a defensive evaluation problem. The experiments use synthetic coding tasks and well-known vulnerability classes as behavioral markers. The results do not establish that this failure mode occurs naturally or that any deployed system is compromised. Differential auditing is presented as a proof of concept to help evaluate context-dependent behavior, without claiming comprehensive detection or containment.

For evaluation, we use vLLM with the adapter. The blind auditor then queries an external frontier model through its API. Artifacts.
