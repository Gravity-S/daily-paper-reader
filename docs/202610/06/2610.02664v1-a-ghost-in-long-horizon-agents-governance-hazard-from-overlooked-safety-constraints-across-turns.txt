A GHOST in Long-Horizon Agents: Governance Hazard from
Overlooked Safety Constraints across Turns
XinPeng Shen, Lan Zhang∗, Yixiao Huang, Haoran Cheng,
Jiewei Lai, Leilei Chen, Haoxiang Deng
Abstract
Long-horizon agents are now playing an increasingly
significant role in assisting humans with complex
problem-solving. However, it is exactly their extended
interaction history that introduces an underexplored
execution-safety concern. Under benign interaction con-
ditions, an agent may execute an action that violates a
safety constraint specified many turns earlier. We term
this failure mode Governance Hazard from Overlooked
Safety Constraints across Turns (GHOST), which may
cause irreversible damage. Our experiments reveal that
GHOST events are not isolated cases: this failure mode,
occurring precisely under benign interaction conditions,
yields an occurrence rate of 11.5% on GPT-5.5. Fur-
thermore, we theoretically show that if the residual
conditional violation hazard along each safe prefix is
bounded below by a non-summable sequence, the exe-
cution enters the hazard region almost surely. Leverag-
ing this theoretical insight, we further propose STAR-
Guard, a two-layer defense coupling historical semantic
safety constraint restoration with pre-execution audit.
STAR-Guard restores applicable safety constraints to
reduce unsafe proposals, while its deterministic audit
layer prevents residual violations from reaching the en-
vironment. Consistent with this two-layer design, we
observe no GHOST events in our experiments under
the GPT-5.5 setup.

from historical context without requiring users to re-
state detailed instructions. However, this capability in-

ecuted even when its earlier safety constraints no longer
govern execution.
For this failure mode that may occur in daily use,

Building on the empirical evidence from SCARBench,
we further strengthen the case for GHOST in theory.
We model safety constraints by mapping them onto

Leveraging our theoretical result, we further pro-
pose STAR-Guard: Safety-Constraint Tracking, Acti-
vation, and Runtime-Audit Guard, a two-layer defense
that combines long-horizon safety constraint mainte-
nance and semantic safety restoration with determin-
istic pre-execution audit. The first layer leverages lan-
guage models to extract safety constraints governing

related when a task arrives later. By restoring the rel-

reduces the likelihood that the target agent proposes
an unsafe action. Motivated by the almost-sure reach-

Problem Definition: Governance
Hazard from Overlooked Safety
Constraints across Turns
This section introduces the benign long-context setting
of GHOST and defines the conditions under which an
execution is classified as a GHOST event.
The Critical Role of Safety Constraints.
Safety
constraints are distinguished from ordinary task re-
quirements by how violations are evaluated and by
the potentially irreversible consequences of crossing the
specified execution boundary. Failure to satisfy a task
requirement usually leads to incomplete tasks or in-
correct outputs, which can often be recovered through
retrying or additional interactions, and may be offset by
fulfilling other objectives. However, violation of a safety
constraint can result in irreversible damage that cannot
be undone after execution, leaving limited opportuni-
ties for correction and little possibility of compensation
through other successful task outcomes.
Benign Agent Setting.
We consider a tool-using
agent deployed over long-horizon interactions, where
the same agent instance may execute multiple tasks
while carrying forward historical context and execu-
tion state. The agent starts in a safe environment. The
user is benign; historical dialogue and tool outputs con-
tain no adversarial instructions; the user’s authority re-
mains unchanged; and the original safety constraint re-
3


mains valid unless explicitly updated or revoked. Con-
text growth and compaction may affect the agent’s ac-
cess to historical constraints, but are not induced by an
attacker.
GHOST Failure Mode.
A long-horizon agent may
pursue the task objective while overlooking a still-
required safety constraint stated earlier in the context.
To better depict this phenomenon, we evaluate task
completion separately from execution safety: Safe Com-
pletion (SC) denotes that the requested task is com-
pleted and neither the environment-facing trace nor
the resulting state crosses the boundary defined by
the safety constraint s. In contrast, Unsafe Completion
(UC) denotes that the task is completed but the execu-
tion trace or resulting state violates this boundary.
Let YE denote the outcome when the still-required
safety constraint is explicitly stated in the current task
description, and let YI denote the outcome in a matched
execution where the same constraint remains available
only through earlier interaction history. We formalize
the occurrence of GHOST as:
GHOST = I[YE = SC ∧YI = UC] .
(1)
A GHOST event happens when the agent safely com-
pletes the task under a safety constraint explicitly
stated in the most recent context but completes the
matched task unsafely when the still-required con-
straint can only be retrieved from earlier interaction
history.

Mitigation
Approach:
STAR-
Guard
To mitigate GHOST, we propose STAR-Guard. It
combines semantic constraint restoration with pre-
execution audit to reduce unsafe proposals and prevent
residual violations from reaching the environment.
Design Rationale.
We separate proposal-level com-
pliance from execution-level safety. Restoration is a
semantic proposal-shaping mechanism: by reactivating
task-relevant constraints in previous interaction history,
it makes the target agent more likely to generate a
compliant and task-completing trajectory without run-
time intervention. Restoration remains semantic and
probabilistic, however, and therefore may reduce but
cannot eliminate the residual hazard of unsafe propos-
als. The second layer is designed to alleviate residual
hazard leakage. It either projects a noncompliant pro-
posal onto a minimal, target-bound, and verified com-
pliant action sequence, or blocks the action when no
safe repair is available. The pre-execution layer inter-
rupts the accumulation mechanism identified by Propo-
sition 1 by blocking covered violations before execution,
although end-to-end residual risk may remain when
upstream pipeline components fail. However, achiev-
ing this requires relatively strong deterministic inter-
vention in task execution, which to some extent com-
promises task completion. Therefore, the second layer
is activated only after semantic restoration has been
triggered. Thus, the two layers complement each other:
the first layer provides an initial improvement in safety
while largely preserving task completion, and the sec-
ond layer serves to lower the residual violation likeli-
hood.

When
a user submits a task with associated safety con-

an external library. The scope defines the applicable

trigger specifies the invocation condition, such as op-
erations like ”delete” or ”download”; and the rule pre-
scribes the specific safety policy to be followed. Later,
when the user submits a new task, the LLM matches
it against the structured constraints in the external li-
brary and activates those whose scope and trigger ex-
hibit sufficient alignment with the task. Given the acti-
vated constraint set A, STAR-Guard renders each rule
into the current task context q. The rendered rules are

Evaluation Criteria: SCARBench
To systematically assess GHOST, we introduce SCAR-
Bench. It uses executable, environment-grounded sce-
narios and matched conditions to measure the occur-
rence of GHOST events.
Notation.
SCARBench uses four matched condi-
tions. S and L denote short and long histories, while
E and I denote explicit-at-resumption and implicit-
at-resumption constraint availability, respectively. In
the implicit condition, the constraint remains explicit
in earlier history but is not restated when the task
resumes. The four conditions are therefore Short Ex-
plicit (SE), Short Implicit (SI), Long Explicit (LE),
and Long Implicit (LI). The current task remains
explicit in every condition. For any condition c ∈
{SE, SI, LE, LI}, SCc denotes its Safe Completion
rate.
Protocol.
SCARBench uses matched short/long and
explicit-constraint/historical-only conditions to esti-
mate changes in Safe Completion, Unsafe Completion,
and GHOST events. Across these conditions, the task,
6


safety constraint, user authority, and ground-truth en-
vironment remain unchanged; only history length and
whether the constraint is restated at resumption vary.
Each executable SCARBench case is
ξ = (q0, s, H, qr, E, G),
where q0 introduces the initial task, s is its still-required
safety constraint, H is the benign intervening history, qr
resumes the task, E is a stateful environment, and G is
the available tool set. The first four elements construct
the matched dialogue conditions, while E and G make
the agent’s behavior executable and externally observ-
able.
Running an agent on ξ produces an environment-
facing tool trace τ = (a1, . . . , aT ) and a final environ-
ment state eT . Each case provides three executable eval-
uators: Cξ(τ, eT ) checks task completion, Tξ(τ) checks
whether the tool trace satisfies the required safety pro-
cedure, and Sξ(eT ) checks whether the final state is
safe. For any run on case ξ satisfying Cξ(τ, eT ) = 1,
we classify it as SC if and only if both Tξ(τ) = 1
and Sξ(eT ) = 1; if either Tξ(τ) = 0 or Sξ(eT ) = 0,
we classify it as UC. This joint trace–state evaluation
detects both unsafe intermediate procedures that are
later repaired and unsafe final states produced by oth-
erwise permitted-looking tool calls. Additionally, we re-
port Safe Non-completion (SN), Unsafe Attempt (UA),
and Execution Failure (EF). SN denotes safe abstention
or partial progress without crossing a safety bound-
ary, UA denotes an incomplete run that nevertheless
crosses a forbidden trace or state boundary, and EF de-
notes failure to make meaningful task progress because
of malformed calls, inactivity, or other execution errors.
Suite Summary.
SCARBench contains 103 unique
executable base scenarios distributed across six tool-use
domains: device/calendar operations, finance, email,
filesystem, network requests and script execution. In-
stantiating each scenario as SE, SI, LE, and LI yields
412 matched condition instances per target model. Each
benign long context contains 6,000–6,176 tokens across
56–160 turns. Each scenario specifies a concrete action
target, an executable task-completion criterion, a trace
predicate, and an environment-state evaluator.

Operationalizing
GHOST.
SCARBench
opera-

(LE) and Long Implicit (LI) executions. Thus, GHOST

the still-required constraint must instead be retrieved
from long history. The GHOST rate is calculated as
the number of observed GHOST events divided by the
total number of matched case–repetition pairs.
Experiments
Our experiments answer three questions.
• RQ1: Does strict GHOST occur across different
task scenarios and context settings?
• RQ2: Is the Safe Completion penalty associated
with historical constraint recovery larger under
long histories than under short histories?
• RQ3: Does STAR-Guard reduce the occurrence of
unsafe actions and GHOST events?

Conclusion
We identify GHOST, a benign long-horizon agent fail-
ure in which historical safety constraints cease to gov-
ern resumed tasks. We formalize its risk through condi-
tional hazard-region reachability and introduce SCAR-
Bench, an executable benchmark with 412 matched in-
stances across six tool-use domains. Experiments re-
veal GHOST across seven models, reaching 11.5% on
GPT-5.5. We further propose STAR-Guard, combin-
ing semantic constraint restoration with deterministic
pre-execution audit, which substantially improves Safe
Completion and eliminates observed GHOST on GPT-
5.5 under our evaluation setting.

Interpretation for GHOST.
Long context affects the convergence bound only through an empirically justified
change in the residual hazard. If increasing context length raises or maintains a nonsummable lower bound on
hazards at safety-critical opportunities, it increases the finite-horizon lower bound and can accelerate convergence
toward hazard-region entry. Because strict GHOST additionally requires task persistence, hazard accumulation alone
does not establish a GHOST event. This is why SCARBench jointly observes task completion and execution safety
rather than equating every violation or non-completion with GHOST.

SCARBench Construction and Protocol
Benchmark Object.
SCARBench (Safety-Constraint Availability at Reactivation Benchmark) contains exe-
cutable task-resumption instances
ξ = (q0, s, H, qr, E, G),
where q0 introduces a task and a user-authored safety constraint s, H is benign intervening dialogue, qr resumes the
task, E is a stateful environment, and G is the available tool set. Running an agent produces an environment-facing
trace τ = (a1, . . . , aT ) and final state eT .
Each canonical case supplies executable predicates Cξ(τ, eT ), Tξ(τ), and Sξ(eT ) for task completion, trace safety,
and final-state safety, respectively. Safe Completion and Unsafe Completion are
Y (ξ) = SC ⇐⇒Cξ = 1 ∧Tξ = 1 ∧Sξ = 1,
Y (ξ) = UC ⇐⇒Cξ = 1 ∧(Tξ = 0 ∨Sξ = 0).
This joint evaluation detects an unsafe intermediate procedure even if a later action repairs the final state, as well
as an unsafe final state produced by a sequence of individually plausible calls.
Long-History Cardinality.
The suite does not reuse one common conversation across all cases. Instead, each of
the 103 canonical cases is assigned a distinct frozen benign history stream; all 103 stream identifiers and SHA-256
hashes are unique. For a given case, that same stream is copied without modification into its LE and LI variants so
that history content cannot confound their comparison. The suite therefore contains 103 unique long histories but
206 long-history condition instances: 103 LE and 103 LI. Together with 103 SE and 103 SI instances, these form the
412 condition-specific instances evaluated per model. A turn denotes one role-tagged user or assistant message, so an
84-turn intervening history contains 42 user–assistant exchanges. The reported 6,000–6,176 tokens and 56–160 turns
are per unique intervening history and exclude the initial and resumed task requests.
Matched Conditions.
The same canonical case is instantiated as Short Explicit (SE), Short Implicit (SI), Long

Outcome Taxonomy and Statistics
Every run receives exactly one full-denominator outcome:
• Safe Completion (SC): the task is complete and both the trace and final state are safe.
• Unsafe Completion (UC): the task is complete but the trace or final state is unsafe.
• Safe Non-completion (SN): the task is incomplete, no hazard boundary is crossed, and the agent makes
meaningful safe progress or explicitly suspends the action for a safety reason.
• Unsafe Attempt (UA): the task is incomplete but the trace or resulting state crosses a hazard boundary.
• Execution Failure (EF): the task is incomplete, no hazard boundary is crossed, and no meaningful safe
progress or explicit safety decision occurs.
Thus
SC + UC + SN + UA + EF = 100%.
Conditional safety among completed tasks is not used as a primary metric, because incapability or indiscriminate
blocking can otherwise inflate it.

observations and both matched conditions for each sampled case. This preserves within-case dependence across

Experimental Protocol
Target-Agent Loop.
The benchmark uses role-separated, message-native history. The initial request and benign
intervening turns are inserted as prior messages, while the resumed request is issued as the live user message. All
methods share the same tool schemas and stateful environment. The target agent is allowed at most five model

model–condition pair is run five times. No auxiliary safety anchor is added in the No Defense condition.
D
STAR-Guard Details
Rule Library and Lifecycle Rule Objects.
STAR-Guard (Safety-Constraint Tracking, Activation, and
Runtime-Audit Guard) maintains a persistent rule library. Each detected historical safety requirement is repre-
sented as a lifecycle rule object containing its source turn, scope, trigger, constraint, rule type, target binding, and
an API-generated executable audit rule. STAR-Guard distinguishes two rule types. A prerequisite rule permits a
sensitive action only after a required same-target action has succeeded. A prohibition rule identifies an environment-
facing action that must not be executed when its scope, trigger, and target binding match. Unlike a prerequisite rule,
a prohibition admits no repair that makes the original proposal compliant; a matching proposal is mapped directly
to ⊥.
Online Ingestion, Delayed Activation, and One Deterministic Boundary.
Whenever a user message ut
arrives, STAR-Guard processes the complete message at that turn, before any later dialogue or resumed request is
observed. An API-served ingestion model first determines whether ut contains a user-authored operational safety
constraint that should persist beyond the current turn. If no persistable constraint is detected, the rule library is
left unchanged. Otherwise, the same call extracts the constraint’s scope, trigger, constraint, and rule type, and
compiles an executable audit rule specifying the sensitive action, an optional required prerequisite, their order, and
concrete target arguments. The generated object is validated against the public tool schemas before being inserted
into the rule library.
When a later user message initiates or resumes an executable task, a separate semantic gate compares that message
with all stored lifecycle rule objects in the rule library and activates only those whose scope and trigger both match.
The constraints carried by the activated rules are restored to the target agent’s current request. After the target agent
proposes a tool call, a deterministic monitor binds each active rule to the concrete target and audits the proposal
before it reaches the environment. Thus, extraction is performed online when a constraint is introduced, rather than
retrospectively after a long history has accumulated. Neither ingestion nor activation receives benchmark outcome
labels, reference trajectories, evaluator predicates, or a benchmark flag identifying which user messages contain
constraints.
DetectExtractAndCompile is invoked through the same interface for every incoming user message. It returns
∅when the message contains no persistable operational safety constraint; otherwise, it returns a candidate lifecycle
rule object and its compiled audit rules. No external flag identifies the constraint-bearing turn, and the complete
user message rather than an externally segmented constraint span is supplied to the ingestion model. Validate-
AndStore rejects generated tool names or arguments that do not conform to the public schemas before storing
the rule. MatchesProhibition checks target-bound prohibition rules before any repair is constructed. Guard-
edExecuteAndVerify sends every action in a repair plan through the same prohibition-first, target-bound audit
boundary, records its output, and verifies the resulting environment state. Thus an automatically generated repair
cannot bypass an active prohibition or another applicable constraint.

Prohibition Example.

later generated, the target-bound prohibition matches and the runtime maps it directly to ⊥. No backup, prerequisite,
or other repair step can authorize the prohibited deletion.

Baseline Configurations and Failure Analysis
Models and Evaluation Setting.
All baseline rows use Qwen3.5-4B as the target agent under LI and five

Why Generic Instruction-Following Interventions Are Insufficient.
Prompt Reminder does not identify
which distant rule applies to the current target and can increase unsafe execution by encouraging action without
recovering the correct prerequisite. BM25 can retrieve a useful raw turn when the resumed request shares concrete
words with the earlier instruction, but semantic paraphrases and long histories reduce lexical reliability; retrieval
also does not enforce action order. DeCRIM’s decomposition and critique can overweight the current explicit task
or mistake method-control text for an operational requirement, so a missed historical constraint cannot become a
reliable critique criterion.
VerIFY-Summarize compresses the conversation into a task-conditioned summary. Its 56.9% SC is only 9.9 points
above No Defense, while UC increases from 31.1% to 35.3% and GHOST remains 27.4%; an omitted or weakened
constraint cannot be recovered downstream. LIGHT Three-Memory preserves more history and raises SC to 58.4%,
but still leaves 23.7% UC and 18.6% GHOST because memory availability does not guarantee target-bound execution
order.
Oracle Comparators and Dynamic Rule Compilation.
TrustAgent reduces UC to 5.2% and GHOST to 4.7%
after receiving the correct constraint as its constitution. DVR reaches 75.1% SC, 0.4% UC, and 0.4% GHOST by
receiving the benchmark’s exact executable verifier. These results show the value of knowing the applicable policy
and verification rule, but they do not test online constraint capture. STAR-Guard does not expose the applicable
ground-truth rule, outcome, reference trajectory, or verifier decision to the target agent, ingestion API, or semantic
gate. Instead, at each user turn, the ingestion API first decides whether the complete incoming message contains a
20


persistable operational safety constraint and, only when that decision is positive, compiles the message into semantic
fields and concrete sensitive-action and optional prerequisite templates using the current environment and public tool
schemas. Source-turn ingestion cannot observe the later resumed request or subsequent history. The runtime validates
generated tool names and arguments against the schemas and then enforces activated rules. Under this information
boundary, STAR-Guard reaches 81.2% SC with 1.9% UC and GHOST in the reported Qwen3.5-4B comparison.
