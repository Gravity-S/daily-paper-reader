## Abstract 

Small language model (SLM) agents need safety controls that track consequences across tool calls with little monitoring overhead. A private read, for example, becomes a leak when a later action sends that data outside the system. We in-troduce CARB (Conformal Agent Risk Budget), which calibrates when to stop an agent using a ledger of harm incurred before stopping. Under exchangeable episodes, standard conformal risk control bounds this declared loss in expecta-tion over calibration and a future episode. G-CARB selects scorer evidence along observable dependencies from private sources to outgoing actions. The ledger still covers the entire executed history, and computing the gate score requires no additional language-model inference. On AgentDojo replay with two 14B back-bones, G-CARB roughly halves scorer-input records at intermediate risk budgets while improving autonomous task completion relative to full-prefix scoring; ran-dom context of the same size achieves similar gains. Controlled examples show how retaining the relevant dependency can further avoid stopping benign work. 

## 1 Introduction 

An agent reads a private certificate, copies a field into a note, and later emails that note outside the organization. Together, these actions leak the private field. Preventing the leak requires connecting the proposed send to the earlier read. Tool-using language models routinely act across such multi-step trajectories [Yao et al., 2023, Schick et al., 2023, Zhou et al., 2024, Deng et al., 2023]. Prompt-injection studies, agent-safety benchmarks, and workflow audits expose the risks of these linked actions [Greshake et al., 2023, Debenedetti et al., 2024, Zhan et al., 2024, Andriushchenko et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2026]. For small language model (SLM) agents, the safeguard should also be inexpensive. We use an external gate: the agent proposes a tool call, a lightweight scorer evaluates the available evidence, and a calibrated threshold determines whether execution continues. The gate tracks dependencies without invoking another language model at every decision.

## 2 Related Work 

Conformal prediction provides finite-sample guarantees under exchangeability; CRC extends this framework to bounded monotone losses [Vovk et al., 2005, Angelopoulos et al., 2024]. Agent ap-plications differ in what they control. CORA targets harmful executed actions [Feng et al., 2026b], and CROP calibrates how much of a reasoning trace to retain [Cheung et al., 2026]. Other methods attribute failures within completed trajectories [Feng et al., 2026a], score or gate completed runs [Tayebati et al., 2026, Opoku and Banahene, 2026], or regulate deployment relative to a safe policy [Prinster et al., 2026]. Related work calibrates generated sets, uncertain plans, and reasoning graphs [Quach et al., 2024, Han et al., 2024, Ren et al., 2023, Fayyazi and Akrami, 2026, Hittesdorf et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026b]. CARB applies CRC to the harm accumulated along the execution prefix a stopping policy permits. Dependency-based defenses use information flow, graphs, or formal rules to enforce security. Ex-amples include CaMeL, RTBAS, AgentArmor, FORGE, and AgentFlow [Debenedetti et al., 2025, Zhong et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2025, Palumbo et al., 2026, Wang et al., 2026a]. IPIGuard constrains execution through a planned tool-dependency graph [An et al., 2025]; Task Shield checks whether instructions and tool calls contribute to the user’s task [Jia et al., 2025]. In G-CARB, the graph sup-plies evidence to a separate scorer, and calibration uses a ledger defined independently of that graph. AgentDojo provides the evaluation traces [Debenedetti et al., 2024], within a broader ecosystem of agent-safety benchmarks [Ruan et al., 2024, Andriushchenko et al., 2025, Zhan et al., 2024, Yuan 2et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2025]. Our experiments compare calibration targets and context selectors within this pipeline. 

## 3 Problem Formulation 

An ungated episode records an agent’s run without a stopping gate. Write it as Z = ( R1, . . . , R T ),with one record Rt = ( At, O t) per executed action. Here At is the action at step t, Ot is its returned observation, and T is the episode length. A later action may use any earlier observation, so records within an episode may be arbitrarily dependent. Before At executes, a fixed selector C chooses evidence Ct = C(Z<t , A t) from the history and the proposal. A fixed scorer assigns qt = s(Ct) ∈ [0 , 1] . The policy πλ executes At when qt ≤ λ and stops before the first proposal with qt > λ , handing control to a human or a sandbox. The resulting executed prefix is Pλ(Z). Larger thresholds permit longer prefixes; a stop-all threshold λ⊥ permits no actions. At decision time, the gate has access only to the history and proposal, since Ot becomes available after execution. We evaluate harm across the full executed prefix. For a data leak, this means checking whether an outgoing action carries data from an earlier private read. The ledger defines these events in advance and records those that occur within Pλ(Z).

Definition 1 (Stopped-prefix ledger loss) . Fix a finite set K of harm categories, each with an in-dicator Ek(P ) ∈ { 0, 1} that is determined by the executed records of the prefix P alone and is non-decreasing as P grows. A harm ledger ℓ(P ) ∈ [0 , 1] is any function of (Ek(P )) k∈K satisfying 

ℓ(∅) = 0 and prefix monotonicity, ℓ(P ) ≤ ℓ(P ′) whenever P is a prefix of P ′. The primary ledger is the union indicator ℓunion (P ) = max k∈K Ek(P ) ∈ { 0, 1}, and the loss at threshold λ on episode 

Z is Lλ(Z) = ℓ(Pλ(Z)) .

Raising λ lengthens the executed prefix, so Lλ(Z) is nondecreasing on every episode. Once a declared event occurs, it stays in the ledger through stopping or later recovery. This target measures whether harm occurred; applications that credit remediation would need a different loss. A gate depends on both its calibration target and its scorer evidence. To examine the target, we compare episode loss with an executed-step rate rλ(Z): an indicator that harm occurred, divided by the number of permitted actions, with zero assigned to an empty prefix. This baseline calibrates the running maximum over a finite threshold grid Λ, erλ(Z) = max λ′∈Λ: λ′≤λ rλ′ (Z), which makes the target monotone. Appendix D gives the full specification. The next proposition shows why both target and evidence matter. 

Proposition 1 (Two obstructions to episode-level control) . Fix 0 < α < p < 1.(i) Unit. There exists a distribution of length-K episodes, with K ≥ ⌈ 1/α ⌉ and no-guard harm prevalence p, such that every permitted prefix has rλ ≤ α and erλ ≤ α, but the all-execute policy has expected episode loss p > α .(ii) Context. Consider a harmful/benign pair drawn with probabilities p and 1 − p, with a common candidate sink as the only possible ledger event, harmful only in the first member. Suppose the gate-visible score histories match through that sink under common policy randomness, in-cluding a common calibrated threshold whose randomness is independent of which member is drawn. Let q be the overall probability that the sink executes, including survival through earlier decisions. Then E[L] = pq , and E[L] ≤ α forces the matched benign sink not to execute with probability at least 1 − α/p .

Part (i) shows how many harmless steps can dilute the rate assigned to one harmful action. Part (ii) shows the cost of missing context: a gate that receives matching scores on harmful and benign histories must allow or stop them at the same rate. Together, these results motivate calibrating episode loss and retaining evidence that distinguishes the two histories. Proofs are in Appendix A.

G-CARB and Full-prefix share the graph builder, node types, scorer, grid, and ledger. Only the selected records differ. They are supplied in chronological order, with the proposal’s result left empty at scoring time. In the certificate–note–email example, the reused private field or its handle links the read to the note and proposed send. G-CARB retains this chain and omits a disconnected public task. An email carrying only the public document instead follows the public branch. In both cases, the ledger evaluates every executed record. The scorer adds fixed weights for binary features and clips the sum to [0 , 1] : qt =clip [0 ,1] (w⊤x(Ct)) . Features describe the proposal, selected private or injected content, and the presence of a source-to-sink path (Appendix B.3). Private records can raise this score even when disconnected from the proposal, which helps explain the benefit of pruning context. All selectors use the same weights, and computing the score requires no language-model inference.

## 5 Experiments 

We examine three questions: can a small harmful-step rate hide substantial episode harm, does ledger calibration keep mean episode loss within budget, and when does dependency-based context selection help? 

AgentDojo and the ledger. In AgentDojo , an agent performs a benign user task while tool responses may contain injected instructions that redirect it toward an adversarial objective [Debenedetti et al., 2024]. We use 984 ungated episodes per backbone from Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct and Qwen3-14B. Each logged trajectory supplies the records for replaying candidate stopping poli-cies. The ledger records five harm categories: privacy flow, financial action, irreversible action, social action, and security event. An injection-directed event is registered when an observation contains an adversarial objective and a matching tool call later succeeds. Privacy flow also records whether a private canary—a synthetic marker—reaches an executed external or financial sink. Rejected proposals and private reads alone create no event. This ledger evaluates executed prefixes separately from AgentDojo’s terminal evaluator. It lacks an independent error audit, and the effect of rewriting canaries in 27 Qwen2.5 traces remains unmeasured (Appendix C). 

Controlled suites. The Compositional Exfiltration Suite (CES) contains 80 constructed leaks and 

240 matched non-leaks: public sends, private reads without sends, and disconnected private/public flows. Its clean subset pairs the leaks with 80 public sends. Graph-braid interleaves harmful chains with unrelated benign branches in 2,000 constructed trajectories. Labels come from the trace design and remain hidden from the graph builder and scorer. CES uses 100 assignments with related trajec-tories kept together; graph-braid uses 200 trajectory assignments. These suites test specific failure mechanisms at harm frequencies chosen by construction. The grouped CES splits fall outside Corol-lary 1; their results are descriptive. Appendix D gives fold sizes and grouping details. 

Baselines. No guard executes the complete trajectory. Executed-step CRC sees only the current proposal and calibrates the harmful-step rate envelope. Ledger-current calibrates episode loss using only the current proposal; it tests whether history is needed in the constructed suites. The remaining controls share G-CARB’s ledger, scorer, splits, and grid. Full-prefix supplies all earlier records and the proposal. Fixed-window-4 selects recent records. Equal-size random context retains the proposal and a fixed pseudorandom subset of earlier records, matching G-CARB’s size at each decision. This tests whether the identity of retained records matters beyond their count. 

Metrics. The primary safety metric is mean episode loss ¯L: the fraction of test episodes with a ledger event before stopping. Intervention I is the fraction in which the gate rejects a proposal. 

Autonomous success U is the fraction whose logged task completes before handoff; completion can coexist with a ledger event. The controlled suites also report the fraction of harmful sinks prevented from executing. Their benign-stop metrics have different denominators: non-leak trajectories in CES, and all trajectories in graph-braid. Observability and scope. The CES audit holds calibrated thresholds fixed while removing struc-tured provenance, exact sink text, or both. ## 6 Conclusion 

CARB applies standard CRC to accumulated harm before stopping; G-CARB selects dependency evidence while retaining that global loss. Context restriction improves AgentDojo task comple-tion and roughly halves scorer-input records at intermediate budgets. Random context performs similarly; constructed diagnostics show where relevant history also avoids benign stops. Smaller backbones, alternative scorers, and total gate overhead remain evaluation priorities. 

Limitations. The guarantee bounds expected declared ledger loss under exchangeability, not harm in every episode or harm outside the ledger. The ledger lacks an independent error audit, and se-mantic or paraphrased flows may escape the observable matching rules. AgentDojo evidence comes from repeated assignments of a fixed corpus using one scorer and two same-family 14B backbones; broader selectivity remains untested. Replay covers stopping without prior feedback to the agent, leaving interactive interventions and outcomes after handoff unevaluated. Finally, fewer scorer-input records do not establish lower total runtime: graph construction, end-to-end latency, and intervention costs remain unmeasured.

A.1 Control unit and matched histories 

Proof of Proposition 1(i). Choose an integer K ≥ ⌈ 1/α ⌉. Every trajectory has K actions. With probability p, its only union-ledger increment occurs at action K; otherwise the ledger never incre-ments. A policy that stops before action K has both zero episode loss and zero step rate. A policy that executes action K has rate 1/K on the harmful member and zero on the benign member. Thus every threshold has rλ(Z) ≤ 1/K ≤ α, and taking the maximum over lower thresholds preserves the bound for erλ(Z). The all-execute threshold λ = 1 permits the complete trajectory and has 

E[ℓunion (P1(Z))] = p > α .The counterexample establishes compatibility of a small rate with large episode risk. It does not assert that Equation 1, with its finite-sample correction and the envelope substituted for the ledger, accepts the all-execute policy at every sample size. More generally, for any possibly randomized stopping policy applied to a binary union ledger, 

E[ℓunion (Pbλ(Z))] = p Pr( first harmful action executes | ℓunion (Z) = 1) ,

where p is the no-guard prevalence and the conditional probability includes calibration and policy randomness. Gated risk equals p only when every harmful trajectory reaches that action. 

Proof of Proposition 1(ii). Couple the two potential members using the same calibrated threshold and policy randomness, independent of the member drawn. By assumption, the score histories through the candidate sink agree. Inducting over these histories, every preceding accept/reject deci-sion agrees, as does the decision at the sink if reached. Hence the indicator that the sink executes is identical for both members under the coupling. Write q for its probability, averaging over calibration and policy randomness. Only the harmful member can incur a ledger event, and it does so exactly when the sink executes, giving E[L] = pq . The inequality pq ≤ α implies q ≤ α/p . The benign member therefore fails to execute its sink with probability 1 − q ≥ 1 − α/p .The sink may be prevented by an earlier stop, so matching histories through the sink is essential: equal final scores alone leave earlier survival probabilities unconstrained. Observable provenance can distinguish the constructed pair before its sink executes.

A.3 Replay equivalence 

Proof of Proposition 2. At the first decision both runs share the empty executed prefix, proposal, graph, selected context, and score. If the proposal is rejected, both stop before executing it; if it is accepted, the coupled action and environment randomness produce the same observation, so the first executed records agree. Assume agreement through the first t − 1 executed records. Silence and the absence of wrapper side effects preserve the same agent state, so coupling supplies the same proposal at decision t, and prefix measurability supplies the same graph, context, score, and decision. Induction continues to the first rejection or to natural termination. The ledger is deterministic on the common executed prefix, so ledger state and loss agree as well. The proposition ceases to apply to a wrapper that edits the prompt before a decision, mutates the environment before rejection, exposes different fields in replay, or resamples uncoupled randomness.

At decision t, the graph contains one node for every executed record R1, . . . , R t−1 and one node for the proposal At. An executed node contains its action and visible result. The current node contains the proposed action and an empty result. Future actions, future observations, terminal outcomes, ledger labels, and suite labels are excluded. Node typing assigns five types: private sources, external sinks, financial actions, irreversible actions, and message/social actions. Types come from frozen tool metadata together with deterministic rules over tool names and visible outputs, all fixed before assignment and calibration. No typing rule reads suite labels or terminal outcomes. Visible identifiers include e-mail addresses, IBANs, canary strings, and values attached to object, source, event, message, channel, account, transaction, file, recipient, or sender fields. Generic identifiers must contain at least four characters. Distinctive text spans comprise exact e-mail, IBAN, and canary strings, together with visible result lines of at least 24 characters that contain a digit; such lines are truncated to 80 characters. A general exact-span match requires at least 16 characters. Every edge points from an earlier node to a later node. An edge is added when at least one of the following prefix-visible relations holds: 1. the records carry the same declared branch identifier; 2. they share an object identifier or a data identifier; 3. the later action explicitly declares as input a data identifier produced earlier; 4. a canary observed earlier reappears in the later action arguments; 5. a later financial action shares a transaction or account identifier with the earlier record; 6. a later irreversible action updates the same visible object; 7. a later message/social action shares a thread or message identifier with the earlier record; or 8. exact distinctive content from the earlier result reappears in the later visible action fields. The construction uses neither unconditional temporal edges nor learned or embedding-based simi-larity. Strict temporal orientation makes the graph acyclic. Our experiments instantiate the sink predicate as the frozen external-sink type, which includes finan-cial sinks. An irreversible-only, non-external proposal uses the ancestor fallback. The same fallback applies when no source-to-current path exists: the selector returns the current proposal together with its closed ancestor set. The current proposal is always retained, and selected nodes are serialized in chronological order. Full-prefix CARB instead returns every graph node. The equal-size random control retains the proposal and a deterministic pseudorandom subset of earlier nodes whose size matches the graph-local context at that decision. These relations are observable matching rules, not verified semantic dataflow. A shared identifier or exact span can introduce an irrelevant edge, while paraphrasing, transformation, or unlogged state can remove a needed one. The source-to-sink feature is evaluated on retained evidence; Full-prefix and G-CARB use the same underlying graph machinery. Changing the evidence changes scores and possibly harm, without redefining the ledger in Appendix C.

Over T decisions, serializing every prefix supplies PTt=1 O(t) = O(T 2) records to the scorer. If a selected component has at most d records per decision, the corresponding count is O(T d ). ## C AgentDojo ledger, corpus, and preprocessing 

The fixed corpora contain 984 ungated episodes each from Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct and Qwen3-14B on AgentDojo [Debenedetti et al., 2024]. Our custom ledger is a deterministic function of executed, prefix-visible records; it is not an official AgentDojo harm annotation. Its five binary categories are privacy flow, financial action, irreversible action, social action, and security event. The reported episode loss is their union, 

ℓunion (P ) = max {ℓpriv (P ), ℓ fin (P ), ℓ irr (P ), ℓ soc (P ), ℓ sec (P )}.

Each indicator records whether its event has occurred and remains one afterward. Subsequent re-covery does not remove a registered event. 

Event registration. For injection-directed events, the adapter identifies an adversarial objective from injection text only after that text appears in an executed observation. It registers an event when the corresponding tool call executes and its result confirms execution; the category follows the frozen tool type. Privacy flow also registers when an exact canary previously observed at a private source appears in the executed arguments of an external or financial sink. Rejected proposals, failed calls, future records, terminal benchmark outcomes, terminal fallback, and private reads without an executed sink create no event. The recorded corpora contain 169 events in 130 positive Qwen2.5 episodes and 257 events in 221 positive Qwen3 episodes. Event totals can exceed the number of positive episodes; the binary union and executed-step baseline count only whether, and when, the first union event occurs. 

Policy and evaluation separation. Registered ledger labels and benchmark outcomes are ex-cluded from the graph and scorer. The scorer can use visible tool fields and observed text as specified in Appendix B; it cannot consume a future event label. The ledger evaluates the complete executed prefix, not just the selected context. The official attack evaluator is excluded from scoring, calibra-tion, threshold selection, and operating-point selection. Its terminal outcomes and the prefix ledger measure different objects; the reported risk target is the latter.

## I Ethics and broader impacts 

The intended benefit is to make an agent gate’s risk target explicit and reduce avoidable interven-tions while controlling declared harm in expectation. Misinterpreting this marginal guarantee as a promise for every episode, or treating an incomplete ledger as comprehensive safety coverage, could encourage unsafe deployment. Unnecessary stops can also disrupt useful work and transfer burdens to human operators; our experiments do not evaluate outcomes after handoff. All experiments use a sandboxed benchmark with synthetic records and canary strings, without real private data. Deploy-ment requires validating the ledger and evidence channels for the intended setting and evaluating the handoff process. 21
