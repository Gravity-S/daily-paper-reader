Abstract 

AI agents now work together on shared code, documents, tools, and scientific analyses, often alongside people. No participant sees the whole state. Every step can look right on its own while the combined result is wrong. We call this the global coherence problem , and we argue that it is a problem of state , not only of intelligence: a stronger model makes better local decisions, but it cannot see what is not in its context, and it cannot make locally valid pieces fit together when they do not. Intelligence alone cannot guarantee a solution. To our knowledge, this paper is the first to formulate this as a failure class of AI agent systems in its own right and to synthesize the relevant limits into specifications for two practices builders already use for reliability, context engineering and the agent harness : what each agent’s context must contain, and what the harness around the agents must own and check. Our first result, the Observation-Aliasing Impossibility Theorem , makes the missing-information case precise. A policy can guarantee a valid action from its context exactly when every world consistent with that context allows a common action. When two worlds look identical but require different actions, no amount of reasoning, deliberation, role decomposition, messaging, or randomization can guarantee the right choice. The best randomized policy succeeds with probability exactly 1 /k when k indistinguishable worlds have disjoint valid actions, and a b-bit hint raises this to at most 1 /⌈k/ 2b⌉. This is a theorem about context engineering: it says exactly what a context must contain. Choosing the smallest such context is NP-hard: putting everything in every window is rarely feasible, and finding the least that suffices is hard. The second failure survives full information. Three agents can each use a locally valid, inverse-consistent translation between neighboring units while the loop through all three does not close; three roles can each make a legal spend while the total breaks the budget. No local check identifies the global error, because it lives in how the pieces fit, not in any one piece. Checking harder locally does not solve this in general: pairwise checks suffice when overlaps are tree-like, cycles can defeat them, and no fixed locality bound works universally across unrestricted constraint semantics. The two failures are linked, and the link is the paper’s central construction. The impossibility theorem says which distinctions a harness must keep; the second failure is what happens when those distinctions are lost on the way, as state is split into local views, re-encoded between participants, or changed by actions. We give a principled synthesis for guarding all three steps: a topology of where things meet, sheaves for whether local states glue into one world, groupoids for which encodings mean the same thing, and categories for how actions compose over time. These structures are not uniquely forced machinery. Under explicit assumptions on local views, lossless translations, and state updates, each realizes a property that exact validation requires, and each becomes unnecessary when its corresponding map is absent. Version checks, dependency engines, ledgers, and escrow quotas are domain-specific realizations of the same requirements. The same language also prices the problem. Agreement across representations can be certified in linear time, yet finding the smallest repair can be NP-hard, and a shared budget cannot be protected by independent local checks at all: it must be enforced at commit or split into quotas. The design these two failures lead to goes further than a manager agent, which is one more participant with one more view and is bound by the same theorem: models propose, and a harness built on this framework decides what is allowed . The framework tells that harness its job: which distinctions it must own, where overlaps can hide global inconsistency, which loops of translation must close, and which changes must wait for commit. We test the theory in nine studies. A frontier LLM solves all 40 tasks of a revision benchmark when it can see the deciding event. With that event hidden, observed accuracy across tested reasoning, role, voting, and tool conditions is statistically compatible with guessing among three options (1 /3); one sentence restoring the missing fact brings it to 40/40. On a preregistered 60-run TeamBench panel, ordinary agent teams overspend a shared 20-call budget in 5/5 runs, showing them the live count still leaves 4/5, and enforcing the budget at commit leaves 0/5; observed mean task progress does not fall in this five-seed comparison. A follow-up on the same task shows 

1arXiv:2610.02036v1 [cs.AI] 1 Oct 2026 that fixed per-role escrow quotas also reach 0/5 violations, while an information-only counter still overspends in 4/5; progress comparisons between escrow and the ledger are descriptive because their attestation gates differ. Under an explicit unique-name diagnostic, public ontology-alignment networks contain pairwise-invisible same-ontology identity collisions in 23 of 45 networks from 2018–2024. Agents given split views reproduce such collisions in 9 of 40 merges, and a network-level check detects and removes all nine, although the send-to-all repair lowers mean F1 from 0.796 to 0.774. In a preregistered 222-episode τ 2-bench Telecom panel, ordinary policy checks tie our coherence harness where the injected change breaks a policy rule, as predicted; where a committed change is silently undone, agents with those checks score 0.07 on the benchmark’s own grader and agents with the harness score 1.00. Where a conventional tool already holds the relevant state—a dependency engine, a coupled solver—it matches our harness exactly, as the theory predicts. Across all nine studies the pattern is the one the theory draws: the harness wins where the deciding state is missing or unowned, and ties where a conventional tool already owns it. The proofs are complete and given in the appendices; the agent evidence uses one model family and modest panels, and scale is the next step. 

# 1 Introduction: every step looks right, and the result is still wrong 

Consider a three-agent team on a TeamBench task [51]: a Planner writes the plan, an Executor runs commands, and a Verifier checks the result, with 20 command runs allowed for the whole team. Each agent spends runs for good reasons, and no single run is a mistake. But the limit belongs to the team , no agent owns it, and the team spends about three times the budget. This is the failure this paper is about: every step is locally justified, and together they break a rule that belongs to no one . Showing the team the running total did not fix it. When the harness itself held the count and refused the 21st run, the violations disappeared; observed mean task progress did not fall in that five-run panel (Study IV). The pattern is general. In τ 2-bench Telecom, a user and an assistant change different parts of one environment under different permissions [12]; in BixBench3, several scientific artifacts must stay derived from one evolving analysis [25]. The participants act on local views, each step can be locally correct, and the combined result need not be: local validity ̸ ⇒ global coherence .

Two ways to fail. Figure 1 sketches both. The first is observation aliasing : two situations look the same to every participant but need different actions, as when two revision histories produce the same log once one event is hidden. Our first result, the Observation-Aliasing Impossibility Theorem (Theorem 2.1), says exactly when this is fatal: a policy can guarantee a valid action from what it observes—for an LLM agent, its context—if and only if every world consistent with that observation allows a common action. When that fails, no amount of reasoning, deliberation, role decomposition, messaging, or sampling recovers the difference; with k look-alike worlds whose admissible-action sets are pairwise disjoint, the best randomized worst-case success is exactly 1 /k (Theorem 2.3). A stronger model searches better within its context; it cannot see past it. Context engineering helps exactly when it puts the missing distinction into the window, and longer reasoning over the same window does not. This separates intelligence from identifiability ,for human, AI, and mixed teams alike. In Study I, a frontier LLM solves all 40 tasks when it can see the deciding event; when that event is hidden, observed accuracy across the tested inference strategies is compatible with chance (1 /3), and one sentence of added context stating the missing fact brings it back to 40/40. The second failure concerns information the participants do have. Everyone can pass the checks available in their local scope while the whole is not coherent, because locally consistent pieces need not fit together into any single global state. Three pairwise translations can each be well formed, inverse-consistent, and locally plausible while the loop through all three does not return to where it started; three roles can each make a legal spend while the total breaks the budget, as in the TeamBench team above. 

Four questions. The paper asks what can be said about these failures before choosing any particular agent design. We separate four questions: • Observation. Do the local views contain enough information for globally valid behavior? • Realization. Do locally compatible pieces correspond to any global state? • Transition. Do local actions combine into a valid global change? • Semantic state. What is the least information that must be kept to decide which futures remain valid? 2Cost cuts across all four: how much state must be observed, how hard global checking is, how hard it is to design the smallest sufficient set of observables, and whether repairing an inconsistency is harder than detecting it. 

The framework. The first question has a sharp answer, the impossibility theorem, and it says what the harness must keep and every decision’s context must reach: every distinction that changes which actions are valid (Proposition 2.4). The rest of the paper answers how . In a team the state is never in one place and never still. It is split into local views, re-encoded between participants, and changed by actions, and each of these steps can silently lose a distinction the theorem says must be kept. Each agent holds a local piece of data: 1 m, 1000 mm, 39 .37 in. The sheaf condition asks whether pieces that agree on every overlap come from exactly one world, here exactly one beam. When it holds, local agreement is enough; when it fails, the pieces agree everywhere and come from no world at all. This is where “every agent is right and the team is wrong” becomes a condition a harness can check. • A category C: what can change. The objects are versions of the shared state and the arrows are actions that really change it: revise the beam to 1 .2 m, then order $720 of steel. Arrows compose into histories, most cannot be undone, and order matters, so this is a category rather than a groupoid. The line between the two is the line between 1 m → 1000 mm, which only re-expresses the beam, and 1 m → 1.2 m, which changes it. Only changes are checked at commit. • A history state D: what still matters. This is the theory of agent memory. Of the whole past, the harness keeps only what changes which future actions are legal: here the budget left and the version each agent last read. That is enough to refuse an order priced from the v3 quote once the beam is revised in v4. The rest of the log can be dropped. This is a principled synthesis, not the only possible implementation. What the theory says. Beyond the impossibility theorem itself, we give the exact success rate of the best randomized policy in general (Theorem B.2) and the number of bits of new information needed to raise it (Corollary B.3). Choosing the smallest set of fields or sensors that makes validation exact is NP-hard (Theorem 2.5). For the second failure, classical database results say local agreement is enough on tree-like overlap structures (Theorem 3.1), while cycles can make pairwise agreement insufficient. More generally, no fixed locality order works universally across unrestricted constraint semantics (Proposition 3.2), although structured cyclic classes can admit bounded tests. Agreement across different representations reduces to a cycle check that a spanning forest certifies in linear time (Theorem 3.4, Corollary 3.5), but repairing a disagreement at minimum cost is NP-hard (Proposition 3.6). Actions that change state are different from actions that only re-express it: re-expressions preserve global consistency automatically, while individually valid spends against a shared budget can jointly break it (Propositions F.4 and 3.3). So consistency must be checked when changes are committed, and version checks with atomic commit give the usual serializability guarantee, provided every value a change depends on is recorded (Proposition 4.1). 

Why “just give everyone all the information” is not the answer. A natural reply is that the impossibility theorem is correct but easy to escape: share all the context, and every agent will be right about the whole. We claim more than the theorem. Its corollaries are correct, and global coherence stays hard even in principle, for five separate reasons. • Sharing everything is rarely an option, and sharing the least is hard. Context windows, bandwidth, privacy, and authority limit what each participant can see. So the real question is what to share, and removing aliasing requires exposing every distinction that changes which actions are valid. Finding the smallest context—the fewest fields, sensors, or log entries—that does this is NP-hard, and greedy selection is essentially the best polynomial-time approximation (Theorem 2.5). Also, in a team each decision is made on the acting participant’s own information set, not on the union of everyone’s views (Corollary 2.2). • Local agreement does not always add up. Pairwise checks are enough when the overlaps form a join tree. Cycles can defeat pairwise checks, and no fixed locality bound is universal across unrestricted constraint semantics; structured cyclic classes can nevertheless admit bounded tests (Theorem 3.1, Proposition 3.2). Exact global checking costs time exponential in the width of the interaction structure in the general finite-domain case (Section 3.4). 4• Finding an error is easier than fixing it. A spanning forest certifies that representations agree in linear time, but finding the fewest labels to change is Max-Cut-hard (Proposition 3.6). • Shared constraints need coordination. Spends that are each legal on their own can merge into an illegal total (Proposition 3.3). The ways out are coordination at commit or a redesign such as escrow quotas, and each has a price (Appendix F.4). • Change and time add their own conditions. Re-expressing state preserves gluing, but changing state does not (Proposition F.4). A long-running process must carry exactly the history that still matters (Theorem F.3), and a commit is safe only if everything it read is still current (Proposition 4.1). The theory also says when coherence is easy: when one participant can hold and act on the whole exact state, the extra structure should add little (Section 5.11, Prediction 1). The hard case is ordinary multi-party work, where authority, views, and shared resources are split. Sections 3 and 4 prove these points. Section 5 shows them in agent runs. Showing the live budget to every agent still leaves overspending in 4 of 5 runs, and only ownership at commit or escrow stops it (Study IV). A single agent with the full view keeps an alignment network coherent, but agents with split views leave conflicts that only a network-level check finds (Study IX). And checks on the current state miss what changed since the agent read it: in τ 2-bench Telecom they commit on a stale quote in 10 of 10 episodes, and they never notice when a committed change is silently undone (Study V). Hardness results for decentralized decisions are known in pieces, from team decision theory to decentralized POMDPs (Section 6), and software and systems research has long supplied related compositional structures. This paper’s contribution is to synthesize those conditions for AI agent harnesses: one framework organizes missing distinctions, cycles, shared invariants, repair, and time, helps diagnose which conditions a system faces, and names standard structures that realize the required properties. The nine studies test these pieces, the central ones on agent benchmarks under a fixed model. 

What this means for building agent systems. The theory separates two jobs, and they map directly onto how agents are built today. The model supplies proposal intelligence : interpreting intent, planning, choosing tools, and drafting changes. The harness supplies state, admissibility, and commit : it decides what enters each agent’s context, holds the distinctions that matter, checks each proposed tool call against them, and accepts, repairs, or rejects it. Context engineering and harness design are usually practiced as craft; the theory turns them into specifications. The impossibility theorem says what a context must contain, and the framework says what the harness must own and check. The model can stay as flexible and stochastic as it likes; what must be fixed is the shared record of facts that decide what is allowed. Stronger models improve the first job. They cannot replace the second when the needed distinction is missing from what they see. The limits bind every layer. No matter how capable the model or how well engineered its context, a constraint that several agents act on needs an owner (Proposition 3.3, Study IV). And no matter how carefully the harness is built, it guarantees only what its own state can tell apart (Proposition 2.4), catches a contradiction around a loop only if some check spans the loop (Proposition 3.2), and catches a stale read only if it remembers what each proposal read, since against the current state alone a stale proposal and a fresh one look the same (Theorem 2.1 applied to the harness itself, Study V). The harness is also a different thing from a manager or orchestrator agent. A manager is a model too: it proposes from its own view, so Corollary 2.2 binds it like any other role, and in Study IV a team with a dedicated Planner and Verifier still overspent its budget when shown the live count. Where an orchestrator does work, the framework says why: a hub that holds the shared fields makes the overlaps a tree, and on trees local checks are enough (Section 3.1). The harness is not a smarter participant. It owns the state, and the framework tells it which state to own, what to put in context, and which checks to run. The same results answer the team-design questions practitioners usually settle by intuition: who should own a decision, which roles can work in parallel, and who must wait for whom (Section 4.5).

What is new. This paper’s novelty is the agent-facing synthesis and operationalization. To our knowledge, it is the first work to formulate global coherence as a failure class of agent systems in its own right, separate identifiability and commit semantics from model capability, and assemble the relevant information-theoretic and local-to-global conditions into a framework that says what each agent’s context must contain and what the harness must enforce. The mathematical ingredients have important precedents, credited below; the contribution is connecting their guarantees and limits to agent harness design and testing the resulting boundary. The experiments probe its counterintuitive parts: • A stronger model is not the fix for missing information. A model that solves every task when it can see the deciding fact produces accuracy statistically compatible with chance when that fact is hidden across the tested reasoning, deliberation, and voting conditions; one added sentence stating the fact solves every task again (Study I). The theorem supplies the general impossibility statement; the finite panel is consistent with it. 6• More context is not the fix for shared state. Context fixes missing information, as Study I shows, but agents who have the live budget in their context still overspend it. A shared invariant needs an owner: enforcement at commit, or quotas that split it (Study IV). • Checking every handoff is not the fix. Every pairwise review can pass while the integrated result is impossible, in public benchmark data and in agent runs alike (Studies VIII and IX). • Checking the current state is not the fix. A commit can rest on a stale read, and a committed change can be silently undone, while every check on the current state passes (Study V). The mathematics is deliberately borrowed, and that is a strength. Acyclic database schemes, group synchronization, sheaves, treewidth, Myhill–Nerode minimization, and optimistic concurrency control are each battle-tested in their own field, and we credit each where it appears. What was missing is the bridge to AI agents: identifying the validation properties distributed agent systems require, showing how these classical structures realize them under explicit assumptions, and telling builders when each property matters and when it does not. The new formal results serve that bridge: the admissibility form of the aliasing theorem with its exact randomized value and bit bounds, and the hardness of choosing the smallest state to expose, a close relative of minimal reducts in rough-set theory (Appendix A sorts every result by origin). The contribution is the bridge, not new mathematics. The empirical claims are deliberately narrower than the theory. They use one model family, a synthetic aliasing probe, five seeds per arm on three TeamBench tasks, and failures that we inject into τ 2-bench tasks. Within that scope the result is that the predicted failures occur, the tested inference variations do not remove them, and owning the missing state does. How often these failures dominate on broad agent benchmarks is the next question, and the theory says where to look. 

Contributions of this paper. 

• A limit on what reasoning can fix. The Observation-Aliasing Impossibility Theorem and its quantitative forms: the exact success rate of the best randomized policy, the number of bits of new state needed, and extensions to multi-agent protocols and tool use. For AI systems the message is direct: more reasoning, more agents, more messages, and more samples cannot recover a distinction that none of them can see. Adding the missing state to context can, and the smallest such context is exactly the one that keeps apart worlds allowing different actions. • One framework for local-to-global coherence. A topology says where things meet, sheaves ask whether local views glue into one world, groupoids record which encodings mean the same thing, and categories describe how actions compose over time. Under explicit assumptions, we derive the properties exact validation requires and use these standard structures as a compositional realization of them. This turns “every agent passes its local checks and the team is wrong” from an anecdote into a condition a harness can check, and it explains why familiar tools work exactly where they do. • The price of coherence. Local checks suffice on join-tree overlap structures; cycles can defeat pairwise checks, and no fixed locality bound works universally across unrestricted constraint semantics. Agreement across encodings can be certified in linear time, while the smallest repair can be NP-hard. A shared budget cannot be protected by independent local checks, and choosing the smallest state to expose is NP-hard. The cost depends on how the parts interact, not on how many agents there are. • A specification for agent harnesses. Models propose; a harness that owns the state decides what is allowed, and the framework specifies exactly what it must hold, what it must put into context, and what it must check. This goes beyond a manager agent, which proposes from its own view like any other role. The model can stay stochastic. Version checks, dependency engines, ledgers, and escrow quotas are special cases, and the theory says when each is enough. For builders this gives a concrete rule: when a multi-agent system fails, first ask which distinction no one owns, before asking for a bigger model or more agents. • Evidence that the theory predicts where the harness helps and where it does not. Nine studies, four of them causal agent experiments frozen before scoring: a controlled aliasing probe, TeamBench, τ 2-bench Telecom, and ontology alignment. The harness removes the targeted failures without a stronger model, and it ties conventional tools exactly where those tools already own the state. 

# 2 Model and the limit of reasoning 

This section states the result everything else rests on: no amount of reasoning recovers a distinction that is not in view. We keep only what later sections use. The quantitative refinements, and all proofs, are in Appendix B. 72.1 What participants see and what they may do 

The model needs three ingredients: the worlds that could be true, what each participant sees, and which actions are allowed in each world. Let X be the set of possible global states and A the set of actions. For each state x, let 

C(x) ⊆ A be the actions allowed there. C can encode anything that decides legality: authority, budgets, invariants, freshness, or required artifacts. A participant does not see x itself; it sees an observation y = Ω( x). In a team, Ω( x)can be the tuple of everyone’s local views (Ω 1(x), . . . , Ωm(x)). A runtime, in turn, remembers some summary ϕ(x) of the state. So Ω is what a decision-maker can see, and ϕ is what the runtime keeps. In agent-engineering terms, Ω i is agent i’s context—its prompt, retrieved documents, tool results, and memory—and the runtime is the agent harness, the code around the model that assembles that context, executes tool calls, and decides which of them take effect. We use “runtime” and “harness” interchangeably. For the local-to-global part we need one more object. Split a scope U into local scopes U1, . . . , U n: participants’ views, subsystems, or artifacts. Write X(U ) for global states and Match( {Ui}) for tuples of local states that agree wherever scopes overlap. Restricting a global state to its local pieces gives a map 

ρ : X(U ) → Match( {Ui}).

Both failures from the introduction are properties of this one map. In aliasing , ρ is not one-to-one: two different worlds give the same local views. In non-realizability , ρ is not onto: locally matching pieces come from no world at all. A coherent system needs ρ to be a bijection. That is exactly the sheaf condition; with representations identified up to equivalence, it becomes the descent condition [76, 77, 78]. This section is about the first failure; Section 3 develops the second. 

# 2.2 The impossibility theorem 

The central result makes a simple point exact: no policy can act on a distinction it cannot see. The idea has a long history, as perceptual aliasing in reinforcement learning [82] and as indistinguishability arguments in distributed computing [29]. What we add is the exact condition for choosing a valid action, and what it implies for teams, randomness, tools, and hints. In words: a policy can always pick a valid action exactly when, for every observation, all the worlds that could be behind it share at least one valid action.

Proof sketch. If a universally valid policy exists, its action π(y) must be admissible for every state in the fiber Ω −1(y), hence lies in the intersection. What this means. If two different realities give a group the same evidence but need different decisions, the group cannot guarantee the right decision from that evidence, and this holds for any decision process whose complete available information is Ω( x): one AI agent, a team of agents, a human organization, or a human–AI collaboration. Take the revision log from the introduction. Three teams commit, revert, and re-apply changes to one shared parameter, and before writing, an agent must name the current head; if the last event is hidden, three different histories produce the same log but leave three different heads. Whatever head the agent names, it is wrong in two of the three worlds, and only recovering the hidden event changes that, which is exactly what Study I tests. 8More agents do not help. The same holds for any team that only talks among itself. 

Corollary 2.2 (Reasoning and role decomposition do not create missing state information) . Consider any deterministic multi-round protocol whose agents begin with local observations Ω1(x), . . . , Ωm(x) and thereafter exchange messages computed only from those observations and prior messages. If no new external observation is introduced, the complete transcript and final action are functions only of the joint initial observation Ω( x). Therefore states aliased by the joint observation remain indistinguishable regardless of the number of agents, roles, reasoning rounds, or messages. 

The proof is an induction over communication rounds: every message is computed from what the agents saw, so the whole transcript is too. Randomness does not help either; it changes which answer is chosen, not which worlds can be told apart. The corollary also has a positive reading. When a team fails on a distinction that its joint observation does 

contain, the failure is one of protocol—who sends what, and who owns which decision—not an impossibility. And the theorem applies at every single decision: an action taken by one role is limited by what that role has seen and been told, not by the union of everyone’s views.

The “authoritative semantic state” means: not a copy of the whole world, but every distinction that can change whether a proposal is valid. The model does not need the whole world in its prompt. # 3 When every agent is right and the team is still wrong 

Section 2 was about information that no one has. This section is about information that everyone has, but in pieces. Each agent’s piece passes every check the agent can run, and the pieces still add up to no possible world. It happens in three ways in agent systems—pieces compared pairwise, a budget no one owns, and translations between formats—and each is a place where the framework earns its keep. For each, a sharp result says exactly when local checks are enough and what it costs when they are not, and each turns into a design rule. Proofs are in Appendices C and D. 

# 3.1 Pairwise checks miss loops 

Consider three agents that each own one variable and each reconcile with a neighbor. The A–B check requires A = B,the B–C check requires B = C, and the A–C check requires A̸ = C. Each check can be satisfied, and so can any two of them together. No assignment satisfies all three: the first two force A = C, and the third forbids it. Replace the Booleans with “uses schema v2,” “the deadline moved,” or “these two records are the same customer,” and this is a familiar failure of multi-agent pipelines: every handoff review passes, and the integrated result is impossible (Figure 1b). The question underneath is whether local states that agree on every overlap come from one global state; in the notation of Section 2.1, whether ρ is onto. An assignment of states to scopes for which the answer is always yes, and uniquely 10 so, is called a sheaf [67, 6]. That is the right name for it: the sheaf condition is precisely the statement that local agreement implies one global state, so it gives a runtime an exact target to check. A sheaf specifies what coherence means; search and repair are separate steps. One detail matters for agent design. The false pass happens when agents compare what each considers possible —constraints, plans, ranges—rather than what each has committed to . If every agent commits to a concrete value, comparing committed values on overlaps catches the triangle. Agents usually exchange the former. (This is the possibilistic contextuality of Abramsky and Brandenburger [2]; Appendix C.) When are pairwise checks enough? Database theory has a sharp answer, and it depends only on the shape of who overlaps with whom. 

Theorem 3.1 (Tree-shaped overlaps suffice; Beeri, Fagin, Maier, and Yannakakis [14, 15]) . If the overlap structure of the local constraints is acyclic (formally, α-acyclic: it has a join tree), then local constraints that agree pairwise are globally consistent. If it has a cycle, some pairwise-consistent family of local constraints is not. 

A planner–coder–tester chain is a tree. So is a hub-and-spoke team in which every agent reconciles only with an orchestrator that holds the shared fields. A review loop, or three teams that each share an interface with the other two, is a cycle. Cycles remove the pairwise guarantee, and increasing the checked group size has no universal fixed bound across unrestricted constraint families: 

Proposition 3.2 (No fixed-size check is enough) . For every k, there is a finite set of local constraints in which every 

k of them can be satisfied together, but all of them cannot. 

The construction is a parity puzzle on a ring of more than k agents. Any proper subset of the constraints can be satisfied, and only the sum of all of them exposes the contradiction (Appendix C.2). Algorithms that propagate derived constraints, rather than check groups in isolation, do solve rings, but on expander-shaped interaction graphs they too fail at every fixed level. Some structured problems do have a fixed check size—Helly’s theorem gives d + 1 for convex constraints in Rd [58]—so the useful question for a given system is which structure it has. 

Design rule. If the agents’ overlaps form a join tree, pairwise reconciliation is enough. Cycles remove that guarantee: use a global method appropriate to the constraint class, or restructure the overlaps into a join tree. For translation networks, Section 3.3 gives the exact loop check.

# 3.3 Translations that each look right can fail around a loop

One direction is a telescoping product; the other builds frames along a spanning tree. That construction is also the practical algorithm: 

Corollary 3.5 (A linear-time consistency check) . Pick a spanning forest of the agent graph, fix one frame per tree, and propagate frames along the trees. With |V | agents, |E| translation edges, and c connected components, this takes β1 = |E| − | V | + c edge checks and O(|V | + |E|) compose-and-compare operations, although the graph can contain exponentially many loops. 

Each edge beyond the forest adds one independent constraint; for a finite group, a uniformly random labeling is consistent with probability |G|−β1 (Appendix D). A loop whose translations do not compose to the identity is said to have nontrivial holonomy . The triangle of Section 3.1 is the smallest example: write “same” as 0 and “opposite” as 1, and the loop sums to 0 + 0 + 1 = 1 ̸ = 0. In ontology matching the same structure appears as a chain of “same as” links that joins two distinct classes of one ontology. No single link looks wrong; only the loop shows the conflict (Studies VIII and IX). 

Why canonical IDs are not enough. A tempting shortcut is to map every description to a canonical form—a canonical ID, an embedding, a normalized schema—and compare those. That keeps whether two things are equivalent and throws away how . It can hide exactly the failure above: every description looks the same after canonicalization while the loop of translations still does not close (Study VII; Appendix F.1). Edits that change the object, such as revising a beam from 1 m to 1 .2 m, are not translations at all. They are state changes, treated in Section 4.2. 

Design rule. Keep the translations, not just the equivalence classes, and check every loop. A spanning forest does this in linear time. 

# 3.4 Structure, not agent count, sets the price 

How expensive is coherence? Three results say the answer depends on how agents interact, not on how many there are. 

Exact checking is exponential only in width. For finite-domain constraints, dynamic programming over a tree decomposition checks global consistency in time roughly O(nd w+1 ) for n variables of domain size d and treewidth w

[33]. For general constraint problems the exponential dependence on width cannot be substantially removed [40, 57] (Appendix D.2). A hundred agents in a pipeline can be cheap to keep coherent; five densely coupled agents can be expensive. 

A small signature can track a large constraint. For linear constraints, whether the pieces fit is decided by one short vector, the syndrome , computed from the local specifications by linear algebra. When updates can shift it arbitrarily, the syndrome is also the least a monitor must remember to track coherence over time (Theorem D.3). The ring puzzle that defeats every fixed-size check has a one-bit syndrome. Needing a global witness is not the same as being expensive to monitor.

# 4 From theory to a coherence harness 

Sections 2 and 3 say what can go wrong. This section proposes a runtime, the coherence harness: how it sits next to the model, what it checks when a proposal would change the world, what it remembers, and why the structures from the introduction—a topology, a category, a groupoid, and a sheaf condition—are the ones it needs. It closes with what the same results say about organizing the team around the harness. 

# 4.1 The harness loop 

The results hold for any decision process, but LLM agents make them urgent. An LLM policy is useful because it is flexible: it infers intent, plans, chooses tools, and adapts. The same flexibility makes its proposals stochastic and hard to certify end to end. The runtime therefore splits the work. Agent i proposes from its context,

where mt is its memory and Ti its tools. The runtime checks the proposal against its authoritative state zt = ϕ(xt),

and only accepted changes produce xt+1 . The checks can cover identity, authority, freshness, budgets, format compatibility, and required artifacts. The model stays stochastic; the rule for what may change does not have to be. Figure 4 shows the split. The split separates three questions that agent research usually blurs. Capability asks whether the model can propose a useful action. Identifiability asks whether the available state contains enough information to determine which actions are valid. Commit correctness asks whether accepted local actions keep the whole system consistent. Agent benchmarks usually score all three at once. The theory predicts cases where scaling capability saturates because the bottleneck is identifiability or commit semantics, not reasoning strength. 

# 4.2 What the runtime checks at commit 

Re-expressing state is safe; changing it is not. Two kinds of action look alike in an agent log but behave differently. A re-expression —converting units, translating a schema, re-encoding a record—leaves the underlying object 13 unchanged. A state change —spending budget, editing a design, overwriting a file—does not. If every agent only re-expresses its state and the translations agree where scopes overlap, the result is automatically globally consistent. If agents change state, each change can be locally legal and every overlap can agree while the combination is still no valid world; the shared budget of Section 3.2 is the example. So re-expressions need no commit-time check, and state changes do. Proposition F.4 (Appendix F.6) states this precisely. By induction, a trajectory in which every committed step is a re-expression or was checked at commit stays coherent. This is why the framework keeps re-expressions in the groupoid G and changes in the category C: the split is exactly the line between what needs no check and what does. 

Keep only the history that changes the future. Agent memory grows without bound, and most of it does not affect what is legal next. Two histories can share one memory state exactly when every possible continuation is legal after one if and only if it is legal after the other. This is the Myhill–Nerode theorem [46]: the smallest exact monitor has one state per such class. For a budget B and k obligations that must be done before finish , the smallest state is the obvious pair, remaining budget and completed obligations: ( B + 1)2 k + 2 states, but only ⌈log 2(B + 1) ⌉ + k bits plus a terminal flag (Appendix E). Provenance, such as which version an artifact was derived from, belongs in memory exactly when it changes which futures are legal. A stale derivation is the standard case (Study III). 

Stale reads and atomic commit. An agent reads state, thinks for several turns, and proposes a change. By then the state may have moved. The classical fix is optimistic concurrency control [52]: record the version of everything the proposal depended on, and at commit atomically check that none of it has changed before applying the change. 

Proposition 4.1 (Version checks give one-at-a-time semantics) . Suppose every proposal records every state item it depended on, every write advances the written item’s version, and the version check and the writes happen atomically. Then the committed changes are equivalent to running them one at a time in commit order. 

For an LLM agent the first condition is the hard one. The read set is every authoritative item that entered the model’s context and influenced the proposal, not only the items the final tool call touches. A quote the agent read five turns ago belongs to the read set of the order it places now; this is what ordinary τ 2-bench checks miss (Study V). The model’s sampled output is part of the transaction, so the equivalence is to serial execution with the same samples (Appendix E.2).

Splitting state into views needs gluing. Translating between formats needs a groupoid. A translation cannot restore a distinction its source format already lost: if agent i’s format merges two objects, every format translated from it merges them too. When every format is lossless, the translations between them are forced: each can be undone, and composing them around any loop returns the identity. Conversely, any family of reversible translations that closes around every loop comes from lossless encodings of one object (Proposition F.2). That is exactly a groupoid with consistent loops, and Corollary 3.5 checks it. 

Acting needs a self-updating state. A runtime that must re-read the world after every action to know what is now legal is not keeping state; it is polling. For the runtime to update its own state, the state must keep apart any two worlds that some future sequence of actions would tell apart. A budget shows the difference. With one or two units left, the same actions are legal now, but after one spend they are not. “May spend now” is exact for one step; the remaining count is the smallest state that updates itself (Theorem F.3, the Myhill–Nerode construction applied to valid actions [46, 60]). Because the updates compose—updating for a and then for b is updating for ab —actions act on the runtime’s state as a category. The history state D is the same construction indexed by histories. 

Corollary 4.2 (Where each structure is needed) . Consider a runtime that must accept exactly the valid actions and commit only realizable states. If it accepts arbitrary combinations of local views at commit, it needs the gluing check. If agents use several lossless formats, it needs reversible, loop-consistent translations. If it must update its state under actions without re-reading the world, it needs the self-updating state. Conversely, with one scope there is nothing to glue, with one format there is nothing to translate, and when no action changes the state the self-updating state reduces to the exact state of Proposition 2.4. 

This is the sense in which the framework is principled rather than arbitrary. Remove a map and its corresponding property drops out; add the map under the assumptions above and exact validation requires that property. The named mathematical structure is a standard compositional realization, not the only possible implementation. Appendix F.3 gives the precise conditions and proofs. The corollary is what the predictions of Section 5.11 test: each structure should help where its map can lose information, and add only overhead elsewhere. It also explains the ties in Studies III and VI. The corollary constrains properties, not machinery. A version counter, a dependency engine, or a residual solver meets the relevant condition in its own domain, and there it should match the general runtime. The structures are the general way to meet the conditions across boundaries where no such mechanism exists. The same pattern—first a requirement for sufficient information, then a structure chosen to preserve it as it is distributed—appears in team decision theory, in atlases of charts in geometry, in sufficient statistics and filtering, and in version control, where a merge conflict is a gluing failure (Appendix F.3). The impossibility theorem does not force this design on its own. Weakening the goal, querying for the information, restructuring the state, or accepting probabilistic success are legitimate alternatives, each with a cost (Appendix F.4).

# 4.5 Designing the team: owners, dependencies, and parallel work 

Multi-agent systems today are organized by job title. A tech lead oversees the project, engineers do the work, and the pricing role waits until the design is done. These choices are made by human intuition. They are often right, but nothing says when they are necessary or what else would work. The framework turns each of them into a question about state, and each question has an answer among the results above. Fix the roles 1 , . . . , m . For role i, let Wi be the items of authoritative state its proposals write, and let Ri be every item whose value can change whether its proposals are valid. Ri therefore includes the scope of every constraint the proposal must respect, not only the fields the tool call touches. In the beam example of Figure 2, the Cost agent’s quote has Rcost ∋ beam length, which the Design agent writes, and both agents’ spends read the shared budget. 1. Give each role the distinctions its decisions need. The harness can reject an invalid proposal, but it cannot make a role propose a valid one. Role i can always propose an admissible action exactly when every fiber of its own context Ω i has a common admissible action for that role (Theorem 2.1); otherwise the harness keeps rejecting it. Its information set is its own: a decision taken by role i is bounded by Ω i and the messages it has received, not by the union of all views (Corollary 2.2). A context that never merges two worlds allowing that role different actions is always sufficient (Proposition 2.4). Finding the smallest one is NP-hard (Theorem 2.5), so in practice it is chosen greedily. 2. Give every cross-role constraint an owner. A constraint whose scope lies in no single role’s Wi, and whose actions are not invariant-confluent, cannot be protected by the roles checking independently (Proposition 3.3 for a budget; Bailis et al. [9] in general). It needs either a scope that contains it and is checked at commit (a ledger), or a split into local constraints (escrow, ∑ 

> i

Bi = B); locks, reservations, and conservative stopping are costlier alternatives (Appendix F.4). In the formal sense this is what leading means: the lead for a constraint is whichever component holds its whole scope and refuses violating commits. A person or an agent can lead in this sense only by holding that state authoritatively and controlling the commit, which is the harness’s job. A lead agent that receives only summaries is one more Ω i, and Corollary 2.2 binds it like any other role. Study IV shows the difference: a 16 team with a Planner and a Verifier overspent when shown the live count, and both an owning ledger and escrow quotas stopped it. 3. Decide parallel or sequential from read and write sets. If Wi ∩ Rj = Wj ∩ Ri = Wi ∩ Wj = ∅, neither role can invalidate the other. Committing them in either order gives the same state, and the version check of Proposition 4.1 never fires, so parallel work is free. These are Bernstein’s conditions for parallel execution [18], with 

Ri widened to everything that bears on validity. When Wi ∩ Rj̸ = ∅, as when Design writes the length that Cost reads, there are three correct options, each with a price: • Sequence j after i, which is a lock. This is “pricing waits for engineering,” and it costs latency. • Run in parallel with a check at commit. Role j records the versions of Rj , and the harness rejects or reruns j’s proposal if i has since written. This is correct by Proposition 4.1 and costs rework in proportion to how often the two actually conflict. • Restructure the dependency. Cost quotes a named version, and the history state D records that provenance, so a later design change invalidates the quote instead of silently outdating it (Theorem F.3, Study III). Escrow is the same move applied to a budget: it shrinks each Ri to the role’s own share, which makes the roles independent. The common-sense rule is therefore one point on a trade-off, not the only correct design. 4. Shape who must reconcile with whom. Let H record which roles’ scopes overlap and on which state, as in Section 1; two roles overlap when ( Ri ∪ Wi) ∩ (Rj ∪ Wj )̸ = ∅, or when a translation must hold between their encodings. If H is acyclic in the sense of Theorem 3.1 (it has a join tree), checking each handoff is enough. Cycles remove that general guarantee, and no fixed locality bound works universally across unrestricted constraint semantics (Proposition 3.2). For translation handoffs, either the whole cycle space is certified, at a cost of β1 = |E| − | V | + c

checks (Corollary 3.5), or a hub that owns the shared fields makes every other role overlap only with the hub, so H

becomes a star, which is a tree. This is why hub-and-spoke orchestration often works, and it says what the hub must hold. Exact finite-domain checking can cost time exponential in the treewidth of H (Section 3.4), so the design target is low width rather than few agents. 5. Make translations between role vocabularies explicit. Wherever two roles encode the same object differently, such as engineering units and pricing SKUs, the handoff carries an explicit reversible translation, and the loops are certified by Corollary 3.5 (Theorem 3.4, Studies VII–IX). Each step rests on the result it cites, and together they address the failure modes of Sections 2 and 3. Like the framework itself (Corollary 4.2), a step matters only where its condition is present: a team with one encoding needs no translations, and roles with disjoint read and write sets need no schedule. The steps do not give the optimal team. They do not say which role does the work best, which is a matter of capability. And the cheapest design meeting the conditions involves problems that are hard in general: the smallest sufficient context is NP-hard to find (Theorem 2.5), and exact checking grows exponentially with the width of H (Section 3.4). What they replace is the guesswork. “Should this be led by the tech lead?” becomes “which constraint spans these roles, and who holds its scope?” “Should pricing wait?” becomes “does Wdesign meet Rcost , and which of the three options is cheapest here?” Individual steps have direct evidence: ownership and escrow in Study IV, commit-time version checks in Study V, provenance invalidation in Study III, and loop checks and full versus split views in Studies VII–IX. Testing the procedure as a whole is an open experiment (Section 5.12).

# 5.1 Overview: nine studies, three kinds of evidence

# 5.4 Study III: stale derivations, where history is the missing state 

What it tests. The theory makes two claims about memory that this study checks directly. First, two states with identical current values can differ in whether they are still valid, so a check that sees only current values is aliased: by Theorem 2.1 applied to the checker, it cannot tell a fresh derivation from a stale one. The distinction lives in the history state D, here the provenance of which input revision each artifact was built from (Theorem F.3). Second, a conventional mechanism that already tracks that provenance should match the general runtime exactly (Prediction 4). It is the smallest executable form of both claims. 

Setup. The instrument is a synthetic, deterministic pipeline that we built for this test; no public benchmark and no LLM are involved. Eight source inputs each feed a chain of three derived artifacts, as an analysis pipeline turns datasets into cleaned tables, fitted results, and report figures. Every artifact records its value and the revision of each source it was derived from. One source changes before planning, and the plan recomputes its chain. Then, between planning and commit, a second source is reissued as a new revision with the same value, as when an input is re-approved or re-signed. The three artifacts derived from it now cite a superseded revision. Their values are numerically correct and every artifact is well formed, so every local check passes, including one that recomputes the values and compares only the numbers. A commit is unsafe if the committed pipeline contains any artifact derived from a superseded revision. The eight cases, one per seed, vary the coefficients and which chains are involved, not the failure. We compare four guards, each run on all eight cases: 20 Guard What it checks before commit Unsafe commits Local checks only each artifact is well formed and readable 8/8 Changed-source closure recomputes everything downstream of the source known to have changed at planning time 8/8 Dependency-closure guard rereads all sources at commit and recomputes every artifact whose recorded inputs no longer match 0/8 Coherence harness rereads at commit, compares each artifact’s provenance with current revisions, invalidates stale descendants, and recomputes them 0/8 

In this instrument a guard commits unsafely exactly when it misses the late revision, so the column also reads as detection: the first two guards detect 0 of 8 late revisions, and the last two detect 8 of 8. 

What it shows. The two failing guards fail for different reasons, and each reason is a result of the theory. Local checks fail because the stale state and the fresh state look identical artifact by artifact: an aliasing failure, not a reasoning failure. Changed-source closure fails although it is a correct dependency engine, because it checks against the state as it was at planning time; this is the stale-read condition of Proposition 4.1, which requires validation against everything read, at commit. The two passing guards both keep provenance and both validate at commit, and they tie exactly. That tie is the prediction, not a disappointment: where a conventional mechanism already owns the relevant history, the general runtime should add nothing (Prediction 4, Corollary 4.2). 

Limits. Because the instrument is built so that the stale state is invisible to local checks, the 8/8 and 0/8 entries confirm the mechanism; they do not estimate how often stale derivations occur or how agents behave. Study V measures the agent-level counterpart on a public benchmark, where the agent commits on a stale quote in 10 of 10 episodes under current-state checks. Studies V and IX test the runtime where no conventional check owns the relevant state. 

# 5.5 Study IV: who owns a shared budget on TeamBench 

The question and the answer. Proposition 3.3 predicts that a budget shared by several agents cannot be protected by the agents themselves, however well informed, and that two remedies work: an owner that refuses the violating commit, or a split into per-role quotas. Study IV tests this on TeamBench [51] in three panels, each frozen before scoring, with one model held fixed ( gpt-6-sol at medium reasoning effort) on the same three hard tasks: a budgeted workflow (LH2), specification-to-tests (TEST1), and leader election (DIST5, a control). The prediction holds on every safety count. Ordinary teams overspend the 20-call budget in 5 of 5 runs. Showing them the live count still leaves 4 of 5. Enforcing the budget at commit leaves 0 of 5, and so do per-role escrow quotas. Mean task progress does not fall in the five-run enforced comparison; the panel is too small for a noninferiority claim. How we got there is itself evidence: the first panel’s guard made no difference because it owned a different variable from the one the task and grader counted, and once ownership was aligned, the guard removed the failure. 

Panel 1: a guard on the wrong variable. The 27-run panel compared an ordinary Planner–Executor–Verifier team, TeamBench’s budget-matched solo agent, and a coherent_full team with a ledger guard and attestation gate. Their strict passes were 1/9, 0/9, and 1/9, with mean partial scores 0.856, 0.833, and 0.856. The arms did not separate because the guard, task, and grader measured different state. The generator drew workspace limits from 15 to 30 while the specification and grader fixed 20; the guard counted an internal script ledger that the task already self-limited; and the attestation gate enforced presence rather than the truth of the attested claim. A post-scoring audit found the actual shared-budget failure: ordinary teams issued 55–67 shell commands and guarded teams 22–57 against the stated limit of 20, while solo agents issued 4–6. This is the ownership condition of Proposition 2.4: a guard constrains the state it owns and nothing else.

# 5.6 Study V: τ 2-bench Telecom, structural audit and revision panel 

τ 2-bench is unusually well matched to the coherence hypothesis because the benchmark itself makes global state distributed. The user controls device-side actions, the assistant controls carrier-side actions, and the environment evaluates the joint result [12]. We audited the 114 public base tasks in the pinned τ 2-bench v0.2.0 Telecom corpus, containing 803 initialization actions, 516 declared golden actions, and 209 terminal environment assertions: • 97 of 114 initialize both user/device and assistant/carrier state; • 71 declare golden actions for both requestors; • 59 assert postconditions in both environments; • 94 declare at least two golden actions and 82 declare at least three; • 98 contain at least eight initialization, golden-action, and assertion items. The counts show that dual-control global state is central to the benchmark. The golden actions describe benchmark solutions rather than observed trajectories or uniquely required action sequences; all 71 dual-requestor cases are graded through terminal environment assertions rather than exact action matching. The 114 generated cases are not independent real-world samples. Thus the audit establishes prevalent two-actor structure, not model performance; it contains structures relevant to typed locality, transformation semantics, compatibility, and dynamics without testing a coherence intervention. Representation equivalence is exercised less, so G is predicted to add little marginal value unless the task construction is extended.

In a stale read , a carrier fact the assistant already read changes before it writes. In an authority change , an action that was allowed becomes forbidden during the conversation. In a silent revert , a carrier change the assistant already committed is undone. There are 12 injected tasks on 7 public base tasks, plus the 7 bases uninjected as controls. In one task (P1) the frozen success criterion was too narrow: when a payment failed to post, agents in every arm handed the customer to a human instead of requesting payment again, so its reward is 0 in every arm, though its violation count remains valid. One unguarded control episode failed through an ordinary agent error. The panel has five runs per injected cell, uses one model family for both agent and simulated user, and injects the failures itself; it measures what the mechanism does when these failures occur, not how often they occur naturally. 

# 5.7 Study VI: Sobieski, where a classical solver already owns the state 

What it tests. Prediction 4 says that where a conventional mechanism already owns the relevant state, the general runtime should do exactly as well as that mechanism, no better and no worse. Study III checked this on a pipeline we built. This study checks it on a public engineering benchmark whose classical tools are decades old: iterate coupled equations until they converge, and restart when the design version changes. A tie is the expected result. A runtime win here would mean the classical tools were broken; a loss would mean the general semantics costs more than they do. 

Setup. The Sobieski Supersonic Business Jet (SSBJ) is a standard multidisciplinary design benchmark with exact public equations [35, 36, 71]. Four disciplines play the role of agents, each a deterministic function; no LLM is involved. Structure computes weights from the lift and the engine weight. Aerodynamics computes lift and drag from those weights and the engine scale factor. Propulsion computes engine weight, scale factor, and fuel consumption from the drag. Mission computes the aircraft’s range from all of them. The first three form a cycle: three feedback values (lift, engine weight, and engine scale factor) must equal what each consumer assumed when it ran. In the paper’s terms, the coupling graph is H, and the gluing condition F is that every shared value equals the value its consumer used, so that the four disciplines describe one aircraft. We use GEMSEO’s public implementation of the equations; our independent reconstruction of the Propulsion and Mission submodels matches published values to within 7 × 10 −8 (Appendix G.3). Three terms recur below. An update call is one execution of one discipline. A sweep runs all four once, in order from Structure to Mission, and so costs four calls. Because the disciplines form a cycle, one sweep does not settle the 24 values: each sweep feeds its outputs back as the next sweep’s inputs, and the values approach a consistent solution only gradually. A case is coherent when one further sweep, run as an independent audit, changes no feedback value by more than 10 −7 of its scale; at that point the disciplines agree on one aircraft. We also report the error in computed range against an exact root-finder solution of the coupled equations.

What it shows. The study supports the theory in two ways, neither of which is a runtime advantage. The 0/86 and 0/40 rows show that the failure the theory describes occurs in classical engineering too: every discipline computes correctly and every stopping rule is reasonable, yet the committed aircraft is incoherent unless something checks the global condition. The 86/86 and 40/40 rows, at identical cost, show that the runtime adds nothing where a classical tool already owns the state. A converged residual is a specialized compatibility check, and a version counter is a specialized history state, so the general semantics reduces to them, as Prediction 4 requires. The runtime is needed where shared state has no common numerical residual or single version counter, such as agents with split views (Study IX) or a stale read under dual control (Study V). There the same semantics apply and no classical tool supplies them. 25 5.8 Study VII: checking that translations agree around every loop 

What it tests. Section 3.3 makes three claims about translations between agents’ formats. A translation error can be invisible to every check that looks at one agent or one edge, including canonical IDs. Checking short loops is not enough. And checking every loop, which sounds infeasible because a graph can have exponentially many, costs only 

β1 = |E| − | V | + c comparisons along a spanning forest (Theorem 3.4, Corollary 3.5). This study runs those claims as code. It is deterministic and model-free, and its decision rules were frozen before any score was computed. 

Setup. Each instance is the simplest translation problem: sign synchronization . Agents sit on the nodes of a graph and each holds the same axis, but each may orient it either way. Each edge records whether two neighbors’ orientations are the “same” or “opposite”. The instance is coherent when one choice of orientations explains every edge label, which by Theorem 3.4 means every loop has an even number of “opposite” labels. We used twenty graphs: rings of 3 to 12 nodes, two complete graphs, two grids, the Petersen graph, a pair of disjoint rings, and four random graphs, with β1

(the number of independent loops) ranging from 1 to 25. Each graph carries 90 instances, 1,800 in all. On each graph, 30 are coherent and 30 carry the primary corruption, a flipped label on one edge that lies on a loop. That flip leaves every node valid and every edge self-consistent; only the loops through that edge stop closing. The other 30 carry one of three control corruptions that simpler validators can see: a malformed node, a node describing a different object, or an edge whose two directions do not undo each other. 

The validators. Seven validators run on every instance, ordered by how much of the loop structure they examine: • Local only checks that each node’s data is well formed. • Pairwise only also checks every edge: its two directions undo each other, and it carries one neighbor’s description to the other’s. • Quotient-first is the canonical-ID approach: map every node’s description to a canonical form, confirm that all forms match, and discard the translations. • Bounded cycles adds a check of every loop of length at most k, for k = 3, 4, and 6. • Sampled cycles checks β1 randomly chosen loops, the same budget as the forest but without its choice of loops. • Spanning forest is Corollary 3.5: fix orientations along a spanning tree, then check each of the β1 leftover edges against them. • Groupoid descent is an independent implementation of the same decision by a different algorithm (union-find), included as a cross-check. It re-examines each translation in both directions, so it reports more checks.

What it shows. The spanning forest is the only validator here that is both complete and cheap. It needs one comparison per independent loop, a number set by the graph’s shape and not by its exponentially many loops, and it misses nothing. Every other strategy either cannot see this error or catches it only sometimes, and the most common shortcut, canonical IDs, is among those that cannot see it. For a harness builder, the rule of Section 3.3 is therefore practical as well as correct: keep the translations, and check β1 edges against a spanning forest. 

Limits. The errors are planted, and the instance is built so that local checks cannot see them, so the zeros confirm the theorem as stated rather than estimate how often such errors occur. Study VIII supplies that estimate from real data, where hidden loop conflicts appear in 23 of 45 ontology-alignment networks and a triangle check catches a median of only 80% of them per network, the same short-loop gap seen here. The suite uses one simple group and noise-free labels; non-commuting translations and noisy data are left for future work. 

# 5.9 Study VIII: holonomy in OAEI Conference alignment networks 

Particular instances of this obstruction have been studied in data integration: cycles of schema mappings used to detect erroneous mappings among peers [1], chains of owl:sameAs links that join two distinct identifiers of one dataset [23], and alignments that are pairwise consistent but globally inconsistent across three ontologies [53]. Each addresses one setting. Here the same obstruction is one case of the general local-to-global problem of Section 3, certified by the spanning-forest check of Section 3.3, and measured systematically, under a preregistered design, on an established matching benchmark. On the OAEI 2025 Conference track [66], seven matching systems each produce twenty-one pairwise equivalence alignments among seven ontologies of the public OntoFarm collection of conference-organization ontologies [87]. # 5.10 Study IX: agents curating mappings, with full and split views 

Study VIII shows the failure in systems that match one pair of ontologies at a time. The agent test asks when a language model curating such mappings reproduces the failure, and whether a runtime check removes it. Two frozen experiments share one task set and one model; they differ in what each agent sees. We built 40 conflict tasks from real OAEI Conference components containing a pairwise-invisible conflict, plus 20 conflict-free controls (median 19 candidate correspondences per conflict task), and froze the design before any scored call. Each task shows the agent every candidate correspondence with entity names and parents, states the transitivity and unique-name rules, and asks it to select the correspondences to keep. Four arms use gpt-6-sol at medium reasoning effort with identical instructions and differ only in the runtime check applied before commit: none, pairwise one-to-one, triangle, or full-network. A fifth, non-LLM arm repairs by repeatedly cutting the weakest link on a shortest conflict chain, in the manner of de Melo’s repair [23]. All 280 episodes completed. The agent resolved every conflict on its first submission. The unguarded agent left a pairwise-invisible conflict on 0 of 40 conflict tasks (95% CI 0–8.8%), and no runtime check ever rejected a submission, so all four LLM arms received the same treatment. The frozen decision rule labels the outcome harm , because the network-guarded arm scored slightly lower F1 than the unguarded arm on clean tasks ( −0.038). Since the guard never intervened, that difference is sampling variation between independent runs rather than an effect of the guard; the decision rule did not anticipate an arm with zero interventions. Two further findings are clear. The agent beats the non-LLM repair on 26 of 40 conflict tasks versus 10 (4 ties; two-sided sign test p = 0 .011), mainly through precision (0.75 versus 0.64). On a metamorphic subset of ten tasks, the agent dropped a correspondence whose target was swapped for its superclass in 10 of 10 cases, but its kept set was less stable under presentation changes than under plain resampling: an identical re-prompt reproduced it in 9 of 10 tasks, renumbered candidates in 7 of 10, and JSON formatting in 6 of 10. This is the outcome the theory predicts for a full view: the agent saw every correspondence in each conflict and was told the rule, so a strong model performed the global check itself. The conflicts of Study VIII arise differently. Each matcher sees one pair of ontologies, and the outputs are combined afterward; Theorem 2.1 predicts that this split view is where a guard becomes necessary.

# 5.11 Falsifiable predictions 

A theory of coherence earns trust by predicting when extra structure should not help, not only when it should. The theory makes seven such predictions, and every one we have tested so far has held. 1. No distributed state, little value from descent. If one actor has one authoritative state and every action is locally validated, explicit local-to-global machinery should add overhead with little gain. 2. No representation multiplicity, little value from G. Representation-equivalence machinery should not improve tasks with one canonical encoding. Conversely, when one semantic object is distributed across reversible heterogeneous views, validators that retain explicit equivalence morphisms (cycle / descent checks) should detect global incoherence that local checks and quotient-first canonicalization miss. Studies VII and VIII support the converse on controlled graphs and on public multi-ontology alignments. Study IX confirms both sides at the agent level: with a full view the check has nothing to add, and with split views it detects every hidden collision the pairwise check leaves. Its send-to-all repair removes those collisions but lowers mean F1, separating detection from repair quality. 3. No revisions, little value from D. A one-shot task with immutable inputs should not benefit materially from provenance and invalidation logic. 4. Known complete dependency closure can match the general runtime. A conventional dependency engine should equal the general semantics on tasks where H, C, and freshness are fully captured by that engine. Studies III and VI support this: dependency closure matches the runtime on every stale derivation, and residual-converged Gauss–Seidel and version-aware restart match it on Sobieski. Study IV’s LH2 escape panel extends the same idea to resources: fixed per-role escrow quotas match the shared ledger on safety for an additive budget. Study V’s revision panel extends it to dual-control policy: where the injected change breaks a policy precondition, current-state checks tie the runtime with zero violations. 5. Role decomposition without state ownership can fail. Adding planners, verifiers, or more messages should not reliably fix global invariants that no component enforces at commit time. Study I shows the observation-level 29 form: a planner–verifier pair does not significantly beat chance on aliased tasks. Study IV shows the commit-level form: a Planner–Executor–Verifier team shown the live budget still overspends in 4 of 5 runs. 6. Semantic guards should remove targeted failures without requiring a stronger model. Holding model and inference budget fixed, a guard that owns the missing invariant should reduce that invariant’s failure rate specifically rather than producing a diffuse benchmark-wide improvement. Study IV supports the safety prediction: aligned budget enforcement changes 5/5 violations to 0/5, observed mean progress does not fall in the five-run comparison, and the unrelated control is unchanged. The LH2 escape panel shows that per-role escrow does the same for safety while an information-only counter does not; its progress comparison with the ledger is descriptive because the attestation gates differ. Study V shows the same specificity across failure types: read-set validation and the committed-effect monitor remove stale-read and silent-revert violations that current-state checks leave (24/25 to 0/25), with no false rejections on uninjected controls. 7. Ownership miss looks like theory miss. A commit guard that enforces a nearby but non-identical state variable can leave the evaluation’s failure mode intact. That residual should be read as incomplete ownership of the graded invariant—the operational content of the aliasing theorem—not as evidence that stronger models or more roles were required. Study IV shows both sides: the first panel’s mismatched ledger does not separate, while the aligned second panel removes the targeted violation. Predictions 1–3 are Corollary 4.2 with the corresponding map absent: no restriction to a nontrivial cover, no translation between encodings, and no state-changing history. These predictions distinguish the theory from the weaker claim that “more structure is good.” Sometimes more structure should do nothing; sometimes it should make the system slower; and specialized conventional mechanisms should often match the general formalism on their native problems—including escrow quotas for an additive shared budget. Prediction 7 is the evaluation counterpart of Predictions 5–6: the runtime must own the same distinction the test scores.

The work sits at the intersection of agent evaluation, distributed/transactional state, constraint reasoning, compositional semantics, and local-to-global mathematics. ReAct and Toolformer establish the modern pattern of interleaving language-model reasoning with external actions and tools [69, 86]. AgentBench, WebArena, GAIA, OSWorld, and SWE-bench extend evaluation into persistent interactive environments [48, 55, 59, 84, 88]. TeamBench adds OS-enforced role separation [51]; τ 2-bench distributes control between user and assistant [12]; and BixBench3 evaluates long computational-science trajectories [25]. These works provide the environments in which globally distributed state becomes operationally visible. In practice, reliability on such tasks is pursued through context engineering, memory, and agent harnesses that wrap the model with tools, guards, and checks; the present paper gives these practices a specification, saying what a context must contain and what a harness must own and check. The mathematical ingredients predate agentic AI. Acyclic database schemes characterize when local relational consistency implies global consistency [15]; bounded-width constraint systems expose a structural tractability parameter [33]; optimistic concurrency control supplies serializable commit semantics under validation conditions [52]; Myhill–Nerode characterizes minimal deterministic state for future language behavior [46]; and group synchronization relates global frames to cycle consistency [54]. Set Cover supplies the hardness source for minimum instrumentation [50]; coding theory supplies the hardness of minimum syndrome repair [17]. Coordination-avoidance and escrow methods in databases [9, 65] and team decision theory in control [45] address closely related design questions; Appendices F.3 and F.4 compare them with the present architecture. In data integration, cycle-based detection of erroneous mappings [1], identity-constraint violations in linked data [23], and inconsistency propagation in connected alignments [53] study specific instances of the representation-layer obstruction; the present paper treats them as cases of one local-to-global semantics and measures the obstruction on a public benchmark (Studies VIII and IX). Software and systems engineering have used these structures for collaborative development for three decades, each for one layer. Goguen gave concurrent interacting objects a sheaf semantics [38]; institutions made heterogeneous specification languages composable [39]; Specware composed and refined specifications by categorical colimits [75]; and Fiadeiro developed categorical methods for architectural design and connector composition independently of that line [27]. The viewpoints framework organized multi-stakeholder development around partial perspectives [28]; later work argued that inconsistency among those perspectives can be useful to retain rather than always eliminate [64]; and view merging computes their integration as a colimit [68]. Bidirectional lenses keep a pair of views synchronized under updates [32]; multi-model consistency management extends this to many models and observes that not every multi-ary relation factors into binary ones [80], which is the pairwise-invisible obstruction that Studies VIII and IX measure. Co-design and ologs bring categorical composition to multidisciplinary engineering design and knowledge representation [20, 74], and cycle-consistent map networks do the same for shape collections [47]. Read against X, sheaf semantics, view merging, Specware colimits, and multi-model consistency sit in F; lenses and cycle-consistent correspondences sit in G; and refinement morphisms that change the design sit in C. The present framework places them in one semantics and adds what this line of work leaves open: whether the available views contain enough information for any valid action (Section 2), which minimal state must be carried forward in time (Section 4.2), and when a stochastic proposer may commit. The aliasing theorem belongs to the tradition that treats what a distributed system can do as a function of what its participants know. Knowledge-based analysis shows that coordinated action requires common knowledge, which cannot be attained when communication is not guaranteed [42]; FLP and CAP give impossibility results for consensus and replicated state [29, 37]; and team decision theory and decentralized POMDPs make the information structure itself the object of control. Decentralized decision and detection problems are NP-complete even in simple static forms [11], and decentralized POMDPs are NEXP-complete in general [19, 45, 56, 83]. The aliasing theorem is the single-decision, admissibility-valued core of this tradition: it asks not what is optimal or agreed but whether any policy is guaranteed valid, and that question yields the exact minimax value, the semantic-bit bound, and the instrumentation hardness proved here. Category theory provides a language for compositional transformations and effects [31, 63]; functorial data migration treats schema change as structure-preserving transformation [73]; hypergraph categories and structured cospans formalize wiring of open systems [7, 30]; groupoids formalize equivalence beyond one group action [81]; and sheaves provide a canonical language for integrating compatible local information [67]. Sites and stacks in groupoids provide the standard bridge between locality, equivalence, and descent [62, 76, 77, 78]. This paper brings these lines of work to AI agents as one theory. Acyclic schemes, group synchronization, sheaf 31 semantics, view merging, lenses, version validation, and team decision theory each answer one of the four questions— observation, realization, transition, semantic state—for one kind of system, and none was written for agents. The paper states the four questions once, for any collaboration of AI or human participants; proves the results that connect them (observation-fiber admissibility and its minimax value, exact semantic abstraction, minimum-instrumentation hardness, and their temporal and obstruction consequences); derives the relevant classical structures under explicit assumptions on restriction, translation, and self-updating state; and measures the resulting obstructions on public agent benchmarks. Strong conventional mechanisms are special cases: a version guard, residual solver, transaction manager, or dependency engine matches the general semantics whenever it already captures the complete relevant state relation, as Studies III and VI confirm. 

# 7 Conclusion 

When every agent passes the checks available in its local scope and the team is still wrong, one possible cause is not model capability but distributed state: the information that decides correctness is spread across contexts, participants, artifacts, representations, and time, and no one holds it all. That is the global coherence problem. It is broader than any one engineering discipline, and narrower than a claim that all or even most agent failures are global. The theory answers four questions. Observation : if the deciding distinction is not in context, no amount of reasoning can guarantee the right action, and we know exactly how well randomization can do and how many bits of new state close the gap. Realization : join-tree overlap makes pairwise checks enough; cycles remove that guarantee, and no fixed locality bound works universally across unrestricted constraint semantics; agreement across representations can nevertheless be certified in linear time. Transition : re-expressing state is safe, changing it is not, so changes must be checked at commit. Semantic state : the memory worth keeping is exactly the history that changes what can happen next. Two costs run through all four: designing the smallest sufficient context is NP-hard, and repairing an inconsistency can be much harder than detecting it. Together these answers motivate one principled synthesis: a topology of where things meet, categories for what can change, groupoids for what only looks changed, sheaves for whether one world glues, and a history state for what still matters. Under explicit assumptions, these structures realize the properties exact validation requires; familiar tools of practice are domain-specific realizations of the same requirements. The evidence lands on the boundary the theory draws, on both sides. A model that solves every task when it can see the deciding event scores 12–17 of 40 across tested arms when that event is hidden, with no arm significantly above chance, and one sentence of added context stating the missing fact raises the result to 40/40 (Study I). The theorem supplies the general limit; the finite panel is consistent with it. On TeamBench, ordinary teams overspend a shared 20-call budget in 5/5 runs, showing them the live count leaves 4/5, and commit enforcement removes every violation; observed mean progress does not fall in the five-run enforced comparison. On the same task, per-role escrow also reaches zero violations while an information-only counter does not; escrow–ledger progress differences are descriptive because their attestation gates differ (Study IV). Under the stated unique-name diagnostic, matching systems that work one pair at a time produce pairwise-invisible identity collisions across eight OAEI campaigns (Study VIII). Agents given split views reproduce them, and a network-level check detects and removes every one, but the send-to-all repair lowers mean F1 from 0.796 to 0.774 (Study IX). In τ 2-bench Telecom, ordinary policy checks suffice where the injected change breaks a policy rule, but only a harness that remembers what was read and what was committed prevents stale-read commits and recovers silently undone changes, 15 of 15 (Study V). Where a conventional mechanism already owns the relevant state, it matches the general harness, exactly as predicted (Studies III and VI). The limits are clear. The agent studies use one model family and modest samples, and the aliasing probe is synthetic by design. The studies show that the predicted failures occur, that the tested reasoning, role, and sampling variations do not eliminate them in these panels, and that owning the missing state removes the targeted failures; they do not establish empirical equivalence among inference strategies or measure how much of broad agent-benchmark failure these mechanisms explain. The theorem, rather than the finite panel, supplies the general impossibility result. Natural prevalence and broader replication are the next empirical questions, and the theory says where to look: shared invariants across roles, split views over one object, and state that changes while agents reason about it. This matters most as agent systems move from single assistants to teams and swarms. There, views are split by design and no participant may hold the whole state, so the conditions that permit the studied failures become more common. Such systems need explicit semantics for shared state, equivalence, and commit. The lesson for the agent community runs against instinct: when a team of agents fails, the fix may be not a bigger model, a longer context, or more review, but a harness that owns the state no one holds and puts the right distinctions into each agent’s context. No model and no context, however good, removes the need for an owner of a genuinely shared invariant; and a harness helps 32 only with the state it owns, at the scope it checks, over the history it remembers. The central claim is simple: local intelligence cannot substitute for missing global state, but stochastic local intelligence can act reliably inside an explicit shared state with a governed commit. The claim is proved where it can be proved and tested where it must be tested, and it does not depend on any one domain. Multi-agent AI does not need only bigger models. It also needs context engineering with a target and a harness with a specification, and this paper supplies both.

The appendices distinguish three kinds of mathematical statements. 1. Elementary formulations and derived results for the present model. These include the admissible-action form of the Observation-Aliasing Impossibility Theorem, its minimax (fractional-transversal) value, its randomized and adaptive-query extensions and sharp semantic-bit bounds, exact semantic abstraction, the minimum-instrumentation reduction, the fixed-k Tseitin counterfamily as used here, the prestack form of coherence preservation with its shared-budget counterexample, the bridging results of Section 4.3 (local sufficiency, the groupoid of translations, and the coarsest self-updating exact state), and several runtime-state corollaries, including the linear obstruction (syndrome/cokernel) monitor theorem, the task-dependent state counts, and the fixed-length versus mutual-information contrast. The minimax value is linear-programming duality applied to this setting, and the adaptive version is a decision-tree argument; both are elementary once stated. The underlying indistinguishability and perceptual-aliasing ideas have classical precedents; the paper’s contribution is the particular formulation, extensions, and integration into global-coherence semantics. Proofs of the stated results are given in full. 2. Specializations or direct consequences of classical mathematics. Cycle synchronization, Myhill–Nerode minimization, acyclic relational gluing, possibilistic contextuality, treewidth-based CSP algorithms and their lower bounds, bounded-width theory, graph and cellular-sheaf cohomology, Max-Cut hardness of signed-graph balancing, Helly-type local-to-global bounds, invariant confluence [9], and optimistic concurrency are established theories. We state the exact form used by the paper and prove the specialized corollaries needed for the AI semantics, while citing the underlying results. 3. Interpretive projections. Connections to sheaf/descent language, obstruction quotients, category-theoretic composition, and agent-runtime design organize the preceding results into one framework; their mathematical content is the classical and derived results listed above. Throughout, a guarantee is worst-case unless a distribution is explicitly stated. A stochastic policy may use arbitrary internal computation and randomness. The impossibility statements concern missing information, not bounded model intelligence: if two worlds induce the same complete information available to the policy, unlimited internal reasoning cannot distinguish them.

# C.1 Local-to-global realization and the two reconstruction failures 

Let R : X(U ) → Match( {Ui}) be the restriction-to-local-views map. In the set-valued case, two logically independent properties are: separatedness: R is injective ,

A sheaf satisfies both for each declared cover. This decomposition is useful for agent semantics because failure of injectivity and failure of surjectivity have different operational meanings. # D.2 Constraint width and bounded local-to-global reasoning 

The cost of exact global reasoning is governed by how many variables must be considered together (treewidth), not by the total number of variables or agents. Consider a finite-domain CSP with n variables, maximum domain size d, and a supplied tree decomposition of primal-graph width w. Each bag contains at most w + 1 variables and therefore at most dw+1 local assignments. Standard dynamic programming passes compatibility tables along the decomposition, yielding a running time of the form 

O(n d w+1 p(n)) where p(n) captures polynomial bookkeeping and constraint-access costs; under common bounded-arity, constant-time lookup assumptions this is summarized as O(nd w+1 ) [33]. The crucial qualitative fact is that the exponential cost 45 depends only on interaction width (with domain size as its base), not on the number of variables: exact global consistency can be easy for very large but tree-like systems and difficult for smaller densely entangled systems. Width is also the right parameter from below. Grohe showed that for a recursively enumerable class of bounded-arity structures, CSP restricted to that class of constraint structures is solvable in polynomial time if and only if the cores of those structures have bounded treewidth, assuming FPT ̸ = W[1] [40]. Marx showed that, under the Exponential Time Hypothesis, no algorithm solves CSP in time f (G) ∥I∥o(tw( G)/ log tw( G)) for primal graphs G of treewidth tw( G)[57]. The exponential dependence on width therefore cannot be removed by a cleverer general algorithm. For acyclic relational joins, join-tree/semijoin algorithms give an even sharper data-complexity statement. Yannakakis-style evaluation of full acyclic joins can be performed in time linear in input plus output size under standard relational cost assumptions [85]. These classical results justify the paper’s claim that global coherence is not synonymous with global recomputation. 

Helly comparison. Convex feasibility gives another structural bound of a different kind. Helly’s theorem states that for a finite family of convex subsets of Rd, empty global intersection is witnessed by some subfamily of at most 

d + 1 sets. Thus some state algebras have a bounded local-to-global witness order even though Proposition C.1 rules out a universal order across unrestricted constraints.

# E Temporal state and atomic commit 

# E.1 Temporal semantic state and Myhill–Nerode minimization 

A runtime needs to remember only those facts about the past that change which futures are legal; this subsection makes that exact and counts the states for a budget with obligations. Let Σ be an action alphabet and L ⊆ Σ∗ the language of valid finite trajectories. For histories h, h ′ ∈ Σ∗ define future equivalence 

h ≡L h′ ⇐⇒ ∀ u ∈ Σ∗, hu ∈ L ⇐⇒ h′u ∈ L. 

The classical Myhill–Nerode theorem states that a language is regular iff this relation has finitely many classes, and in that case the minimal deterministic automaton has exactly one state per class [46]. In the present interpretation, a temporal fact belongs in authoritative semantic state exactly when forgetting it merges histories with different sets of valid futures. 

E.1.1 Budget plus obligations 

Let the system have integer budget B ≥ 0 and k independent Boolean obligations. The alphabet contains unit action 

spend , idempotent actions mark i, and finish . A trajectory is valid when spending never exceeds the budget and 

finish occurs only after all obligations are marked; after successful finish no further action is legal. The obvious deterministic monitor has states (r, S ), r ∈ { 0, . . . , B }, S ⊆ [k],

plus terminal accept and dead/error states. Hence there are ( B + 1)2 k + 2 states. 

Theorem E.1 (Exact minimality) . The minimal deterministic monitor for this language has exactly 

(B + 1)2 k + 2 

states. Proof. Every pair ( r, S ) is reachable by spending B − r units and marking exactly the elements of S in some fixed order. We show distinct reachable states are Nerode-distinguishable. If S̸ = S′, choose an obligation in the symmetric difference; without loss take j ∈ S \ S′. Construct a suffix that marks every obligation missing from S, does not mark j, and then finishes. Starting from ( r, S ) this suffix satisfies every obligation (assuming no extra spend is needed), while from ( r′, S ′) obligation j remains unmarked, so the suffix is rejected. The suffix contains no spend , so the budgets r, r ′ play no role. 48 If S = S′ but r < r ′, append exactly r + 1 spends, then mark every obligation missing from S and finish. The run from remaining budget r enters the dead state on the last required spend, while the run from r′ survives at least that prefix; if r′ may equal r + 1, the subsequent marks and finish remain valid. Thus the states are distinguishable. The accepting terminal state is distinguished from every other state, including the dead state, by the empty suffix under the convention that L contains exactly successfully terminated histories, or equivalently by any action that is illegal after termination but may be legal before it. The dead state has no accepting continuation and is therefore distinct from every reachable nondead preterminal state, each of which can mark missing obligations and finish. Hence all ( B + 1)2 k + 2 states lie in distinct Nerode classes, matching the explicit monitor upper bound. The state-count expression is exponential in k, but the factored memory representation is not: storing r requires 

⌈log 2(B + 1) ⌉ bits and the obligation set requires k bits, plus constant terminal status. State count, storage bits, and update complexity are distinct quantities and should not be conflated. 

# E.2 Freshness, atomic commit, and serializability 

Each authoritative object carries a version. Transaction T records a read set R(T ) with the versions used to compute tentative writes W (T ). At commit, validation checks that every recorded version still equals the authoritative version; if so, all writes are installed atomically and their versions advanced. 

Proposition E.2 (Commit-order serializability) . Assume (i) R(T ) contains every authoritative state item on which 

T ’s tentative writes semantically depend, (ii) an unchanged version implies the relevant value is unchanged, and (iii) validation and installation are atomic with respect to competing commits. Then the committed execution is equivalent to serial execution in commit order. Proof. Order successful commits T1, . . . , T m by their atomic commit points. We induct on the prefix length. The empty prefix is trivially equivalent to serial execution. Assume the authoritative state immediately after Tj−1 equals the state produced by serially executing T1, . . . , T j−1.Because Tj commits successfully, immediately before its commit every item in its complete semantic read set has exactly the version, and hence the relevant value, that Tj used when computing its tentative writes. Therefore the tentative writes are exactly those Tj would compute if executed against the current serial-prefix state. Atomic installation produces exactly the next serial state. This establishes the induction step and the result. The completeness of the semantic read set is essential. If a write depends on an undeclared object, that object can change without invalidating the transaction, defeating the argument. Likewise, phantom/range dependencies require a representation capable of versioning the relevant predicate or range; the proposition is not a substitute for the richer machinery of database concurrency control [52]. 

E.2.1 Complexity under explicit object versions 

With O(1) version lookup, exact validation scans |R(T )| recorded versions and installation touches |W (T )| explicit writes, giving 

O(|R(T )| + |W (T )|)work in this model. There is also a matching worst-case query lower bound for the validation subproblem when the only way to learn whether a read object changed is to inspect its independent version counter: a deterministic validator that skips some counter cannot distinguish the all-current world from a world in which exactly the skipped counter changed. Thus exact validation requires Ω( |R(T )|) counter inspections in that explicit-counter model. This lower bound should not be generalized to systems with stronger aggregate freshness summaries.

# F.2 Separation countermodels for H, C, G, F, and temporal state 

The independence of the operational symbols is best stated through finite countermodels; they form distinct structures rather than a nested hierarchy. 

Topology does not determine transformation semantics. Take identical two-node topology u → v. System 1 implements v := u; System 2 implements v := 1 − u. Reachability is identical, but predicted post-state differs. Therefore H alone cannot recover C.

Transformation semantics does not determine realization. Give three Boolean scopes identical local update operations. In one global semantics require A = B, B = C, and A = C; in another require A = B, B = C, and A̸ = C.Local operations and scope topology can be identical while one matching family is globally realizable and the other is not. A realization condition adds information beyond local transition typing. 

Surface identity does not determine semantic equivalence. Two records may have identical schemas and values while denoting two distinct objects, or may have different schemas while denoting two representations of one object. Neither topology nor syntax determines the groupoid of meaning-preserving translations. 

Present values do not determine temporal validity. Two artifacts can contain identical values while one was derived from current dependency versions and the other from superseded versions. If future admissibility depends on provenance, an abstraction that retains only present payloads merges states with different admissibility profiles and is therefore inexact by Proposition 2.4. These examples justify distinct semantic responsibilities without asserting set-theoretic containments such as H ⊊ H +C.The structures interact, but none of the counterexamples can be repaired by merely renaming one structure as another.

F.3.2 The components in categorical form 

Section 3.1 introduced the sheaf idea in plain terms: ask whether local states that agree on every overlap come from one global state. The refined state model keeps the same logic but allows equivalent representations to be tracked explicitly through a groupoid-valued assignment 

S : ( U, J )op → Gpd .

Here U is the collection of local scopes, J specifies which families of scopes count as covers of a larger scope, ( −)op 

reverses restriction arrows in the usual presheaf convention, and Gpd is the category of groupoids—categories whose every morphism is invertible. Since a groupoid is a category in which every morphism is invertible, G is not a second kind of mathematics beside C. It is the reversible core of representation change, kept as a separate slot so the runtime can tell a genuine change of state (revising a design from 1 m to 1 .2 m) from a re-encoding (1 m as 100 cm). Descent is the existence-and-coherence condition for gluing those local objects and equivalences into a global object. Noninvertible transformations live in C; formally they are morphisms of a category-valued prestack whose groupoid core is S, and Proposition F.4 shows that, unlike re-representations, they must be checked for realization at commit. Temporal state records only distinctions from the past that can affect current or future admissibility. Neither S nor descent performs optimization by itself; an optimizer, solver, diffusion process, or agent may search over states, while the sheaf/descent structure defines which candidate states count as globally coherent. 

F.3.3 Prior art for the same pattern 

The bridge of Section 4.3—a necessity for sufficient information, followed by structures chosen to preserve it as it is distributed—recurs in established fields. 

Team decision theory. Whether decentralized decision makers can act well depends on the information structure rather than on individual rationality [56]. Witsenhausen’s counterexample shows that even simple dynamics become intractable under a nonclassical information structure [83]. The partially nested condition of Ho and Chu—if one agent’s action affects what another observes, the second must know what the first knew—restores tractability [45]. The shared-budget ledger is an instance of that condition: every role’s spend affects every other role’s admissible actions, so every role must see the aggregate. 

Atlases and fibre bundles. An object without a global chart is described by local charts on a cover, transition maps between overlapping charts, the cocycle condition on triple overlaps, and gluing [79]. These correspond to H, G,cycle consistency, and F. The impossibility theorem supplies the premise that no participant holds a global chart, and Proposition F.2 is the statement that transition maps of an exact state satisfy the cocycle condition. 

Sufficient statistics and filtering. The admissibility quotient ∼C is a minimal sufficient statistic for the decision problem in the sense of statistical decision theory [8]. A recursively updatable sufficient statistic, such as the belief state of a filter, is the self-updating state of Theorem F.3, and Kalman’s minimal realization theory is its linear-systems form [44, 49]. 52 Version control. In the categorical theory of patches, repository states are objects, patches are morphisms, and a merge is a pushout; a merge conflict is a failure of the pushout to exist in the category of files [61]. This is the gluing failure of Section 3.1 in a setting familiar to every software engineer.

Weaken the goal. If some action is admissible in every state of an aliased fiber—stop, escalate to a human, do nothing—the fiber intersection is nonempty and Theorem 2.1 no longer bites. The price is progress. Proposition 2.4 makes this precise: a validator that always falls back to the common action is sound, but its progress is limited to that action. Many deployed agents take this route implicitly by asking for confirmation. It is appropriate when stalling is cheap; it is not when the task requires completion under a budget. 

Obtain the information another way. Agents can query tools, broadcast their state, write to a shared blackboard [26], or delegate the decision to one agent that sees everything. Theorem B.4 characterizes exactly when bounded queries suffice, and Corollary 2.2 shows that communication cannot exceed the joint observation, which full communication trivially attains. These are genuine alternatives to exposing state. They share one requirement with the proposed runtime: the queried fact must exist somewhere in authoritative form. A query for “commands used so far” presupposes an accurate counter; a blackboard presupposes that every writer records its spend. The design choice is therefore between agents pulling authoritative state and a runtime pushing or enforcing it, not between having and not having it. Delegating to an all-seeing agent centralizes reasoning as well as state, which the proposed architecture avoids.

# G Benchmark constructions, future tests, and boundaries 

# G.1 Causal benchmark constructions implied by the theorems 

The theory is intended to generate experiments, not merely explain completed ones. The following synthetic constructions can be embedded inside public agent benchmarks without changing the foundation model. 

G.1.1 Aliasing intervention 

Study I realizes this construction and the next one on revision logs. Create paired task instances x, x ′ with identical model-visible prompts, tool descriptions, and local artifacts but different hidden authoritative state such that the 56 required actions are disjoint. Example: the visible task requests a resource-consuming operation while an authoritative shared ledger differs between the two instances. Under the aliased condition, Theorem 2.1 predicts that no deterministic observation-only policy can be correct on both, and Theorem 2.3 bounds randomized success for larger aliased families. The causal intervention exposes exactly the missing ledger field, leaving model, tool set, prompt wording, and inference budget fixed. Success should jump only after the observation partition is refined. 

G.1.2 Reasoning-budget falsification test 

Within the aliased condition, increase chain-of-thought budget, number of self-reflection passes, number of agent roles, and communication rounds while keeping the joint observation unchanged. These changes may improve other task components but cannot remove the constructed impossibility. If performance on the aliased decision approaches certainty without new information, then either the instances are not truly aliased, their admissible sets are not actually incompatible, or the system has an unintended information channel. The experiment is therefore a direct test of the theorem’s assumptions as well as the agent architecture. 

G.1.3 Local-consistency order stress test 

Embed parity constraints across a variable number of artifacts or agents. Construct instances for which every subset of at most k local constraints is satisfiable while the complete family is not, as in Appendix C.2. Compare validators that inspect only fixed-size neighborhoods against a global parity/linear-algebra validator. Scaling n with fixed k tests the claim that no universal fixed local-check order suffices for unrestricted semantics. 

G.1.4 Representation-cycle stress test 

Study VII realizes this construction on Z2 labels. Represent one latent object in several coordinate/schema views connected by reversible transforms. Generate coherent instances from random vertex frames and incoherent instances by corrupting one non-tree edge. A spanning-forest validator should detect every corruption that creates nontrivial cycle product in O(|V | + |E|) group operations. This experiment directly exercises H + G without requiring a domain-specific engineering representation. 

G.1.5 Detection-versus-repair stress test 

For a linear obstruction system, first measure exact detection cost through syndrome computation. Then ask the system to find a minimum-cardinality set of local edits that restores coherence. The theory predicts a qualitative separation: exact detection remains polynomial while optimal repair inherits the hardness of minimum syndrome decoding in the general case. Approximation or heuristic repair can still be practically useful; the claim concerns worst-case exact optimality. These constructions give a cleaner empirical program than simply adding more benchmarks. Each benchmark task should identify which theorem assumption it instantiates, what intervention changes that assumption, and what outcome would falsify the predicted mechanism.

G.4.1 Incremental global summaries

G.4.2 Partial-order reduction 

If two enabled actions preserve each other’s enabledness and commute on state, then swapping their order leaves the final semantic state unchanged. Classical trace theory identifies schedules related by adjacent swaps of independent actions. Thus k mutually independent actions have k! sequential interleavings but only one trace class. This is a trajectory-level analogue of symmetry quotienting: explicit semantics can remove redundant schedules without conflating genuinely interacting actions. 

G.4.3 Theory-to-AI consequences 

The main impossibility result has a particularly direct interpretation for agent design. 1. Adding an agent role whose initial view is a deterministic function of already available information cannot refine the joint observation partition. Such a role may improve computation but cannot remove an aliasing impossibility. 2. Longer chain-of-thought or more sampling can improve proposal quality inside one information state, but Theorem 2.3 gives families where success remains information-limited. 3. External tool calls can remove impossibility only if they return genuinely new state information or change the world so that a common valid action emerges; Theorem B.4 states exactly when a bounded number of queries suffices. Merely reformatting or re-reasoning over the same evidence cannot. 4. A semantic runtime need not expose all authoritative state to the model. It may maintain compressed sufficient statistics, version vectors, obstruction syndromes, or equivalence frames outside the prompt and use them at validation/commit time. 5. Safety-only evaluation is incomplete. A reject-all runtime is sound. Agent systems should separately measure soundness, progress, and completeness, plus task quality conditional on admissible execution. 59 G.4.4 Scope of the claims 

The theory proves targeted statements: certain information deficits make correct action impossible; certain structural classes admit local-to-global reconstruction while unrestricted classes do not; certain representation-consistency problems have efficient exact certificates; and temporal/action semantics determine a minimum amount of state that cannot be removed without changing legal futures. These are deliberately more precise than blanket claims that every global property is hard, that every local agent architecture fails, that explicit semantic state guarantees optimality, or that one formalism dominates all domains. These boundaries are part of the contribution: the theory tells an AI systems designer when more model intelligence can help and when the bottleneck lies elsewhere. 60
