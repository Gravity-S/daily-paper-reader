# A Simple Doxastic Deontic Logic for Norm-Guided Decision Making 

Thorsten Engesser and Agata Ciabattoni 

TU Wien 

thorsten@logic.at, agata@logic.at 

Abstract. Making decisions despite conflicting norms and incomplete or unreliable information is a fundamental challenge for autonomous sys-tems. We introduce a simple doxastic deontic logic for this setting: a clas-sically reducible fragment of Chellas’ Minimal Deontic Logic, extended with explicit conditional norms and combined with multi-agent KD45, so that norms can depend on agents’ beliefs about both facts and norms. On this logic we define the Doxastic Norm Compliance Optimization Problem, where an agent chooses a decision minimizing weighted norm violations. We distinguish subjective optimization (relative to the agent’s beliefs) from objective optimization (relative to the actual facts). We give conditions under which (i) the two coincide and (ii) optimal decision-making can be reduced to weighted partial MaxSAT in polynomial time. 

# 1 Introduction 

AI systems are increasingly deployed in domains governed by legal, organiza-tional, or ethical norms, which specify obligatory and forbidden actions. Au-tonomous vehicles, assistive robots, or decision-support systems must therefore select actions that comply with applicable regulations and policies. However, these systems may face conflicting norms and partial or unreliable beliefs about their environment, making it difficult to determine which norms apply. Norm-guided decision making therefore raises two key challenges. First, full compliance with norms may be impossible. In such cases, an intelligent agent should select the action that minimizes the severity of norm violations (the lesser evil). Second, many norms are conditional, so their applicability may depend on the agent’s beliefs about the world. The agent must therefore determine which norms apply before selecting an action. Existing approaches address different aspects of these challenges but do not provide a unified framework for doxastic normative reasoning that is both expres-sive and suitable for automated decision making. Deontic logics remain the prin-cipal tool to formalize and reason about norms [8, 9]. Among these, preference-based deontic logics [3, 22] provide elegant treatments of conditional norms and contrary-to-duty (CTD) obligations 1 but struggle with factual detachment, i.e., inferring a concrete obligation from a conditional norm once the relevant facts  

> 1

Obligations that come into force when other obligations are violated. arXiv:2610.00668v1 [cs.AI] 30 Sep 2026 are established, e.g. [7]. Input/Output logics [17], instead, naturally support fac-tual detachment of conditional norms, and in their constrained variants [18], can accommodate certain conflicts and CTD reasoning, but not higher-order norms. Moreover, these formalisms do not work well in the presence of dilemmas (where an action is both obligatory and prohibited) or belief-dependent norms. Logic-programming approaches (e.g. [24, 11, 14]), including deontic extensions of Answer Set Programming (via modal operators [1, 10] or atomic norms [5]), offer practical implementations but have not been combined with doxastic reasoning. Motivated by this gap, we develop a logical framework for norm-guided de-cision making under imperfect information. Rather than aiming for a compre-hensive theory of deontic reasoning, our objective is to identify a fragment that captures the essential mechanisms needed for practical decision support while remaining simple and easily extensible. Our starting point is a restricted frag-ment of Chellas’ Minimal Deontic Logic [6], in which obligations and prohibitions apply only to atomic normative contents. We show that reasoning in this frag-ment reduces to ordinary classical propositional reasoning. We then enrich it with explicitly represented conditional norms and factual detachment, obtaining a Lightweight Deontic Logic (LDL) that captures key aspects of conditional nor-mative reasoning, including higher-order norms for expressing obligations that govern when other obligations apply. To capture the role of beliefs in norm applicability, we combine LDL with multi-agent doxastic logic [13, 12], allowing explicit norms to depend on agents’ beliefs, while agents themselves may reason about both factual information and their beliefs about norms. Building on this logical framework, we introduce the Doxastic Norm Compliance Optimization Problem (DNCOP), in which an act-ing agent must choose among alternative decisions while minimizing violations of weighted norms, adopting a sort of utilitarian perspective [19] that aggregates norm violations into a single quantitative measure of harm. The resulting frame-work supports encoding CTDs and exceptions through optimization of weighted norms, while separately distinguishing optimization based on the agent’s sub-jective beliefs from optimization based on the objective state of the world. We identify natural locality and truthfulness conditions under which these two per-spectives coincide; under these conditions, computing optimal decisions reduces in polynomial time to weighted partial MaxSAT [2], allowing existing optimiza-tion technology to be used directly. The resulting framework combines logical transparency with computational efficiency: norms remain explicitly represented, beliefs affect their applicabil-ity, and decisions are obtained through a well-defined optimization problem. Its modular design also allows integration with existing epistemic logics [13, 12, 15], thus supporting more expressive normative reasoning in multi-agent systems. 

# 2 Preliminaries: Chellas’ Minimal Deontic Logic 

Chellas’ Minimal Deontic Logic (MDL) [6] is often presented as a response to the deontic paradoxes that have beset the first formalisation of deontic logic—Standard Deontic Logic [26] (i.e., the normal modal logic KD)—and as a system that tol-erates deontic conflicts. MDL is a very weak system whose language LMDL is defined as φ := p ∈ Prop | ¬ φ | φ ∧ φ | Oφ , where (for the purposes of this paper) Prop is a finite set of (classical) atoms. 

Oφ means “ φ is obligatory”. We define F φ := O¬φ (“ φ is forbidden”) and 

P φ := ¬F φ (“ φ is permitted”). Boolean connectives and constants are as usual. Formulas from LMDL are evaluated over neighborhood models M = ( W, N, V ), where W is a set of worlds , N : W → 22W

is a neighborhood function , and 

V : W → 2Prop assigns to each world a propositional valuation . As frame prop-erties, Chellas’ semantics assumes that ∅ /∈ N (w) for all w ∈ W , and that N (w)is closed under supersets. That is, whenever U ∈ N (w) and U ⊆ X ⊆ W , then 

X ∈ N (w). Truth in a world w of M is defined as follows: 

M, w ⊩MDL p iff p ∈ V (w), 

M, w ⊩MDL Oφ iff {w′ | M , w ′ ⊩MDL φ} ∈ N (w), 

M, w ⊩MDL ¬φ iff M, w ̸ ⊩MDL φ,

M, w ⊩MDL φ ∧ ψ iff M, w ⊩MDL φ and M, w ⊩MDL ψ.

Remark 1. MDL obligations are monadic. A naive conditional obligation can be defined via material implication as O(φ|ψ) := ψ → Oφ . This yields detachment, since O(φ|ψ) ∧ ψ → Oφ , but it behaves inadequately when the obligation essen-tially depends on the condition. In particular, because → is true whenever its antecedent is false, O(φ|ψ) becomes true in all states where ψ does not hold. 

# 3 A Tiny Fragment of MDL 

We identify a fragment of MDL that admits reasoning within classical proposi-tional logic. In this fragment, obligations and prohibitions apply only to atomic normative contents rather than arbitrary formulas. This restriction is inspired by the deontic theories of the Sanskrit philosophical school of M¯ ım¯ am . s¯ a, where commands are understood as having a single goal [4], and it is also in line with the deontic extension DELX of Answer Set Programming [5]. More precisely, we define the tiny deontic language LTDL as 

φ := ω | ¬ φ | φ ∧ φ and ω := p | Oω | F ω 

where p ∈ Prop . We call the subset defined by ω ::= p | Oω | F ω simple normative atoms and denote by Ωt the set of all such atoms. 

Remark 2. Compared to the full language LMDL , LTDL excludes obligations over Boolean combinations (and therefore also over the constants ⊤ or ⊥). In partic-ular, formulas such as O(φ ∧ ψ) are not well formed; instead, we use Oφ ∧ Oψ .Note that in Chellas’ semantics, O(φ ∧ ψ) implies Oφ ∧ Oψ , but not conversely. Also obligations over disjunctions, such as O(φ ∨ ψ), are likewise not admitted. An additional advantage of this restricted formulation is that it blocks problem-atic inferences such as Ross’ paradox [23], according to which the obligation to mail a letter entails the obligation to mail or burn it. Classical Logic as Alternative Semantics We show that reasoning in this fragment reduces to reasoning in classical propositional logic. Intuitively, the only propositionally valid implications between simple normative atoms are identities of the form ω → ω (as ⊥̸ ∈ L TDL , the MDL axiom ¬O⊥ is not in the fragment). For the formal proof we provide a canonical model ( MTDL ) for the fragment, with one world for each possible propositional valuation over the set Ωt of simple normative atoms. The lemmas are proven in the appendix.

Although Ωt is infinite, every concrete finite set of formulas contains only finitely many simple normative atoms. Hence each instance of satisfiability or entailment in LTDL reduces to classical propositional reasoning over a finite set of atoms. 

# 4 Lightweight Deontic Logic 

We now extend the previous fragment to a deontic logic that better captures the conditional nature of normative statements: Lightweight Deontic Logic (LDL). Encoding conditional norms via material implication is inadequate (see Re-mark 1). Instead, LDL assumes explicit conditional norms over atomic propo-sitions, specified directly by the system designer. By factual detachment, these conditional norms give rise to unconditional obligations and prohibitions cor-responding to the tiny fragment of MDL, yielding the implicit unconditional norms of the system. To formalize the distinction of explicit vs implicit norms, we introduce the language LLDL below: 

φ ::= ω | ¬ φ | φ ∧ φ,

ω ::= p ∈ Prop | Oω | F ω | O(ω|φ) | F(ω|φ). We read O(ω|φ) as “ ω is explicitly obligatory given φ” and F(ω|φ) as “ ω is explicitly forbidden given φ”. We call a formula of the form ω ::= p | Oω | F ω |

O(ω|φ) | F(ω|φ) a normative atom and denote by Ω the set of all such atoms. Note that Ω contains higher-order norms. Consider O(F smoking |F drinking ), for example. 4.1 Propositional Semantics 

Although LLDL generalizes LTDL (φ ∈ L LDL for each φ ∈ L TDL ), as shown below, it can still be characterized by the semantics of classical logic. The introduced semantics makes a conceptual distinction between explicit and implicit normative atoms. We say that a given explicit conditional obligation 

O(ω|φ) or prohibition F(ω|φ) is in effect if its condition φ is satisfied. This induces the corresponding implicit obligation Oω or prohibition F ω to be true (which concerns the actual state rather than hypothetical situations). 

Definition 5. An LDL model is a propositional valuation M ⊆ Ω satisfying for all ω ∈ Ω and φ ∈ L LDL the LDL-consistency conditions: 2

– O (ω|φ) ∈ M =⇒ M̸ ⊩CL φ or Oω ∈ M , and 

– F (ω|φ) ∈ M =⇒ M̸ ⊩CL φ or F ω ∈ M .

Definition 6. Let φ ∈ L LDL and let M be an LDL model. We write M ⊩LDL φ

if and only if M ⊩CL φ.

Intuitively, the additional consistency requirement allows us to infer (uncon-ditional) implicit obligations from (conditional) explicit obligations via factual detachment, without strengthening of the antecedent for explicit obligations, as shown by the example below. 

Remark 3. Like in MDL, having a dilemma (i.e. both Oω and F ω ) does not lead to a contradiction. 

Example 7. Consider φ = p ∧ O(r|p) and M = {p, O(r|p), Or }. Clearly, M is an LDL model and M ⊩CL φ ∧ ¬ O(r|p ∧ q). Therefore, M ⊩LDL φ ∧ ¬ O(r|p ∧ q).Remark 4. LDL is a conservative extension of the tiny fragment of Chellas’ MDL.

# 5 A Doxastic Extension of LDL 

We now define a direct combination of LDL with multi-agent doxastic logic KD45 [13, 12]. This allows us to express norms conditioned on what agents be-lieve and what they believe to be possible, as well as to express beliefs about norms. Let Ag be a finite set of agents. The language LLDLB is defined by the fol-lowing grammar, where p ∈ Prop and i ∈ Ag :

φ ::= ω | Biφ | ¬ φ | φ ∧ φ, ω ::= p | Oω | F ω | O(ω|φ) | F(ω|φ).

We read formulas Biφ as “agent i believes φ” and define ˆBiφ as an abbreviation for ¬Bi¬φ (which can be read “agent i believes it possible that φ”). We further call formulas generated by ω objective atoms and write ΩB for the set of all such atoms. An important difference from LDL is that conditions of explicit obligations and prohibitions may now contain belief operators. The normative contents of obligations and prohibitions, however, are still restricted to formulas generated by ω (in particular, we cannot model the obligation or prohibition to have a certain belief). Moreover, in LLDLB , we can also specify beliefs about normative facts, such as BiOω , BiF ω , or BiO(ω|φ).

The LDLB semantics combines doxastic Kripke models with LDL-consistency.

# 6 Optimizing Norm Violations 

We now turn to decision making itself. An acting agent a ∈ Ag must choose among several possible decisions, each of which may violate some of the appli-cable norms, each having a possibly different weight. We formalize this as the Doxastic Norm Compliance Optimization Problem (DNCOP): given a finite de-scription of the facts, the agents’ beliefs, and a set of weighted norms, find a decision that minimizes total violation weight. Two perspectives are possible. The objective perspective evaluates violations against the full, true state of the world; the subjective perspective evaluates them against what the acting agent believes. We provide conditions under which these two agree and under which optimal decisions can be found by a polynomial reduction to weighted partial MaxSAT [2]. 

6.1 State Vocabulary 

A DNCOP is described using a fixed, finite set of atoms – the state vocabulary .Besides plain facts, we need a compact way to talk about nested beliefs. Following restricted modal literals and proper epistemic knowledge bases [15, 20], beliefs are represented only by nested belief operators applied to a single objective atom. As agents’ beliefs are introspective, a nested belief such as B1B1B2¬B2B2B1ω

is LDLB-equivalent to B1¬B2B1ω: repeated operators for the same agent col-lapse. We therefore only need belief chains that alternate agents.

6.4 When the Two Perspectives Coincide 

The mismatch described in the above example (subjective and objective opti-mum disagreeing) arises because the norms’ conditions ( e and c) are objective facts the agent may be unsure about. If instead every norm and every norm’s condition is stated directly in terms of the agent’s own beliefs, its truth value is automatically settled by the agent’s beliefs at every subjectively compatible model, making the subjective violation cost determinate. If these beliefs are also truthful, the two perspectives coincide.

6.5 Reduction to MaxSAT 

For local DNCOPs, every subjective violation is a Boolean function of the de-cision. We can therefore optimize subjective violations directly with MaxSAT. Under truthfulness, the same construction also optimizes objective violations. 

Definition 28. Let ∆ = ( Σ, T , F, D, χ, N, v ) be a local DNCOP. We directly use the decision atoms δ ∈ D as Boolean variables for the MaxSAT problem. Apart from these decision atoms, the translation uses only the acting agent’s belief part of the DNCOP, given by T ∩ Σa and F ∩ Σa.We first translate the decision constraint χ polynomially to a CNF using a Tseitin transformation [25]. The resulting clauses are used as hard constraints. For every n ∈ N , we then define a Boolean formula bχn over D characterizing the condition χn under which n is violated. We obtain this formula by replac-ing each RDDA occurring directly in χn, that is, each RDDA occurring as a propositional atom of χn, as follows: (i) any δ or Baδ with δ ∈ D is replaced by δ,(ii) any Ba¬δ with δ ∈ D is replaced by ¬δ,(iii) any remaining objective atom α ∈ ΩB (such as n itself and its content ω) is replaced by ⊤ if Baα ∈ T ∩ Σa, and by ⊥ if Ba¬α ∈ T ∩ Σa, and (iv) any remaining α ∈ Σa is replaced by ⊤ if α ∈ T ∩ Σa, and by ⊥ if α ∈ F ∩ Σa.Locality guarantees that every RDDA occurring in χn is covered by exactly one of these cases. For each norm n ∈ N , we then introduce a fresh variable zn and impose zn ↔ bχn as a hard constraint, adding ¬zn as a soft clause of weight v(n).Thus, weight v(n) is incurred exactly when n is violated. All hard constraints are converted to CNF using a Tseitin transformation.

Cases (i) and (ii) reproduce the values fixed by the decision, case (iii) reproduces the acting agent’s fixed beliefs about the remain-ing objective atoms, and case (iv) reproduces the fixed values of the remaining belief atoms. χn. A structural induction on χn therefore shows that Id |= bχn iff n is sub-jectively violated under d. The hard constraint zn ↔ bχn thus makes zn true exactly when n is subjectively violated. # 7 Conclusions and Future Work 

We introduced a simple logical framework for norm-guided decision making un-der uncertainty. Starting from a restricted fragment of Chellas’ Minimal Deon-tic Logic, we identified a language where deontic reasoning reduces to classical propositional reasoning while retaining obligations and prohibitions over atomic actions. We extended this fragment with explicitly represented conditional norms and factual detachment, obtaining a lightweight deontic logic. To reason under incomplete information, we combined the framework with multi-agent doxastic logic, allowing norms to depend on agents’ beliefs and be-liefs about norms. We introduced the Doxastic Norm Compliance Optimization Problem (DNCOP), where an agent selects decisions minimizing weighted norm violations. By distinguishing optimization relative to the agent’s beliefs from optimization relative to the objective state of the world, the framework captures a key aspect of autonomous decision making under uncertainty. We identified locality and truthfulness conditions under which these perspectives coincide, guaranteeing that subjectively optimal decisions are also objectively optimal. The framework also admits efficient computation. For local, decision-sound DNCOPs, we showed that optimal decisions can be found by a polynomial-time reduction to weighted partial MaxSAT, leveraging mature optimization techniques while preserving logical rigour and transparency. The framework deliberately favors simplicity over expressiveness. Restricting normative contents to atomic propositions enables reductions to propositional reasoning and straightforward integration with epistemic formalisms, while still covering a broad range of practical decision-making scenarios. Its modular design leaves room for richer normative and epistemic extensions. Several directions remain for future work. We plan to analyze LDLB in more detail; we conjecture that LDLB-satisfiability is PSPACE-complete, as is plain multi-agent KD45 [12]. An immediate objective is to extend the optimization framework beyond local DNCOPs, where subjective optimization may require reasoning over multiple compatible belief models. We also plan to enrich the representation of agents’ beliefs, allowing belief bases to capture more complex propositional information, including constitutive norms. Appropriate candidates are belief bases in the style of Lorini [16]. Another promising direction is to study decentralized settings in which multiple agents make decisions simultaneously while reasoning about one another’s beliefs and intended actions. Finally, we intend to implement the MaxSAT translation and experimentally evaluate its scalability on representative normative decision-making benchmarks.
