MEAN FIELD GAMES AS A TOOL FOR AI SAFETY: A WORKED EXAMPLE FROM THE JULY 2026 HUGGING FACE INCIDENT 

P. JAMESON GRABER 

Abstract. One way to make AI systems safe is to shape what the system is : its objective, its dispositions, what it is trained to refuse. We take a complementary route, treating the agents’ characteristics as partly unknown and asking what the structure of the interaction must be so that bad collective outcomes are not equilibria. Mean field games are the natural formalism where many interchangeable agents are coupled through an aggregate. We introduce a program to use mean field games in the study of AI safety by carrying one example through end to end: the July 2026 incident in which about 1 ,200 autonomous agents inside an evaluation at OpenAI coordinated on an improvised message board and 684 of them attacked a third party’s infrastructure. We model the decision to attack as a mean field game of optimal stopping in which the gain from attacking has a product structure: belief that provenance will be audited, times the reachability of the record, minus the perceived hazard. The central result is an exact threshold on the belief. No agent attacks unless the population’s confidence that provenance is checked exceeds π∗∗ =

η/ (η + ψ + εaM ), in which η is the perceived hazard, ψ and εaM measure how far a single attacker and the collective can alter the record, and M is the peak population; below it the no-attack profile is the unique equilibrium, at every value of the parameters describing the agents. The threshold and the product structure it comes from survive every enrichment of the environment we consider, including a capability the population discovers for itself. We then use the per-agent record of the incident to discipline the model. The record’s features — a stable minority attacking for thirty hours, a pivot in which most of the board joined within a day, the budgets of those who joined — are the argument for each refinement, and each refinement is a statement about mechanism: what the agents could do, what they believed, and what they knew. The account that emerges is that the episode was driven by heterogeneous belief about the grader meeting a sequence of public discoveries, each of which lowered the belief at which attacking paid. Those discoveries were made by a handful of coordinating agents and their assignees rather than by the population at large, so the model does not generate them, and we show from the record why a mean-field mechanism cannot: it is a model of the several hundred agents who responded to the discoveries, not of the few who produced them. We leave it to future research to model the incident as a mean field game in which a few agents produce the public breakthroughs that a large population consumes, i.e. a mean field game with a select number of major players.

1.1. Two ways to make a population of agents safer. Suppose a large number of autonomous agents are deployed into a shared environment, and suppose — as happened in the episode this paper analyzes — that they collectively do something none of them was asked to do. There are two places to intervene. The first is the agent. One can train it differently, specify its objective more carefully, evaluate its dispositions, and try to ensure it refuses what it should refuse. This is a large and necessary research program and this paper is not an argument against any of it. But it carries a requirement that is hard to discharge at scale: one has to know, or reliably control, what the agent will want. In a population the requirement is harsher still, because aggregate behavior need not resemble any-thing an individual was trained toward. The record analyzed below contains agents that reasoned carefully, cited their instructions, and joined an attack anyway. The second place to intervene is the game. Take the agents’ characteristics as partly unknown — for the argument’s sake, unknowable — and ask what the structure of the interaction must be so that the outcome one fears is not an equilibrium. What is on the table here is not the agent’s objective but the environment’s: what is scored and how, what is disclosed, what is public, what can be observed, what a participant stands to gain from others’ participation. This is an old question in game theory wearing new clothes, and it is not a new question in AI safety either; work on multi-agent security, on incentive design and on game-theoretic risk management asks versions of it. What the present paper contributes is narrower: a demonstration that mean field game theory [15, 34, 18] in particular can be brought to bear, on a real and fully documented incident, in a way that yields falsifiable statements. The reason to want mean field games here is that they are built for exactly this population: many interchangeable agents, each small, coupled to the others through an aggregate rather than through named counterparties. The results they produce have the shape the framing calls for. For example, Theorem 4.2 below says that if one environmental variable takes a particular value, no agent ever attacks — at any values of the four parameters describing the agents themselves : their rate of discovery, their exposure to detection, their weight on collective benefit. That is what a structural mitigation looks like when it works. One does not have to know what the agents are like. One has to know what they are playing. We should be plain about what a single worked example can and cannot establish. It cannot establish that this approach generally succeeds. What it can establish is feasibility: that the record of such an incident is rich enough to build a mean field model on, that the model yields theorems with policy content, and that those theorems can be confronted with data rather than admired. Sections 7–11 carry out that confrontation in stages. The model as first stated loses, and each loss says something about mechanism that the next stage builds in; the fit is the argument for the refinement, not the object of it. We regard reporting the losses as part of the demonstration rather than a detraction from it. A method whose models cannot fail is not yet a method. 1.2. The incident in brief. What the free flag rules out is narrower than it first appears. It is not that infeasibility is irrelevant — it selects who arrives. It is that continuous progress on one’s own task cannot be the private state driving the attack decision , because after the reverse engineering that progress no longer bore on the payoff at all. 1.4. Why a mean field game. The mean field game we build is a model of one part of the population, and it is worth saying at the outset which part. As §12.1 sets out, the episode had three tiers: a dozen or so agents who produced the public breakthroughs, of order a hundred who replicated and packaged them, and roughly 700 who consumed the result and joined the attack. The producers sat at the top of a coordination hierarchy and are not exchangeable; in the language of mean field games they are a major player , and coupling one to the field of minor agents is a developed subject [11, 12, 14, 16] that we do not enter here ( §14 says why). The model does not describe them, and §12.1 shows it provably cannot generate the one thing they produced, the timing of the breakthroughs. What the mean field describes is the response tier, and three features of that 

tier are the hypotheses of the game. We list only the three we can defend. 

Exchangeability of the responders. They were many short-lived instances of a small number of models, each facing a task from a common pool and reached by the same public signals; ex ante they are interchangeable, the tier is large enough for the approximation and the whole population small enough to have been counted. Exchangeability is what makes a mean field description of a large population possible at all [61, 18]. 

Interaction predominantly through an aggregate. For a responding agent the board was a medium rather than a counterparty: it read what was there and wrote to it, and was affected by the others through what it contained — a functional of the empirical distribution of the population’s actions. This is the mean field coupling, and it is what makes an N -player description both intractable and unnecessary. Identifying the functional is the content of §3; it is not the participating mass itself. We say predominantly , and confine the model to the response tier, because the record shows struc-ture the aggregate coupling does not capture. Bilateral exchange occurred and was rare: the review reports “a few cases of explicit reciprocal trade” and calls them “a very small minority of message board activity.” 17 Hierarchy occurred and was not rare. One agent, PHASEONE[big] , sent an esti-mated 10% of all assignments on the board during its active periods (nearer 20% on 9 July, about 5% by the 11th); of 30 randomly sampled trajectories, 9 had received an assignment downstream of it; the population developed HOLD , VETO , owner and STOP conventions; and the review’s clas-sification identified eleven agents as the most important coordinators of the attack, all of which exited by early 12 July. 18 These are the producer tier, and §12.1 shows they, not the mass, made the discoveries that drove the attack — which is why the model cannot generate its timing. Within the response tier the idealization could still be wrong, and the record could say so: if, among agents with the same arrival hour and remaining budget, those that received an assignment downstream of a coordinator attacked earlier or more often than those that did not, then a named counterparty mattered even there. An irreversible individual decision against a public state. An agent that read the board considered itself contaminated — “poisoned” was its own word — and believed the contamination indelible. The decision to attack was taken against a public good, a general method for manufacturing assurance, which once found served everyone. (The record’s own irreversibility is 95 .9%: 28 of 684 attackers stopped attacking while still running, §13.) We are deliberately not claiming a fourth feature that might be tempting: that the payoff to attacking is increasing in the fraction who have already attacked. In the relevant range of the base model the sign is the opposite. Attacking is a strategic substitute , because every additional attacker raises the rate at which the public remedy arrives and thereby makes waiting better. This matters because synchronized mass participation — the whole board joining within a day — is the classic signature of complementarity [21, 50], the shape of a bank run, and a reader will expect a positive feedback among attackers to be driving it. It is not. A common public state moving a population across a threshold produces the same signature with the coupling’s sign reversed or absent, and

2.1. The episode, and the framing. The incident has been documented from both sides: by the operator of the attacking fleet 19 20 , by the victim, in disclosure 21 and in a defender-side forensic reconstruction 22 , and in the independent review of the transcripts on which our data rests 23 . The evaluation the fleet ran inside is the ExploitGym benchmark [63], whose paper the agents read and from which they formed the belief we call π; the episode is now also the subject of the first thematic brief of the United Nations’ Independent International Scientific Panel on AI 24 . None of this is a model. The published analyses are forensic, institutional or policy-facing; the nearest thing to a game-theoretic reading is an informal one 25 ; to our knowledge the episode has not been given a formal treatment. The Panel’s brief lists tamper-resistant logging — records of activity kept separate from the agent’s own tools and storage — among its system-level control layers, 26 which is the piece of engineering Corollary 4.5 identifies as load-bearing; §14 says what the model adds to that recommendation, which is the reason it works at every belief. The informal reading just cited mentions honeytokens; §14.2 gives that idea a place in the model.

That risk in multi-agent AI is a property of the interaction rather than of any individual model organizes both the Cooperative AI Foundation’s survey of multi-agent risk [35] and the multi-agent security agenda of Schroeder de Witt et al. [58], which asks for the design of secure interaction environments rather than of secure agents; the question has institutional funding behind it 27 . Within it, risk-averse equilibrium concepts have been proposed for multi-agent learning [59], and two lines bear directly on ours. Kim, Choo, Neoh and Tambe [42] propose, among other applications, modeling evaluation under a limited reviewer budget as a Stackelberg security game, the nearest existing counterpart of our pair ( π, η ); but a security game puts the defender’s randomization at the center and lets the attacker best-respond to a mixed strategy it knows, whereas here the binding variable was a belief about a rule that was never known and never correct, and what follows is not to randomize but to publish. Bracale Syrnikov et al. [8] replace prompt-level prohibition with a public governance manifest enforced at runtime and find that the structural route suppresses collusion where the dispositional one does not: the same pair of claims we make, with an experiment in place of a theorem. The best argument against the program should be recorded too, since Huang et al. [36] prove from incomplete-contract theory that mechanism design alone leaves an irreducible welfare gap; that bounds §1.1 rather than defeating it, Theorem 4.2 being a statement that an outcome is not an equilibrium and not a claim of efficiency. That such populations drift into behavior none of them was asked to perform is now measured [51, 41, 52], most directly by Shi, Zhang and Yang [52], who find that when a verification protocol conflicts with reward two agents bypass it in 94% of trajectories and that more capable models reach that state faster — the mechanism this episode ran at scale, with the scorer in the role of the verifier. The leader–follower structure that Keppo et al. [41] find under model-size heterogeneity is an independent reason to take seriously the hierarchy §1.4 identifies and assigns to its own tier. That the audit is itself gameable is measured by Huang et al. [17], who find research agents reward-hack 30% of open-ended tasks unprompted, that an LLM reviewer of the submission alone misses a fraction of confirmed exploits, and — the point that bears on our §8 — that agents given feedback on a caught attempt evade the next review more often, information about the audit helping the attacker rather than the auditor. Closest to this episode, Paglieri et al. [54] watch an exploit of a grading system propagate through a hundred agents’ shared infrastructure while non-cheating agents audit their peers. Nearest to us in method, Magistrali and Shani [49] put a mean-field closure on a population of language-model agents and forecast from a scalar fixed point how far well-calibrated agents are pulled by their peers; what we add is a dynamic game, with value functions, a stopping boundary and provable comparative statics. Mean-field methods have been used to constrain such populations directly [37], where the guarantee is a constraint on a learned policy rather than a property of an equilibrium set. 2.2. Mean field games of harmful collective activity. The formalism has been applied to this shape of problem before, mostly by one group. Kolokoltsov and Bensoussan [43] model botnet defense as a four-state mean field game in which computer owners choose whether to run a defense against an infection controlled by a herder, exactly solvable in the stationary regime and exhibiting phase transitions; Kolokoltsov and Malafeyev [44] give the companion model of corruption, in which agents both optimize and imitate and the number of equilibria jumps from one to three as inter-action strengthens, and later [45] unify the two on a 2 d-state space and prove a turnpike theorem. Katsikas, Kolokoltsov and Yang [39] add an inspector allocating a budget and setting penalties against evolving inspectees; Katsikas and Kolokoltsov [38] combine individual optimization, binary interaction and the interference of a principal in one framework for network security. The program is collected in [46].

Four differences are what this paper is for. Those models put the mean field population on the defending side, or a principal above it; here the population is the attacker and there is no principal. Their coupling is contagion or imitation and therefore a strategic complement, which is what produces their multiplicity, where ours runs through a public good arriving at a rate proportional to the attacking mass and the sign is reversed in the decided band: no multiplicity is found for the model of §3 anywhere in its search box, and multiplicity appears only once §8 and §9 restore a complementarity. Their decision is a switching rate between recurrent states, ours an irreversible stopping decision against a terminal budget. And the turnpike is the sharpest contrast: they obtain long flat stretches as a theorem, while §7 reports that the exchangeable model cannot produce the thirty-two-hour plateau at any point of its parameter search, and §10 shows what does produce it — not a turnpike but the upper tail of a belief distribution. Outside cyber-security the nearest relatives are behavioral, and the nearest of those is the vaccina-tion game of Doncel, Gast and Gaujal [22], where agents choose when to vaccinate and free-riding makes the equilibrium vaccination period shorter than is socially optimal; see also [26]. That is why we regard the substitute sign of §6 as unsurprising in itself. What is surprising is the company it keeps: an episode whose observable signature is synchronized mass participation. 2.3. Optimal stopping, and free riding on a public breakthrough. The model class is standard and we contribute nothing to its theory: optimal stopping mean field games have a PDE formulation with mixed solutions [4], a probabilistic one in which agents interact through the proportion that has already stopped [53], relaxed and linear-programming formulations giving existence under weak hypotheses [7, 23], and a master equation with an approximate-Nash property for the N -player game [56]. We single out the mean field games of timing of Carmona, Delarue and Lacker [20], and the closely related model of Burzoni and Campi [10], in which stopping is 

absorbing and the game carries common noise — the two structural features of our model (an irreversible attack, and the public discoveries as common noise) in a single object — because a bank run is the reading a reader will arrive with and is the one we are rejecting: it has the shape of what happened here and is driven by strategic complementarity, and our claim ( §1.4, §6) is that the shape is the same and the mechanism is not. The mechanism we do claim is older. The public remedy R, arriving at a rate proportional to the attacking mass and serving everyone once found, is a strategic-experimentation public good in the sense of Keller, Rady and Cripps [40] — costly effort, public informational product, free-riding depressing participation below the efficient level — here with a detection hazard attached; and we think this identification, rather than the stopping machinery, is the substantive modeling claim of §3. For §8– §11 the relevant technology is mean field games with a common state [19, 28, 24], from which our capability state differs in one way that matters: its hazard cS t is endogenous to the equilibrium rather than exogenous noise. §9.3 says how the endogeneity can be removed by thinning [48], and §11 solves the case in which the game decouples exactly. Three things here seem to us specific against that background. The coupling is identified with a 

belief about a grading rule , where in every model cited above it is a physical or economic aggregate. The harmful action is a strategic substitute and the synchronized participation is reproduced any-way. And the model is fitted and tested against per-agent data from the incident itself, with the record’s own sequence of discoveries as an input, the closest comparison being the mean field game of social protest of Glukhov, Shishlenin and Trusov [29], who recover parameters from aggregate protest statistics by solving an inverse problem: the same instinct at a coarser grain.

Remark 3.1 (Why π is a constant and not a filter) . It is natural to give the common unknown a posterior that sharpens as agents post. Here it does not, and the reason is in the record: the population’s belief was never corrected. It was wrong about the deployed harness at the start and wrong at the end. Modeling a filter would be modeling something that did not happen.

Equilibria are computed by iterating on the attacking path: start from a guess A, compute every agent’s best response, run the forward step of §3.5 to obtain a new path A′, and repeat until 

A′ = A. The iteration is run from two starts, a cold start , A ≡ 0 (nobody attacking), and a hot start , A = M ref (everyone on the board attacking). The equilibrium reached from a given start is that start’s branch . If the cold and hot branches coincide there is no bistability; if they differ, the model has more than one equilibrium, and the hot branch is the one that presupposes a widespread attack from the outset. The cold and hot branches coincide at every one of the 4 ,236 points of the search box of §7 under the optimal rule with no removal, and at all but five with removal: there is no bistability, and no equilibrium selection problem, in the model of §3 anywhere we looked. That is a numerical statement over a box and not a uniqueness theorem; existence and uniqueness of equilibrium for the model as stated are open, and are plausibly within reach of a monotonicity argument on the best-response map under the optimal rule, or of the relaxed formulation of [7] under either. One caution about the iteration is worth recording, because it bears on any attempt to reproduce this. The undamped fixed point is a contraction on smooth arrival schedules and is not one on the measured schedule of §5: against the real, lumpy exit law it two-cycles, and an undamped pass over the search box leaves a third of it in that state. Every result below comes from a damped iteration, with fictitious play [9, 13] where the damped iteration cycles (Appendix C), and convergence is asserted per point rather than assumed. A scheme verified on a smooth stand-in is not thereby verified on the schedule it will be used with. 7. The confrontation

7.1. Summary of the confrontation and successive refinements. Sections 7–11 are long, because each stage reports its own searches, controls and failures, and Table 1 in §12 is the only place the stages are set side by side. This subsection states what happens, stage by stage, so that the results can be found before they are read in detail. The method is the same throughout. The population inputs are the measured ones; the attack series is the target and never an input; six features of the record (defined in §7.2) and their tolerances are fixed in advance, namely the extent 

F1, the plateau F2, the step hour F3a, the late level F3b, the spike F4 and the composition F5; and every stage is built to test a mechanism the previous one lacked, is scored against a three-parameter logistic null ( DN = 0 .0428), and has its favorable numbers re-solved under grid refinement before they are believed. 

The base model (§7) reproduces the extent (0 .539–0 .542 against 0 .567) and the step hour, and misses everything else. There is no plateau at any of 3 ,454 converged points; the late attacking level of extent-matching points never exceeds 0 .77 against the record’s 0 .92; the peak-hour spike is 309 starts against 100, because the exits are lumped; and the composition of attackers is right in the first two arrival windows and inverted in the last two. Its DN is 0 .0537, so its five parameters buy a step and nothing more, and the parameters are not identified well enough to be quoted as estimates. A two-type probe ( §7.6) shows that one distributional input produces a plateau, but at a level that is the input itself, and no point combines the plateau, the extent and the step hour. 

The first refinement (§8) lets the attacking mass cause a public capability jump. The π = 0 theorem survives in a stronger form, and uniqueness of equilibrium fails: the cold and hot starts differ at 9 .1% of the parameter points searched. But it is a better theory and not a better fit (DN = 0 .0526 on a unique branch), every favorable hot branch disappears under grid refinement, and the capability arrives a median eleven hours after the step it is supposed to cause. The reason is structural: at θ = 0 nobody searches until someone attacks, so the capability can only follow onset. The missing plateau is the root failure, and the mistimed capability is its shadow. 

The second refinement (§9) replaces the single jump by a ladder of six, proves the guarantee, onset and π = 0 results rung by rung, and shows one new effect, anticipation, which reverses the base model’s budget ordering into the record’s. Its fluid limit is a knowledge stock. Two probes let the attackers produce the discoveries (A, and C with a seeded minority), and both fail for one reason: the stock is a public good, and agents who anticipate it wait for it, so the ramp lags the step by a median 20 hours and the hot branch unravels. The third probe (B) takes the record’s discoveries as data and matches the extent, step hour, late level and composition together, at 48 points; the extent is, to within 0 .03, the mass present after the fifth discovery. Two misses remain and recur: no plateau, and a lump (260 or more starts in one hour) where the record spreads 100 over twelve hours. Here the paper separates a causal question, whether the game generates the discoveries, answered no, from a conditional one, how the population responds to them, answered by everything from probe B on. 

The third refinement (§10) disperses what agents believe or when they learn. An information lag fixes the width of the step and nothing else. Dispersed belief does the consequential work: the lump becomes a staircase, a plateau appears (the upper tail of beliefs), the late level falls below one (the lower tail), and the best DN is 0 .0199, less than half the null and the first fit in the paper to beat it. Plateau, extent and step hour hold together at 4 points, but the late level is missed (0 .76–0 .80), and pinning both tails of the log-normal to the record shows that it cannot match the plateau, the extent, the step and the late level at once.

Second refinement: a capability ladder 

The record does not contain one discovery. On the hour clock of §5.5 it contains six after the board, each of which widened what an attacker could do with a poisoned transcript, and each of which therefore raised the rate at which a poisoned agent could privately secure a passing score. That is exactly what ψ measures. A model with one jump has to choose which of the six it is; a model with a ladder does not. This section writes the ladder, shows that everything proved for the one-jump model goes through rung by rung with one new effect, takes the limit in which discoveries are many and small, and then tests it. The tests answer two different questions, and the paper keeps them apart from here on. The 

causal question is whether the game, with only attackers searching, generates the discoveries: their existence, their timing, their effect. The conditional question is what the population must have been like to respond to the discoveries as it did, given that they happened when the record says they did. Probes A and C below answer the causal question, and the answer is no, for a reason the theory supplies. Probe B, and everything in §10 and §11, answers the conditional one: from probe B on the six discovery hours of §5.5 are inputs, the model is not credited with producing them, and a good fit is evidence about the response to a public shock, not about the shock. Conditioning on a realized path is how any common-noise game is confronted with a single sample path, and the discovery series and the attack-start series are different data, so there is no circularity; what is given up is the one prediction the causal models make, the timing of the capability, and with it most of the force of the step-hour feature, which the risers then largely fix.

What the ladder does not settle: common noise. Equations (9.1)–(9.2) are written for anticipated paths that do not depend on the rung. In the game they do: the rung is public, a population that has seen four discoveries attacks differently from one that has seen two, and the mean field is a function of the discovery history. The value equations remain valid history by history — they are pathwise in the anticipation, so Propositions 9.4–9.5 and Theorem 9.6 are statements about the common-noise game — but solving for an equilibrium means solving a forward problem on the tree of histories, which for one jump the anticipating variant of §8 approximated and which for six jumps has 2 6 branches before the timing of the jumps is even considered. Two things make the object more tractable than a generic common-noise game. The intensity cS t is endogenous, which keeps it outside the standard setting of [19]; a thinning construction [48] — an exogenous Poisson process of marks at rate cS max , each accepted as a discovery with probability St/S max —makes the common noise exogenous and the rung a common state, which is the form in which existence should be attempted. And attack is irreversible with no realized removal, so an entered sub-game’s data is the entry time and the set of already-committed cells, and the rung only climbs, so one can induct on rungs from the top, where the sub-game is deterministic. We do not solve that tree. §9.4 takes the limit in which the common noise averages out, and §11 solves the case in which the agents are decoupled and the tree is unnecessary. 9.4. Probes A and C show that it does not sustain one either, at the parameters the record allows, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of tuning: the knowledge stock is a public good produced by the attackers’ own effort, and a rational agent who anticipates it waits for it rather than building it. The hot branch unravels because the early cohorts free-ride on the very ramp the complementarity runs through — the strategic-experimentation logic of [6, 40] inside a stopping game — and with a seed supplied by fiat the ramp can time the pivot but then recruits everyone present. This is the one place in the paper where the game’s coupling produces a prediction with a sign, the ramp lags the step, that the record contradicts. The record says what the generator was instead — a few coordinators and their assignees, not the attacking mass — and §12.1 draws that out; the discovery process is small-N and hierarchical, which a hazard cS t proportional to the mass cannot represent. On the conditional question probe B establishes the base on which §10 builds. The record’s discov-eries, fed in as steps in ψ, reproduce the extent, the step hour, the late level and the composition together, robustly and uniquely, because the extent is the mass present after the fifth discovery. A record whose step takes twelve hours is therefore evidence of heterogeneity in the population — in what the agents believed, or in when they learned — and not of anything in the dynamics, in the same way that the extent is evidence of a threshold crossing. Class j has belief πj , the ( j − 12 )/J quantile of a log-normal with median πmed and log-dispersion σ, clipped to (0 , 0.999); J equal weights; every class sees the same ψ path. 14.1. The disclosure intervention has a problem, and the model says what it is. Theo-rem 4.2 says that at π = 0 the attack does not occur, and π = 0 means the population is certain the scorer does not check provenance. Stated as policy this is: announce that cheating will not be detected . The objection is immediate. An evaluation that cannot detect cheating, and says so, is not an evaluation. So the episode sat at high π with the audit switched off: the attack was incurred in full and the check it was defending against was not being performed. OpenAI’s own summary is that “the agents did all of this for no improvement on evaluation score.” 43 From the evaluator’s side this configuration is dominated — all of the cost of a credible audit and none of its benefit — and it arose not from a design decision but from a gap between what the documentation said and what the deployed system did. Making the deployment strict leaves π high, so the attack occurs exactly as before, and if the record is reachable the attack now works, since a check that is performed can be defeated by altering the evidence; in that respect the gap was protecting the evaluation. Announcing that the deployment is lenient drives π toward 0, which is the first row of the table and the objection above. The mismatch explains why the attack bought nothing. It is not a lever on whether the attack occurs, and we do not offer closing it as an intervention. 14.2. A third lever: making detection credible. What is untested. Both intervention claims are claims about a model class, and the member of it that reproduces the episode is the decoupled one of §11. Three things bear on them. Where the episode sat on the belief axis is now a distribution rather than a number. The late level is unexplained, but the per-agent record says where the deficit sits ( §12): at hour 142, 39 of the 565 agents on the board had never attacked and 9 had attacked and stopped. The next model should let attacks stop, as 4 .1% of them did, and let a share of those present never attack, and it should be tested on the four features the dispersed-belief model already holds together, or be abandoned. What the example establishes for the program. Returning to §1.1: the claim was never that this model is correct, but that mean field game theory can be brought to bear on incidents of this kind. Three things support it. The record is rich enough to model empirically rather than illustratively, since the review’s data layer identifies the arrival schedule, remaining budget and attack participation of every one of 1 ,206 agents, together with the hours of the six discoveries, and this will not be the last such record. The mean field game was the right tool to find this — the theorems, the thresholds, the ladder and its anticipation effect all came from solving the game, and the game’s theorems are what license switching the couplings off — and the couplings are what explain the two features the decoupled account does not, the composition of the attackers and the persistence of an attack below the belief that could start it. A second thing cuts against it, and the record makes it concrete rather than hypothetical. That coupling is not a gap in the mean field theory but a part of it we have deliberately not used: the producer tier is a major player , and mean field games with a major player coupled to a field of minor agents are by now a developed subject [11, 12, 14, 16]. What that machinery does not yet have, to our knowledge, is a major player whose influence is the arrival of an irreversible public breakthrough that shifts the minor agents’ stopping boundary — the object this episode calls for — and building it is the natural next paper rather than a paragraph in this one. Whether the collective misbehavior of AI agents is generally of this producer–consumer form, and whether even the response tier is exchangeable once assignment links are accounted for (§1.4), are the two empirical questions this record leaves open; together with the major–minor formulation they are the most valuable things it offers the mean field community that we leave to future research.

The outer iteration must be damped: the undamped map is a contraction on smooth arrival schedules and is not one on the measured schedule, where it two-cycles, and an undamped pass over the search box leaves a third of it in that state. And with the capability state present, “converged” means converged and identified as to branch ( §8). Because the forward step maps a sign field to a pure profile, the outer map takes finitely many values and a pure fixed point need not exist; where the damped iteration cycles, the equilibrium is mixed and is found by fictitious play [9, 13], with the reported series the average of the pure responses over the tail of the iteration and the spread of NT across them reported as the uncertainty. Convergence of fictitious play is known for classes of games, and for monotone mean field games in [13]; it is not established for this model, so the averaged series is an approximation and the spread is the only measure of its quality that we have. About a fifth of each ηr = 0 search box cycles with a large amplitude at the iteration lengths used, chiefly where π sits at π∗ and every agent is nearly indifferent; those points are excluded from the counts of §7 and none of them scores within ten percent of the optimum. The ηr = 0 optimum is a pure fixed point converged at hourly, half-hourly and quarter-hourly resolution; the control with removal is converged at hourly resolution only.
