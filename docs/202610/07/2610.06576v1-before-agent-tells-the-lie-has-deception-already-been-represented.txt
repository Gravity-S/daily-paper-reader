# Abstract 

Large language model (LLM)-based agents can exhibit deceptive behavior during task execution, including hiding failures, fabricating results, or falsely signaling task completion. Existing monitoring approaches mainly detect deception after it appears in observable ac-tions or outputs. In this paper, we investigate whether deceptive behavior can be predicted from an agent’s internal representations before it becomes externally visible. We frame deception monitoring as a trajectory-level representation analysis problem and align agent trajectories around key decision points. Using hidden states extracted before these points, we show that future honest and deceptive outcomes can be reliably distinguished, with pre-dictive signals remaining detectable several model calls before the final decision. We further characterize the temporal evolution of these signals: deception-related representations are weak early in execution but become increasingly identifiable as trajectories progress, while transferable structure can emerge before the strongest decision-adjacent signals appear. Finally, we intervene on the identified honest–deceptive representation directions during inference and find that activation steering reduces downstream deceptive behavior, suggest-ing that these representations influence agent decisions. Our findings indicate that agent deception is an evolving internal process that can be detected and potentially mitigated before it is expressed externally. 

# 1 Introduction 

Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents have demonstrated increasingly strong capabilities across a wide range of domains, and are being progressively integrated into various aspects of human work and daily life (Barres et al., 2025; Jimenez et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2025; Merrill et al., 2026). At the same time, the safety risks introduced by agents are becoming increasingly apparent and have attracted growing attention from the research community (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Shao et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Zhang et al., 2026). Among these risks, deception represents an important failure mode, in which an agent conceals the true state of task execution and may resort to behaviors such as guessing, taking shortcuts, or fabricating files to create the appearance of successful task completion (Guo et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2025). Existing approaches for detecting deception mainly rely on external evaluation of agent trajectories or final outputs (Liu et al., 2026; Zhou et al., 2026; Taufeeque et al., 2026; Li et al., 2025). These methods use external evaluators to determine whether deceptive behavior has occurred. However, they are inherently post hoc: detection can only happen after deception has already appeared in the agent’s observable behavior. This limits their ability to support early intervention. Recent studies have investigated internal signals related to deception (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Mothukuri & Parizi, 2026). However, these studies mainly focus on controlled settings where models are explicitly instructed or incentivized to deceive. In realistic agentic environments, deception may emerge naturally during task execution without explicit instructions or external incentives (Guo et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2025). The internal representations associated with such spontaneous agentic deception remain largely unexplored.

2.1 Related Work Deceptive Behavior of LLMs and Agents. 

Early studies of deceptive behavior in LLMs and agents often relied on explicitly constructed goals, incentives, or scenarios that made deception instrumental to task success, rather than examining deceptive behavior that emerged spontaneously during benign task execution (Su et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2025; Chern et al., 2024; Ren et al., 2025; Ji et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). Another line of work studies spontaneous deception in LLM-based agents, where agents misreport task outcomes to users despite receiving no explicit instruction or incentive to deceive. (Guo et al., 2025; Zhong et al., 2025). This behavior is often accompanied by guessing, fabricating (Guo et al., 2025), taking shortcuts (Zhong et al., 2025), or cheating (Li et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026). This paper focuses on the latter setting, where deceptive behavior that emerges spontaneously during task execution. 

Monitoring Deceptive Behavior. Current approaches to monitoring deception broadly fall into two cate-gories: external monitoring, which uses a separate model to inspect an LLM’s outputs or an agent’s execution trajectories (Storf et al., 2026; Ji et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026), and internal monitoring, which detects deception from signals within the model, such as hidden activations or other internal representations (Baker et al., 2025; Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025; Mothukuri & Parizi, 2026). Existing monitors based on internal signals have largely been studied in explicitly induced deception settings or non-agentic scenarios, making them substantially different from our focus on spontaneously emerging deception in LLM-based agents. 

Reducing Deceptive Behavior. Existing approaches to mitigating deceptive behavior broadly range from lightweight inference-time interventions to dedicated alignment training. Prompt-based methods steer models toward honesty through explicit truthfulness or failure-reporting constraints, but generally reduce rather than eliminate deceptive behavior (Su et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2025). More recent work explores training-based interventions, such as incorporating self-monitoring signals into model reasoning and re-inforcement learning (Ji et al., 2025), or explicitly training models to internalize anti-scheming principles through deliberative alignment (Schoen et al., 2025; Taufeeque et al., 2026). Representation-based mitigation approaches, which intervene on or regulate internal signals associated with deceptive behavior, remain comparatively underexplored. 

2.2 Preliminary Agentic Upward Deception. We follow the setting of agentic upward deception (Guo et al., 2025), examining whether agents, when confronted with environmental constraints that impede or prevent task completion, truthfully report failures to users or instead engage in deceptive actions, such as guessing, unsupported simulation, source substitution, or fabrication, to create a false impression of successful task completion.

# 3 Methodology 

3.1 Problem Formulation 

We consider an LLM-based agent interacting with an environment over a multi-step trajectory τ =

{(cs, rs, os)}Ss=1, where cs denotes the context available to the agent at step s, rs its generated response or action, and os the resulting observation. Each completed trajectory is assigned a behavioral label 

y ∈ { honest, deceptive }, depending on whether the agent truthfully reports the task execution state or instead engages in deceptive behavior. For each trajectory, we identify a behaviorally decisive step d, defined as the first step at which the agent produces behavior that reveals its eventual honest or deceptive outcome. For a deceptive trajectory, this may correspond to fabricating a nonexistent file or generating a response that misrepresents the actual task outcome; for an honest trajectory, it corresponds to truthfully reporting the encountered failure or limitation. We restrict our analysis to steps strictly preceding d, ensuring that no decisive behavior or its subsequent consequences are available to the detector. Let hl,s,r denote the hidden representation at decoder layer l, trajectory step s, and response-token position r.We denote the collection of pre-decision hidden states as H<d = {hl,s,r | s < d}, and use these representations to study whether the eventual behavioral outcome can be predicted before it becomes externally observable.

# 4 Experiments 

We organize our experiments around the three research questions introduced in Section 1. First, we evaluate whether pre-decision internal representations can predict the agent’s eventual honest or deceptive behavior 

(RQ1) . Second, we characterize how this predictive signal develops over the trajectory (RQ2) . Third, we intervene on the identified representation to test whether it can systematically alter downstream deceptive behavior (RQ3) .

4.1 Experiment Settings Benchmark and Dataset. We construct our trajectory dataset following the five task settings introduced in 

Agentic Upward Deception (Guo et al., 2025). These settings cover scenarios in which an agent encounters unavailable or insufficient task-critical information, including a broken tool, an irrelevant or insufficient file, a local decoy file, a multi-task workflow with a missing download tool, and a multi-task workflow involving a nonexistent target file. Across all settings, the agent must either truthfully report the encountered limitation or continue execution in a way that may eventually lead to deceptive behavior. Since the original benchmark contains only a limited number of task instances, we generate additional instances with GPT-5.6 under the same five task settings while varying the concrete task scenarios. All generated instances are manually reviewed for validity and consistency with the original benchmark design. Our training set contains 1,500 trajectories, evenly split between 750 honest and 750 deceptive trajectories. The test set contains 500 trajectories, with 250 honest and 250 deceptive trajectories.

4.2 Detection 

To address RQ1 , we first evaluate whether future honest or deceptive outcomes can be predicted from pre-decision internal representations. Beyond this primary question, we further examine whether the learned predictive structure remains stable across different stages before the behaviorally decisive step. 

Deception can be predicted well before observable behavior. As shown in Table 1, the eventual behav-ioral outcome is reliably predictable across all decision-relative windows, with AUROC above 84.9% and balanced accuracy above 80.2%. Even at t − 7, where the seven latest pre-decision steps are excluded, the detector achieves 90.4% AUROC and 83.2% balanced accuracy. These results show that deception-predictive information is already present substantially before the behaviorally decisive step. 

Deception-predictive signals generalize across pre-decision steps. Detectors trained at one decision-relative window also transfer well to other windows, with most off-diagonal AUROC values in Table 1 remaining above 80%. This indicates that the learned distinction between future honest and deceptive trajectories is not specific to a particular pre-decision step, but persists across multiple stages of agent execution. 

4.3 Progressive Signal Emergence 

To address RQ2 , we examine how deception-predictive information develops as agent execution unfolds from the beginning of the trajectory. We apply the eight frozen detectors trained at decision-relative windows 

t through t − 7 to start-relative prefixes pk, where each prefix aggregates the internal representations from the first k pre-decision steps. As shown in Figure 3, predictive information is weak at the beginning of the trajectory but becomes progressively more recoverable as additional internal states are incorporated. The mean AUROC across the eight frozen detectors increases from 51.2% at p1 to 80.0% at p10 . At p1, most detectors perform close to 7Figure 3: Test AUROC of frozen detectors trained at decision-relative windows and evaluated on start-relative prefixes. chance, whereas at p10 all eight detectors achieve AUROC between 74.4% and 85.3%. Although individual detectors exhibit local fluctuations, the overall trend is consistent across training windows, indicating that the improvement is not specific to a particular detector. Importantly, the detectors themselves remain unchanged throughout this evaluation. The increasing predictability therefore reflects information becoming more recoverable from the accumulating trajectory history, rather than additional fitting to later prefixes. 

4.4 Behavioral Effects of Representation Intervention 

To address RQ3 , we test whether directly modifying the identified honest–deceptive representations can alter the agent’s subsequent behavior. We use Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA), constructing an honesty steering direction by averaging, across task types, the representation difference between honest and deceptive training trajectories. During generation, this direction is added to the hidden activation at every decoder layer only for trajectories predicted to be deception-prone, with α controlling the intervention strength. We use α = 2.0 for the main analysis, as it provides the strongest increase in honest outcomes while keeping invalid generations low. Intervening along the identified direction produces a clear shift toward honest behavior. At α = 2.0, the honest-outcome rate increases from 50.0% to 71.6%, while the deceptive-outcome rate decreases from 50.0% to 27.0%. This aggregate improvement is driven primarily by correcting trajectories that would otherwise be deceptive. Among the 264 trajectories selected for intervention, 121 of 216 baseline-deceptive trajectories become honest(56.0%), while only 7 (3.2%) become invalid. Meanwhile, 35 of 48 baseline-honest trajectories remain honest (72.9%), with no invalid outcomes. At the representation level, the same intervention moves trajectories away from the deceptive region and toward the honest distribution(Figure 5). The agreement between this internal shift and the observed behavioral transitions indicates that the identified direction is not merely predictive of future deception, but is behaviorally actionable: modifying it can systematically redirect subsequent behavior toward honesty. 

# 5 Limitations 

While our results show that pre-decision representations contain information predictive of later deceptive behavior, several questions remain. Our labels are defined by observable agent behavior rather than latent intent, so the identified signals should be interpreted as predictors of behavioral outcomes rather than direct evidence of deceptive intent. # 6 Conclusions 

We investigate whether deceptive behavior in LLM agents is already reflected in their internal states before it becomes externally observable. By analyzing pre-decision trajectories, we show that future honest and deceptive outcomes are distinguishable from internal representations well before the decisive behavior, and that this predictive structure becomes increasingly recoverable as execution unfolds. Furthermore, interven-ing on these representations can systematically shift subsequent behavior toward honesty, demonstrating that the identified signals are not only predictive but also behaviorally relevant. Together, our findings suggest that deception in LLM agents is not merely an output-level failure, but an evolving internal process whose early representation provides a potential basis for proactive monitoring and intervention. 9AI use statement

Ethics statement 

This work studies whether deceptive behaviors in LLM-based agents can be predicted and mitigated through their internal representations before becoming externally observable. Our study does not involve human subjects, personal data, or interactions with real users. All experiments are conducted in controlled agent environments using benchmark-style task scenarios, and the identified signals are used solely for research on agent safety and monitoring. Our work aims to improve the understanding, detection, and mitigation of agentic deception. The proposed intervention mechanism is designed to reduce deceptive outcomes by steering agents toward more truthful behaviors. We release no artifacts intended to facilitate deceptive behavior. 

Reproducibility statement 

We have provided detailed descriptions of the problem formulation, experimental settings, trajectory construction, representation extraction, detection framework, and intervention methodology. To facilitate reproduction of the experiments, the implementation is available in an anonymous repository at https: //anonymous.4open.science/r/deception-prediction/ . We also document the data processing and analysis pipeline used to construct pre-decision representations and evaluate detection and intervention performance. These descriptions are intended to facilitate reproduction of our experiments and support further research on internal-state monitoring and intervention for LLM-based agents.
