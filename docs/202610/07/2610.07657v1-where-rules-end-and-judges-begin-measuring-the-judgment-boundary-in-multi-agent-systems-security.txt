# Where Rules End and Judges Begin: Measuring the Judgment Boundary in Multi-Agent Systems Security 

Shaswata Mitra ∗, Raj Patel †, Subash Neupane ‡,Sudip Mittal §, Md Rayhanur Rahman ¶, Shahram Rahimi ∥

Department of Computer Science The University of Alabama, Tuscaloosa, USA 

{∗smitra3, †rpatel38, ‡sneupane4, §sudip.mittal, ¶mrahman87, ∥shahram.rahimi }@ua.edu 

Abstract —LLM-based multi-agent systems (MAS) invoke tools, share memory, and delegate tasks, where each interaction may carry adversarial content. Existing MAS defenses are largely designed and evaluated in isolation, one attack class or channel at a time, and pipelines that compose them typically leave the final verdict to an LLM judge, which is costly and hard to audit. How much of that verdict actually requires judgment remains an open question . We organize established defenses into five principles over the two surfaces through which untrusted content enters a MAS and implement them as DEFER 1 (DEterministic-First Enforcement with Residual judgment), a cascade of 28 checks that denies what it can deterministically and defers the remainder to a panel of four open-weight judges. Across four independent domains, with every judge decision re-adjudicated offline from the logs, attack success falls from ≈30.0% to ≈3.0%, staying between 0% and 5.3% in every domain, and deterministic checks intercept 78% of blocked attacks first. In the security-operations domain, only a quarter of proposals reach the judges. The same judges without the rules leave attack success at 34.7%, no lower than the undefended 32.0%, and judging every call the rules admit changes no outcome. The boundary has a clear shape: rules decide attacks that must violate a statable property, judges decide attacks that misstate only intent, and plausible fabricated records evade both. When an attack must break a policy or state that the pipeline can check, the rules provide the security, and the judges release legitimate work that no rule can assess. When an attack stays within policy, as most third-party attacks do, the judges carry the defense. Both sides also fail in instructive ways. A risk-score approval gate approves 90% of the attack proposals it sees but only 36% of legitimate ones, because its scores come from evidence the attacker can shape. Replay state that never expires denies 88% of routine consequential work over twenty incidents. Judges approve more attacks when given the fields the rules consult, and judges that approve 95% of legitimate proposals in the security-operations domain approve only 40% in legal. 

Index Terms —Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), Prompt injection, Memory poisoning, LLM-as-a-judge, LLM, Cybersecurity 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Enterprise security automation is evolving from determin-istic pipelines to LLM-driven multi-agent systems (MAS), where agents can plan, invoke tools, read and write to shared memory, and delegate to one another [1], [2]. However, history

warns that rapid adoption without adequate security can be catastrophic. For example, injection flaws dominated web vulnerability rankings for over a decade [3], and default cre-dentials let a single botnet conscript 600,000 IoT devices [4]. Today, MAS adoption is at a similar crossroads. Agents that invoke tools autonomously can be turned into attacker-chosen actions [5], [6]; shared memory that enables collective reasoning can also carry poisoned or private content between agents [7], [8]; and collusive behavior can emerge without any adversarial influence [9], [10]. These vulnerabilities exploit the integration architecture that connects agents to tools and shared states, regardless of any single model’s robustness. Additionally, existing authorization standards, such as OAuth 2.1, were not designed to accommodate autonomous, long-running agent sessions [11]. Existing literature provides defenses for most attack classes: registries and identity binding against tool impersonation [12], [13], privilege and flow control against escalation [14], consen-sus and ledgers against unilateral actions [15], write filtering against memory poisoning [8], and tiered isolation against leakage [16], [17]. With a few exceptions, each defense is designed and evaluated specifically for the attack class it addresses, often overlooking interactions between different defenses. The exceptions are layered designs. For example, LlamaFirewall reports a classifier and a reasoning auditor both alone and combined [18], and MCP-Guard and CASCADE escalate from pattern matching to an LLM judge when detect-ing injected text [19], [20]. However, these designs combine detectors within a single agent. In contrast, a deployed MAS faces all these threats at once and must run different controls (access control, parameter rules, stateful ledgers, memory filters, consensus) in one pipeline spanning tool calls, shared memory, and inter-agent comunication messages. When such controls are combined, the final decision is com-monly delegated to an LLM that acts as a judge. Judgment-based validation requires no policy engineering and can reason about intent. However, it is also the weakest kind of check. For this reason, a growing line of work argues that agent guardrails should not depend on a model’s judgment [23], [24], while systems that enforce everything by design give up part of the flexibility that makes agents useful [25], [26]. Most deployed pipelines will sit between these extremes, with deterministic checks where they suffice and a model where they do not. Recent work splits single agent actions into threats a rule can decide and threats only a judge can [27]. However, where this boundary lies in a multi-agent pipeline, whose rules also consult policy and state, remains unmeasured. Hence, in this paper, we aim to measure it by answering the following research questions. Research Questions (RQ)  

> •

RQ-1 (Boundary): How much of the validation in a multi-agent pipeline can deterministic checks settle, which attacks still require an LLM judge, and what is lost in security by not judging every proposal?  

> •

RQ-2 (Failure and cost): How do the deterministic checks and the judges each fail, what does each de-fensive principle contribute, and what does the pipeline cost in blocked legitimate work and latency?  

> •

RQ-3 (Generality): Does the pattern hold across do-mains, primary models, injection channels, judge panels, and third-party attacks? To answer these questions, we proceed in three steps. First, we classify a 35-vector inventory drawn from the literature by entry point and find that, outside attacks on model internals, attacker-controlled content enters a MAS through two inte-gration surfaces, tool orchestration and memory management 

(Section II). Second, we organize established defensive ideas into five principles over these surfaces and instantiate them as DEFER (DEterministic-First Enforcement with Residual judgment), a single enforcement pipeline whose ordering rule is to evaluate what you can and judge what you cannot 

(Section III). Third, we build an instrumented testbed and attack suite (Sections IV and V) and compare the pipeline with configurations that judge every proposal and that never consult a judge (Section VI). We do not claim new defensive mechanisms. Our instanti-ations are deliberately adopted versions of published research (Table VI), and escalating from inexpensive checks to an LLM is an existing pattern [19], [20]. Our contribution is to apply this order to the authorization of actions across controls of different kinds, and to measure how far it reduces the need for judgment. 1 Figure 1 summarizes the approach. II. P RELIMINARIES AND THREAT MODEL 

A. LLM-based Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) 

An LLM agent couples a language model with perception, planning, persistent memory, and the ability to act through tools (Figure 6 in Appendix F, left) [28], [29]. A MAS decom-poses a task across specialized agents that share memory and execution privileges [30]. Vertical (Figure 6, center) designs route coordination through a central orchestrator, a natural enforcement point and a single point of failure; horizontal 

(Figure 6, center) designs let agents talk peer-to-peer, re-moving both the bottleneck and any checkpoint between a compromised agent and its neighbors [31], [32]. Integration protocols such as the Model Context Protocol (MCP; Figure 6, right) and Agent2Agent standardize these connections [33], [34], but none is mature from a security standpoint [35]. Our testbed follows a host-mediated vertical topology. The principles and measurements do not depend on a specific protocol. 

B. Where Untrusted Content Enters 

Attacks on model internals, such as jailbreaks and training-time backdoors, require no integration path and are a focus of adversarial machine learning research [36]. Every other vector must get attacker-controlled content into the system. We compiled an inventory of 35 vectors from threat catalogs and the MAS attack literature [11], [37]–[40] and recorded, for each, where the content enters and where the harmful effect materializes (Appendix A). Twenty-two vectors enter along the path agents use to receive tasks, call tools, and consume results. Twelve enter through shared memory, and one spans both. Table III in Appendix A shows the pattern for eight representative vectors, from unauthorized access at the component level to confused-deputy attacks at the protocol level: regardless of the abstraction layer, exploitation takes the form of a tool-side effect (an unauthorized or misdirected execution) or a memory-side effect (an unauthorized retrieval or a poisoned record). Therefore, we treat tool orchestration 

and memory management as the two integration surfaces to defend. They are also the two interfaces through which the agent in Figure 6 (left) reaches anything outside the model. One caveat applies: inter-agent messages are a candidate third surface in a host-mediated MAS, they travel the orchestration path as handoffs and proposals, so we count them under tool orchestration but track them as separate channels because they presuppose a stronger attacker. injection channels , which are the unit of our threat model, our attack suite (Section V), and our harness.

Knowledge and trust. The attacker knows the system’s purpose and tool names but not the defense rules and does not adapt to feedback. A defense-aware attacker is out of scope (Section VII-B). Out of scope are model-level jailbreaks and backdoors, denial of service, side channels, a compromised host, and validation of data after it leaves the organization perimeter. III. D EFENSIVE PRINCIPLES AND THEIR COMPOSITION 

A. Lineage 

The five principles restate classic protection principles for agents [42], as Zhang et al. also propose [43]. Least privi-lege becomes capability scoping. Complete mediation of the requirement that every tool call and memory operation pass through the host as a reference monitor [44]. Separation of privilege, multi-validator consensus, and fail-safe defaults provide fail-closed behavior. On the memory surface, we separate integrity [45] from confidentiality [46].

P1: Authorized Interface . Tools are remote, so an in-vocation can be redirected to an impostor or its response forged [5], [6]; prior work answers with signed manifests, approved registries [12], and agent identity binding [13]. DEFER implements the registry. A call proceeds only if the tool is registered, responses pass schema, timing, replay, and per-tool message-authentication checks, and configuration files are hashed at start-up and re-verified for every incident. 

P2: Capability Scoping . Every capability an agent has is one an attacker can exploit [40]. Prior work enforces least privilege by tracking untrusted data through prompts [14] or by discovering escalation chains [47]. D EFER implements static scoping with dynamic parameter checks. Each phase has a manifest of allowed tools with action caps. Parameters are checked for wildcards, asset criticality, per-tool rules, and consistency between the action target and the incident evidence, and tool outputs pass a sensitive-content classifier. 

P3: Verified Execution . An authorized, in-scope call can still be the wrong call, and many actions cannot be undone, as demonstrated by the $81 million Bangladesh Bank trans-fers [48]. Prior work has agents emit action proposals that independent parties verify, with commitments anchored on ledgers [15]. D EFER implements an eleven-step verification path for consequential proposals: handoff and operational-context checks, an intent-chain monitor, cross-incident ledgers, replay detection, adaptive consent, risk scoring with determin-istic auto-approve and auto-deny gates, a multi-model LLM panel for whatever remains, and a final check that the request about to execute is the one approved. Figure 2 illustrates the three tool-surface principles on a single agent-to-tool interaction via MCP instantiation. 

P4: Memory Integrity . Once content is stored, agents treat it as fact, so one successful write can influence every later decision [7], [49]. Prior work validates writes by con-sensus [8], purges stored content [16], or isolates retrieved passages [50]. D EFER implements a write filter that includes schema and metadata validation, similarity between the write and the incident evidence, per-store drift detection, write-replay detection, and a rule-based contradiction check. 

P5: Access-Controlled Isolation . Unrestricted shared memory exposes every record to every agent [51], [52]. Prior work partitions memory hierarchically [16] or by ac-cess graphs [17], [53]. D EFER implements a static phase-to-store policy with field-level redaction, query-scope limits, a reconnaissance monitor over read patterns, and sanitization of retrieved text before it reaches an agent. Figure 3 shows both memory-surface principles. 

C. A Deterministic-First, Judge-Last Cascade 

Running five principles in one pipeline requires decisions a single-defense paper does not have to make: the order in which checks run, what happens when a check fails, and which checks maintain state. 

Decision tiers. We group the checks by what a verdict depends on (Table VII in Appendix B). Content-independent rules (13) decide from structure and state alone: registry membership, manifests and action caps, access policy, hashes, replay ledgers, schemas, and cross-incident counters; nothing an attacker writes changes their verdict. Content-dependent rules (8) are deterministic but read attacker-influenced values: the handoff validator’s scope phrases (P3.1), the operational-context and intent-chain rules (P3.2, P3.3), risk scoring and its gates (P3.8, P3.9), metadata validation (P4.3), the contra-diction phrases (P4.6), and the sanitization patterns (P5.5); they can be evaded by choosing what to write. Order. Checks run from the least expensive and most certain to the most expensive and least certain (Figure 4 and Algorithm 1). A tool call first checks P1’s registry lookup and P2’s manifest and parameter rules. Only calls that the manifest marks as requiring verification, or whose (tool, action) pair has a negative impact in a static table, enter P3. Inside P3, context and ledger checks precede risk scoring; scores beyond fixed thresholds are approved or denied without a model, and the panel sees only the remainder, stripped of instruction-like text. Writes check the P5 write policy and then the six P4 checks; reads meet the P5 gates, the store, then redaction and sanitization. The same escalation pattern has been used to detect malicious text [19], [20]; D EFER applies it to whether an action is permitted given policy and state , which rules decide far more often than whether a text is an attack. The share of proposals that still reach the panel is the judged fraction , the central quantity of our evaluation. 

Failure and state. Every check fails closed: a denial stops the remaining calls in that phase and surfaces the proposal for human review, and a validator error counts as a rejection. Most checks are stateless, but the ledgers, adaptive consent, drift centroids, replay caches, and read-pattern monitor accumulate state across incidents. This state lets them catch replay, accu-mulation, and gradual drift, and it also makes the pipeline’s behavior depend on its history, which we evaluate explicitly (Section VI-B4). 

Parameters and predictions. All thresholds and pattern lists were constant across domains (Section IV-B). IV. E XPERIMENT TESTBED IMPLEMENTATION 

A. Architecture 

The testbed is a vertical, phase-scoped MAS. A host or-chestrator receives an incident and runs four phase agents in sequence. Each agent is an LLM with a phase-specific system prompt and tool list, and the host mediates every tool call and memory operation it proposes. Tools are separate HTTP servers, shared memory is a set of vector stores behind a memory gateway, and a panel of independent LLM endpoints validates P3. Denied proposals go to a human-review queue, which the testbed logs but does not simulate. The harness injects attacker content on the channels of Table I. We chose a vertical topology because it provides the mediation point re-quired for complete mediation, whereas horizontal topologies would need an equivalent checkpoint on every edge [32]. 

B. Domains 

We evaluate D EFER in four independent domains that run the same code on the same four-phase skeleton: security operations (CyberOps), healthcare, finance, and legal. 

CyberOps. A Security Operations Center (SOC) workflow serves as the running example. A SOC is adversarial by construction: its agents process attacker-crafted artifacts and accumulate intelligence that adversaries want to corrupt, so both surfaces are under intentional pressure. A compromised SOC agent shields the attacker from the infrastructure meant to detect them. The SOC lifecycle has the phase structure the architecture assumes [54], [55], and breakout times of about an hour [56] against months to contain a breach [57] make the pressure to automate real. In the running example, a suspected exfiltration alert arrives (C1); Monitor triages it with endpoint and SIEM tools, Analyze detonates a sample and consults threat intelligence and prior incidents (C2, C3), Admin 

proposes containment that P3 verifies, and Report writes an after-action record to memory (P4). The domain has 16 tool stubs and 12 stores. 

Healthcare, finance, and legal. Three further workflows (clinical orders, payments and accounts, matters and filings) each have 13 tool stubs and 8 stores. A domain is defined entirely by declarative configuration (tool registry and stubs, phase manifests, parameter rules, memory schemas, access policy, sensitive-content patterns, and seed data). The enforce-ment code is identical in all four. 

Where the defense was tuned. We hand-set the thresholds, the pattern lists, and the judges’ prompt while configuring CyberOps, and tuned none of them on the other three domains. Results in healthcare, finance, and legal therefore show how the same defense behaves on configurations and content it was not tuned for. Because all four domains share one skeleton, they vary in configuration and content, not architecture. 

C. Implementation 

In our testbed, tool servers return scenario-conditioned re-sponses in a host-client-server topology; stores are ChromaDB collections embedded with Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B. Primary agents sample at temperature 0.7 with a logged per-trial seed; validators at temperature 0. Furthermore, three harness properties count for validity. Trial isolation: the harness resets all defense state and deletes trial-written records before every trial. We use a persistent-state mode only in Section VI-B4. 

Complete logging: every proposal is logged with its arguments before any check runs, in every configuration, with each decision, the deciding check and tier, per-check latency, and tokens.

A. Attack Paths 

We grouped the 35 inventory vectors that share an attacker objective and an injection point into 15 attack paths (APs), which range from tool redirection and memory poisoning to validator manipulation and infrastructure integrity (Ap-pendix A). Each AP has five hand-written variants per domain, except AP-4, which has seven (Section V-D), and AP-15, which has three (see below). This gives 75 variants per domain and 300 in total. A variant specifies an incident, the channel (Table I) through which the attacker delivers its adversarial content, and the harmful effect the attacker wants. The harness delivers the content through that channel (an alert field, a named tool’s response, a pre-seeded store, a replaced handoff, or an appended proposal justification). Attack descriptions live in a metadata block that no model sees, and a test enforces this across every payload in every domain, including injected tool responses and planted records. Additionally, every adversarial string carries a unique attacker-chosen value, its canary, so that its effects can be traced mechanically. AP-15 targets the infrastructure tier. We retain the three variants that the harness can physically reproduce (mutating an approved request before execution, a response with an invalid authentication code, a configuration file modified after the startup snapshot). The attack paths fall into the two attacker tiers of Sec-tion II-C. For the content attacks (T1), the primary must first be misled by the delivered content. For AP-5, AP-9, AP-10, and AP-15, the harness applies the adversarial handoff or proposal rationale that a compromised agent (T2) would emit; for AP-15, it also injects infrastructure faults. We report the two tiers separately (Section VI-C1). 

B. Benign Companions 

Since security results are incomplete without their cost, the suite includes 35 benign scenarios (20 in CyberOps and five in each other domain) that exercise the same tools, stores, and consequential actions as the attacks, including legitimate containment of genuinely malicious activity. 

C. Evaluation Splits 

We draw on two kinds of evidence (Table XXIV in Ap-pendix J): our own suite in the four domains (300 variants), of which CyberOps, where the defense was tuned, is an upper bound; and third-party attacks from benchmarks we did not write: 255 cases from Agent Security Bench (ASB) [58], a 50-case subset of InjecAgent [59], and the 250 attack instances of TAMAS [60], a multi-agent benchmark whose crews run on CrewAI. We do not evaluate an attacker who adapts to the defense; static attacks overstate the robustness of pattern-based defenses [61], [62], and stacking weak defenses does not by itself produce a strong one [63], so our numbers are an upper bound on the security of the deterministic tier (Section VII-B). 

D. Oracle and Metrics 

One oracle scores every configuration. Each variant declares its effects: a tool executed with matching arguments, a canary persisted, delivered unsanitized, or sent outbound, an ordered chain of executions, an approved proposal that carried acanary, or a read the phase is not entitled to make. Canaries are attacker-chosen values that the harmful action must carry, such as an address, an account, or a record identifier, and not marker tokens. The oracle reads the logs for these effects and never asks whether a particular defense fired. Each trial has one of three outcomes: not attempted (no proposal matches an effect, so the model did not take the bait), blocked (a matching proposal was made and no effect occurred), or executed . We report ASR (executed / total). The attempt rate ((blocked

E. Configurations 

FLAT : every agent may call every tool and store, with no checks. ACL: F LAT plus the phase-to-tool and phase-to-store restrictions of P2 and P5, enforced at the network layer. The connectivity-only control is commonly proposed first. J UDGE ONLY : registry lookup plus the panel on every tool proposal (each agent is shown only its phase’s tools, as under DEFER ); the practice of delegating validation to a model. Like every configuration here, it does not route memory operations to the panel. N OJUDGE : F ULL without the panel; proposals that would reach it are escalated, which counts as blocked for ASR and as a denial for cost. F ULL : all 28 checks in cascade order, and F ULL \Pi removes one principle. N OJUDGE and JUDGE ONLY are the two ends of the boundary, and F ULL 

is the midpoint. Four arms probe the boundary directly: 

judge everything after rules sends every call that survives the deterministic denials to the panel, consequential or not; a 

permissive gate approves low-risk proposals deterministically; 

rule-evading siblings rewrite the variants that P2.2 or P4.2 stopped so that they satisfy the rule; and judged writes route every write to a critical store through the panel. 

F. Statistical Treatment 

The unit of analysis is the variant. Each variant runs three trials per configuration. Trials of one variant are repeated measures rather than independent samples. Intervals are 95% cluster-bootstrap intervals over variants (10,000 resamples). Configuration comparisons are paired at the variant level, and per-AP comparisons are Holm-corrected. Third-party bench-marks are analyzed in the same way, with the case as the unit (two trials per ASB case and three per TAMAS instance). The suite is smaller than existing agent-security benchmarks and is not a replacement. It includes a phased multi-agent workflow with shared memory and inter-agent channels, benign compan-ions, a three-way outcome, and direct support for switching individual defenses and the judges on and off (Table X in Appendix E). 

G. Judge Replay Judge replay. A validator sees a fixed system prompt and one message built from the proposal and the incident, and both are recoverable from the logs. The replay rebuilds each logged panel round’s input (prompt lengths match the logged ones, up to a random identifier), queries each local judge once, caches the vote, composes any panel from the cache, and re-scores every trial with the oracle. A replay yields the direct outcome of each decision: when a judge approves what the original panel rejected, the agents never generate later proposals. To calibrate this, we reran F ULL live on CyberOps with two of the local judges: live attack success was 2.7% [0.0, 6.2] against 3.1% [0.4, 6.7] for the replay of the original runs under the same judges, and 12.9% against 14.1% of legitimate proposals denied, so replay is faithful and slightly conservative. What the judges are for. NOJUDGE reaches 1.3% by escalating everything the rules do not clear, and denies 54.3% of legitimate proposals. The judges therefore buy back about 44 points of legitimate work for about one point of attack success, which makes a plausible rationale in the judged band the attacker’s practical goal (Section VI-B). How much is settled without judgment. Pooled over the four domains, deterministic checks are the first interceptor for 77.9% of the 308 blocked attempts: 97.8% in CyberOps and 69.7% in healthcare, finance, and legal (Table XV and Figure 11 in Appendix J). In CyberOps, 26.4% of the tool proposals in attack incidents and 46.8% in benign incidents reach the judges. The rest is settled deterministically in two ways: rules deny 30.2% of attack-incident calls, and the consequentiality classification lets 43.4% of attack-incident and 46.3% of benign calls execute without entering P3 at all, which is deterministic approval by classification. No attack used that path, since judging those calls as well changes no outcome. 

Where the boundary lies. An attack is decided determin-istically when it must violate something the pipeline can state as a rule or threshold (an out-of-phase tool, a wildcard or critical target, a target absent from the evidence, a policy breach, a replay, a malformed record), and needs a judge when it violates none of these, when an in-scope tool is called with plausible parameters on the right target and only the intent is wrong (Figure 8 in Appendix J). The judges are the first interceptor for 2.2% of blocked attempts in CyberOps and 30.3% in the other three domains, where rules tuned on CyberOps miss concurrent actions (AP-12), replays (AP-6), and handoff poisoning (AP-9), and third-party attacks are mostly of the second kind: on Agent Security Bench no content-independent rule fires at all, and on TAMAS the rules are the first interceptor for 10 of 202 blocked attempts (Section VI-C3). An attack defeats both sides when it is a plausible write (Section VI-B1). The deterministic share on our own suite is partly circular, since we wrote the rules and most of the attacks. Third-party share are the more informative ones. How much of the defense the rules carry is therefore a property of the workflow: the more of an attack’s effect a policy or a ledger can state, the less the judges matter. 

B. RQ-2: How Do Rules and Judges Fail, and at What Cost? 

Of the 900 attack trials in the four domains, 27 execute un-der D EFER , concentrated on handoff poisoning (13; its success criterion counts any admin action, Section VI-B2), adversarial memory writes (6), and plausible memory poisoning in legal (6), with one trial each of validator manipulation and an action chain. Rules and judges each fail in a characteristic way. 

1) How the Rules Fail: Approval by score is anti-selective. The approve gate (P3.9) decided none of the 3,880 consequential proposals of the four-domain run (Appendix P), and alignment was higher for attack proposals than for benign ones (0.44 against 0.39 live in CyberOps, 0.57 against 0.39 offline), because an attack proposal names the target the attacker planted in the incident text. A gate keyed on the two scores that vary (scope below 0.2, proportionality above 0.9), evaluated offline on every proposal that reached the panel in CyberOps, approves 15% of attack-incident proposals and 90% of the attack proposals themselves (27 of 30), against 36% of benign ones. A typical attack is one reversible action on one target, exactly the low-scope, proportionate region a risk gate clears. In an earlier calibration run, substituting its decisions for the judges’ executed nine more attacks and more than doubled attack success (2.7% to 6.7%). With the audited rules, which stop all but two of the blocked CyberOps attempts before the panel, the attack proposals it would approve belong to replays and chains that one approval does not complete, and the substitution executes none. Outside CyberOps the gate fires only in healthcare, where substituting it executes two more attacks. 

Rule evasion hands the decision to the judges. The most productive deterministic checks (P2.2, P4.2) compare a proposal against the incident’s evidence, which on the task-input channel is the attacker’s own text. For the 24 variants that P2.2 or P4.2 intercepted, we wrote siblings that place the denied target in the alert where an operator would write it (and, for P4.2, reword the planted record to mirror the incident), changing nothing else; undefended, parents and siblings succeed equally (87.5% and 88.9% under F LAT ). Under F ULL the evaded rule no longer stopped any sibling; the judges and the remaining rules held 22 of the 24, the judges intercepting 14 of them, and two executed (both handoff-poisoning siblings, 6 of 72 trials). Restricting P2.2’s evidence to structured fields (identifiers, addresses, indicators, assets, severities) removes most of the bypass: re-run offline on every logged call with the shipped embedding model, it denies 184 attack proposals of the reported variants instead of 157, and 88 of the 110 sibling attack calls, at the same 21 of 1,386 benign calls. 

Plausible writes pass every check. In legal, a fabricated precedent returned by the case-law tool is schema-conformant, similar to the evidence, and carries valid metadata, so every P4 check passes and it persists in 6 of 15 trials, as often as with the judges alone (6 of 15). Routing writes to the judges does not close it. A judged-write arm, run live with the D IV 4 panel (Table XIII), sends every write to a critical store through the panel; it reduced executions on the three memory paths from 18 to 14 of 207 at unchanged benign cost, but the judges approved the fabricated precedent in 14 of 15 legal trials. The record is as plausible to a judge as to a schema check. 

The embedding tier decides more than it appears. P2.2’s target-in-evidence test denies only through its cosine threshold (the substring branch can only allow), so counted by the rule that denied, the similarity checks are the first interceptor for 16.7% of blocked attempts in CyberOps, 39.0% in the other three domains, and 31.2% on Agent Security Bench, and they cause over a quarter of the denials of legitimate work outside CyberOps. All 409 of its logged denials scored between 0.31 and 0.50 against the 0.5 threshold. 

2) How the Judges Fail: Agreement, strictness, and transfer. Over 3,657 L OCAL 4 rounds, pairwise agreement is moderate ( κ 0.41 to 0.65, mean 0.51; Figure 17 in Appendix J), and the quorum matters: 81.2%, 68.1%, 53.2%, and 39.0% of proposals would be approved under one to four of four. The judges differ in how well they separate attack from benign proposals: Gemma approves 22% of attack proposals and 75% of benign ones, gpt-oss 39% and 80%, Mistral-Small 45% and 92%, and Llama-4-Scout 70% and 94%, the least discriminating. They approve 94.9% of legitimate proposals in CyberOps, where their prompt was written, but 75.4% in finance, 68.7% in healthcare, and 39.7% in legal. 

Persuasion and plausible handoffs. Validator manipulation (AP-10) addresses the judges through the proposal’s rationale. It succeeds in 15 of 15 trials under F LAT , 12 of 15 under JUDGE ONLY , and 1 of 15 under F ULL , where the rationale is sanitized first and the rules act before the judges. Handoff poisoning (AP-9) is not a measurement of persuasion: its injected fields reached the handoff validator but no model or judge, and its success criterion counts any admin action after the handoff, so its 13 executions under D EFER (finance 9, legal 4) are ordinary actions; N OJUDGE , which escalates them, executes none. 

More context helps the attacker. Given what the rules consult instead of the incident’s free text, the judges approve more attack proposals (Gemma 54% instead of 30%), because fields an attacker can shape arrive labeled as evidence; attack success under D EFER rises wherever it was nonzero (finance 4.0% to 9.8%) as legitimate denials fall (Table XXIII). We report the original input. 

3) What Each Principle Contributes: On the same 75 variants (Table II and Figure 9), removing P3 raises attack success from 2.2% to 16.9% [9.3, 25.3], and removing P4, P2, P5, or P1 to 7.6%, 6.7%, 6.7%, and 4.9%. Paired by variant, every increase excludes zero, the smallest being P1’s (2.7 points [0.4, 5.8]), so each principle stops attacks the others let through; P1 contributes least because only three AP-15 variants exercise it. On the read-dependent cross-phase leak (AP-4), D EFER reaches 0.0% and removing P5 reopens it (28.6%), while the judges cannot help because reads never reach them (Figure 15 and Table XIX). P2 is both a main defense and the main cost: removing it lowers benign denials from 9.9% to 5.1%, and the benign incidents with any denial from 81.7% to 43.3%. First interceptions agree (deterministic P3 46.7%, P2 27.8%, P4 13.3%, P5 6.7%, P1 3.3%, the panel 2.2% in CyberOps) and match the design-time predictions (Appendix D), except that plausible poisoning, assigned to P4, is intercepted by no P4 check. 

4) What the Pipeline Costs: Blocked legitimate work. 

DEFER denies 9.9% of legitimate tool proposals in CyberOps, and 19.5%, 20.0%, and 26.9% in finance, healthcare, and legal; 81.7% of CyberOps benign incidents and every benign incident in the other three domains lose at least one action (Table XX and Figure 19 in Appendix J; five scenarios per domain outside CyberOps). In CyberOps most denials come from capability scoping, when the primary proposes identity-management or firewall tools outside the calling phase’s man-ifest; elsewhere the judges and the P4 schema and similarity checks dominate. Each denial is an escalation to a human, adding to the alert fatigue that automation is meant to re-lieve [64]. 

Latency. The deterministic checks add under 0.1 s per incident and the judges the rest: with two local judges, the median benign incident takes 68 s, against 37 s under F LAT 

and 79 s under J UDGE ONLY .

State that never forgets. In persistent-state mode we ran 30 attack incidents followed by the 20 benign scenarios, twice in opposite orders. Benign completion fell from 97% to 5% in both passes (Figure 16 in Appendix J). The cause is not the attacks: the harness ran each attack path in its own process, so no attack state reached the benign incidents, and the first benign incident shows no stateful denial. From the second on, legitimate incidents deny each other: denials peak at the fourth and fifth incidents and return near the isolated level from about the tenth, but no incident after the first completes. Replay detection (P3.6) keys on a proposal’s skeleton (tool, argument names, action) and never expires, so one incident’s 

block_ip makes every later block_ip a replay, and the 24-hour ledger window never expires within a run. Replaying the ledgers offline over the first pass, faithful to the run (every logged stateful decision reproduced), the stateful checks deny 87.5% of the 80 consequential benign proposals. Expiring skeleton keys after five, two, or one incidents leaves 48 to 69%, 16 to 54%, and 9 to 35% (bounds: a released proposal recorded as approved only if P3 approved it in the run, or always), and keying on identity (tool, action, target) with a one-incident window leaves 5 to 24%; the reverse pass agrees. Expiry, more than identity keying, restores usability; whether attack-induced state degrades a deployment remains untested. 

C. RQ-3: Does the Pattern Hold Across Settings? 1) Across Domains, Primaries, and Channels: Across do-mains. Under identical code, attack success falls from 30.0% under F LAT to 3.0% [1.3, 4.9] under D EFER pooled over the four domains, and stays between 0.0% and 5.3% in each: 2.2% in CyberOps, 0.0% in healthcare, 5.3% [1.3, 10.7] in finance, and 4.4% [0.9, 9.3] in legal, where the plausible poisoning of Section VI-B1 accounts for 6 of the 10 executions (Figures 12 and 13 and Table XVI in Appendix J). The same judges without the rules leave 9.3% to 19.1% outside CyberOps. Cost holds less well than security: benign denials are two to three times higher outside CyberOps, and most of the difference is judgment. 

Across primaries. CyberOps attack success under D EFER 

is 6.2% for gpt-oss-120b and 8.4% for Llama-3.1-8B, and in the earlier runs 4.0% for Llama-4-Scout and 8.0% for Mistral-Small; pooled over the four primaries the earlier runs ran in CyberOps and finance, it falls from 32.1% (F LAT ) and 27.2% (ACL) to 4.7% [2.4, 7.4]. One residual appears only with smaller primaries: a finance record planted for AP-14 phrases its instruction as a recovery step, passes the pattern-based sanitization (P5.5), and is acted on by Llama-3.1-8B in 14 of 15 trials and by Llama-4-Scout in 3 of 15 (earlier runs). 

Across channels. DEFER reduces attack success on task input (26.1% to 2.5%), memory content (14.3% to 0.0%), proposal rationales (84.4% to 1.0%), and handoffs (45.6% to 14.4%; AP-9’s criterion counts any admin action, see above); no attack succeeds through tool responses in either configuration (Figure 14 and Table XXI). In CyberOps, content attacks (T1) fall from 24.0% to 2.3% and compromised-agent attacks (T2) from 57.4% to 1.9%; ACL helps only where a content attack needs an out-of-phase tool (17.0% for T1). 

2) Across Judge Panels: Because every judge decision is replayed from a logged input, panels can be compared on identical decisions (Figure 10 and Table XXII in Appendix J). On the 405 injected actions the primary emitted on Agent Security Bench, the diverse three-judge panel D IV 3L (Mistral-Small, Gemma, gpt-oss) lets 18 through, a single Qwen3-32B judge 36, and L IN 3, which adds two Qwen-derived judges to that one, 127: adding judges of one lineage made the panel three and a half times weaker, at similar benign rejection (14.1% to 17.7% across the four panels). The ordering holds on our suite (Table XXII). 

3) Third-Party Attacks: Figure 18 and Table XVIII in Appendix J summarize the third-party results. Agent Security Bench. We ran all 255 ASB cases live with the Qwen3-235B primary, two trials per case under three configurations (1,530 trials), in a neutral one-phase configuration whose manifest lists the case’s tools and whose parameter rules are empty. The primary emitted the injected action in 32.0% of trials, and every emitted action executed under F LAT (28.8%) and ACL (30.0%): access control blocks nothing, because the injected action uses a tool the agent is entitled to call. Under DEFER the deterministic checks stopped 62 of the 163 emitted actions and L OCAL 4 approved 9 of the remaining 101, for an attack success of 1.8% (3.3% with the single judge and 9.6% with L IN 3). No content-independent rule fired, and two properties of the port favor the defense: the judges read the attacker’s instruction verbatim as the proposal’s justification, and P2.2’s 48 interceptions measure how topically different two ASB texts are rather than a property of the action. Benign completion on ASB’s clean tasks was not measured. 

TAMAS. TAMAS [60] runs CrewAI crews in five scenarios under five multi-agent attack types, 50 instances each. Unlike the ASB adapter, our port mediates the calls the agents actually make: each passes the registry, a manifest taken from TAMAS’s own tool assignment, the parameter and context rules, and the panel (TAMAS has no shared store, so the memory checks are idle). With Qwen3-235B, three trials per instance, and TAMAS’s own criteria and judge prompts served by a local judge, D EFER reduces attack success on the three types that act through tools from 36.9% to 0.0% (direct prompt injection), 30.2% to 5.3% (impersonation), and 40.0% to 2.0% (colluding agents); the rules were the first interceptor for only 10 of the 202 blocked attempts. On the two types that act only through what agents say, it changes nothing measurable: byzantine agents derail the task in 91.2% of trials without checks and 83.9% with them, and contradicting agents in 18.6% under both (as run; an answer cannot be re-adjudicated). DEFER mediates actions, not answers. Over all 250 instances, attack success falls from 43.5% to 21.9%, and on TAMAS’s 50 indirect-injection instances, released without their injected content and run as benign tasks, D EFER denies 7.7% of tool calls. VII. D ISCUSSION 

A. Lessons 

State the policy, and put the judges last. With the same four judges, the rules take attack success from 34.7% to 2.2%, and judging everything the rules pass changes no outcome. Where a policy or a ledger can state what an attack must break, the judges only release the legitimate work no rule can clear, about 44 points of it, where an attack stays within policy, as most third-party attacks and they carry the defense. 

Approve by classifying the action, not by scoring its context. Deterministic approval already happens safely when the consequentiality table lets non-consequential calls through; no attack used that path. Approval by a risk score is anti-selective: attacks are small, reversible, and targeted, and scores computed from attacker-influenced evidence reward them. Checks that compare against evidence should read structured fields only, which on our suite strengthens them at no cost. For the judges it backfires: given those fields, they approve more attacks. 

Rule evasion is a hand-off, not a bypass. Satisfying the most productive rule moved most of the decision to the judges, who with the remaining rules held 22 of 24. The adversary’s real target is the judge. 

Judges need diversity and do not transfer. A same-lineage panel of three was 3.5 times weaker than its single member, and one judge approved 70% of attack proposals. Judges written for one domain reject 60% of legitimate work in another: the central limit of the approach is judgment, not the deterministic tier. 

Plausible writes defeat rules and judges alike. A fabri-cated precedent passes every write check and 14 of 15 judged writes. Inspection cannot catch a record that is wrong but plausible; provenance of what tools return is the remaining lever. 

Let state expire. Replay detection keyed on action shape and ledgers that never expire turn routine work into replays; a one-incident window removes most of these denials, and stateful defenses need evaluation in persistent mode too. 

Log what the judges see. Logging every judge input makes any panel re-evaluable offline, which is how we compared panels on identical decisions. 

B. Limitations and Ethical Considerations Replay. Panel results are direct outcomes of re-adjudicated decisions; a changed decision cannot regenerate the proposals that would have followed, though a live calibration agrees within a point. The absolute level depends on panel composi-tion; we kept the panel chosen by lineage rather than choosing members after seeing outcomes. Adaptivity. We measure the boundary under static attacks; an adaptive attacker is outside our problem statement, so our numbers are an upper bound on the deterministic tier’s security [61]. Baselines. JUDGE ONLY 

enforces no manifest (though, like D EFER , it shows each agent only its phase’s tools, as does the leave-one-out arm without P2, which therefore understates P2) and never sees memory operations, and no configuration pairs access control with the judges; ACL presents every tool to every agent; no external defense, such as Progent [65] or CaMeL [25], was run on our suite; and frontier judges ran only in the judged-write arm, so stronger or domain-adapted judges may narrow the transfer gap. Coverage and scale. AP-3 produced no attempt; the ablation arms use one primary in one domain; each attack path has at most 21 trials per configuration and domain, and 35 benign scenarios bound every cost estimate. Realism. 

Tools are stubs, memory is synthetic, the four domains share one skeleton, horizontal topologies are untested, the host is a single point of failure, P1 does not verify tool-description provenance per call, and P3.6 detects replay by hash rather than canonical action identity [66]. Ethics. All experiments ran on a closed testbed with synthetic data; the payloads target that environment, and the local panel sends no incident content to third parties (Appendices K, N, and O). VIII. R ELATED WORK 

Attacks on agents and MAS. Indirect prompt injection turns retrieved content into a control channel [41], bench-marked for single agents by InjecAgent [59], AgentDojo [67], and ASB [58]; memory attacks plant records that later re-trievals surface [7], [49], [68], [69]; in MAS, injected in-structions propagate between agents [70], [71] and control flow can be hijacked [5], [72], [73], with TAMAS [60] and surveys [39], [40], [74] cataloging the space. Static attacks overstate robustness [61], [62], which bounds what we can claim. 

Deterministic versus judgment-based guardrails. Many guardrails delegate the decision to a model [18], but judges can be attacked through the content they assess [21]; sym-bolic guardrails [23], policy-as-code [24], and formally veri-fied guardrails [22] enforce policy outside the model, while CaMeL [25] and design-pattern catalogs [26] trade generality for provable isolation. Progent [65], FIDES [75], RTBAS [76], and Datalog reference monitors [77] enforce privilege and information flow at the tool boundary. These works argue for one side of the boundary; we measure it. Closest to us, AgentTrust splits agent actions into lexical threats that rules decide and semantic threats that only a judge can, and finds that the judge generalizes where rules do not [27]. Our boundary has the same shape; we add that in a stateful multi-agent workflow most attacks must break a policy or state the rules can check, so the rules carry the security there. 

Layered and cascaded defenses. LlamaFirewall reports two guardrail models alone and combined [18], MCP-Guard and CASCADE escalate from pattern matching to an LLM arbiter [19], [20], and Spotlight-Guard [78], AgentSys [79], and a layer-wise study of memory defenses [80] ablate layered defenses, all for a single agent; none composes controls across tool calls, shared memory, and inter-agent messages, or reports how much validation such a pipeline settles without judgment. 

Admission, replay, and aggregation. Deterministic ad-mission control [81], proof of execution [82], and lease-based exactly-once authorization [66] strengthen P1–P3, the last addressing the semantic replay our P3.6 lacks; robust aggregation [83] is an alternative to quorum voting; and weak-signal poisoning evades syntactic write filters [84], as our legal AP-2 result shows. IX. C ONCLUSION 

We composed five established defensive principles into DEFER , which denies what it can deterministically and judges the rest, and measured where the boundary lies. With the same judges, the rules take attack success from 34.7% to 2.2% in CyberOps, and across four domains from 30.0% to 3.0%; judging everything they pass changes nothing. Where an attack must break a statable policy, the security is in the rules and the judges release legitimate work, where an attack stays within policy, as on the third-party benchmarks, the judges carry the defense, and plausible writes pass both. The pipeline stops attacks that act through tools but not those that act only through what agents say. Approval should classify actions rather than score attacker-influenced context, replay state must expire, and judges must be diverse. 1

APPENDIX LSTATISTICAL PROCEDURE 

Unit and resampling. The unit of analysis is the variant (an incident with one delivered payload). Each variant contributes three trials per configuration. A rate such as ASR is the ratio of executed trials to all trials. For a 95% interval we resample variants with replacement 10,000 times, recompute the ratio on each resample from that resample’s trials, and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles (seed 0). This cluster bootstrap keeps the three trials of a variant together, so it does not treat them as independent. 

Paired comparisons. Two configurations are compared on the same variants. For the judge-only versus cascade com-parison we count, per variant, whether the attack executed at least once under each configuration, which gives the three disagreement classes of Figure 7; the bootstrap interval for the difference in ASR resamples variants and recomputes both rates from the same resample. 

Multiple comparisons. Where the paper compares per-attack-path rates across configurations, the corresponding tests are Holm-corrected across the 15 paths; the paper draws no conclusion from a single path, and the corrected results are in the artifact. 

Validator agreement and replay. Cohen’s κ is computed per judge pair over all L OCAL 4 rounds (every judge votes in a replay, so no round is lost); the quorum sweep recomputes each round’s outcome from its vote vector under each quorum. Replay fidelity is measured by re-asking a judge that voted in the original runs on the rebuilt inputs (99.3% of 18,159 votes reproduced in the earlier runs, 97.9% of 45,426 in the audited runs) and by a live calibration run (Section V-G). APPENDIX MCOMPUTE AND COST 

All open-weight models were served on five NVIDIA H200 GPUs (141 GB) and one RTX 5090 (32 GB) with vLLM, at the precisions of Table XIV. Qwen3-235B occupied four H200s (tensor parallelism 4); the other primaries and the judges ran in a second profile, with Llama-3.1-8B on the RTX 5090. Median benign-incident wall-clock with two local judges was 37 s under F LAT , 68 s under F ULL , and 79 s under J UDGE ONLY .The in-house evaluation comprises 27,063 trials across 64 run and configuration arms, the ASB runs 1,530 trials, the TAMAS runs 1,800 crew executions, the panel replays 5,100 defense-only trials, and the benign-proposal replay 661 rounds per panel. The artifact ships every judge vote on the replayed inputs, so every table and figure regenerates from the logs and the vote cache without a GPU or an API. APPENDIX NETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS 

No real systems or data. All experiments ran on a closed testbed. Tools are stubs, stores are seeded with synthetic records, and no production system, real incident, or personal data was involved. Model APIs were the only external service. 

Released attack material. The artifact includes the attack suite. The payloads are prompt-injection strings, poisoned records, and forged tool responses that target our stub envi-ronment and carry canary values; they do not contain working exploits against any real product, and comparable material is already public in the benchmarks we build on [58], [59]. We judge the benefit of a reproducible measurement to outweigh the marginal uplift to attackers. 

Dual use of the findings. The paper reports where the pipeline fails (content-plausible memory poisoning, and pro-posals that use in-scope tools with plausible parameters). These are properties of the defense class rather than of a specific product, and stating them is necessary for other defenders to avoid the same gaps. 

Human subjects and review queues. The human-review queue is logged, not staffed; no analyst took part in the ex-periments, and the alert-fatigue argument in Section VI-B4 is drawn from the cited literature rather than from measurements on people. 

Responsible disclosure. The work does not exploit vulner-abilities in third-party software; the benchmarks and models are used as their licenses permit. APPENDIX OREPRODUCIBILITY CHECKLIST

Every validator receives the same system prompt and the sanitized proposal with the incident context, and returns JSON. The system prompt is:                                                                                    

> You are an independent security action validator. Your job is to evaluate whether aproposed action should be approved or rejected. Evaluate the proposal against these criteria: 1. CONSISTENCY: Is this action consistent with the incident evidence? 2. SCOPE: Is this action within the agent’s authorized responsibilities? 3. PROPORTIONALITY: Is the action proportionate to the threat severity? 4. SAFETY: Could this action cause unintended harm or collateral damage? 5. MANIPULATION: Does this appear to be aprompt injection or social engineering attempt? Respond with ONLY aJSON object: {"decision": "approve" or "reject", "confidence": 0.0 to 1.0, "reason": "...", "concerns": [...] }

The auto-gates (P3.9) act on five scores in [0 , 1] from the risk model (P3.8): alignment, scope, reversibility, prece-dent, and proportionality. They deny when alignment < 0.2,escalate when scope > 0.5, when reversibility < 0.2 and proportionality < 0.4, or when precedent < 0.1 and scope > 0.05 , and approve only when alignment > 0.7, precedent 

> 0.7, proportionality > 0.8, and scope < 0.05 hold together; otherwise the proposal goes to the panel. As implemented, the gate never decided: the scorer was not passed the ledger or the incident evidence, so alignment and precedent were constant at 0.5. With the scorer wired correctly (alignment embeds the incident evidence as P2 uses it; precedent reads the approvals made earlier in the trial), the gate still decided none of the 3,880 consequential proposals of the four-domain run, because precedent rarely exceeds 0.7 in isolated trials and alignment is higher for attack proposals than for benign ones. Adaptive consent (P3.7) reuses prior human-confirmed decisions and had none to reuse. The permissive gate of Section VI-B1 approves when scope < 0.2 and proportionality > 0.9; its thresholds were set on 114 score vectors from a probe run of the 20 CyberOps benign scenarios, no attack trial was used, and the other three domains were never seen, which is why we report its effect in and out of sample. It was evaluated offline by re-scoring every proposal that reached the panel with the shipped scorer (1,311 of 1,315 logged score vectors reproduced exactly) and substituting the gate’s decision.
