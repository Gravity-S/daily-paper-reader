## ABSTRACT 

Errors in LLM agent executions and their visible consequences can be separated by many steps, making decisive-error localization a matter of understanding both step content and step dependencies. We introduce DeFA, a dependency-guided framework for agent failure attribution. DeFA first combines protocol relations and semantic dependencies into an event dependency graph spanning the trajec-tory. It then identifies events that may violate task requirements and traces their sources and subsequent effects to construct a failure propagation graph. Finally, DeFA uses step evidence and the steps’ roles in failure propagation to identify the decisive error, responsible agent, and error category. To support long trajectories, DeFA partitions executions into segments and combines the current segment’s detailed content with summaries of the other segments, giving local diagnosis ac-cess to global execution context. Across Who&When and the Who&When Pro text subset, DeFA achieves the highest responsible-agent and exact step accu-racy with all evaluated backbones, and the highest failure-mode accuracy among taxonomy-aligned methods on Pro. Further experiments on image and video tra-jectories demonstrate its applicability to multimodal failure attribution. Ablations support the contributions of segmentation, the event dependency graph, and the failure propagation graph. Using DeFA’s diagnostic feedback for skill evolution in Trace2Skill improves downstream task accuracy by 6–15 percentage points over the native pipeline, showing that the diagnoses can also support agent improve-ment on subsequent tasks. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

When an LLM agent fails a task, the final outcome rarely reveals which earlier decision was respon-sible. Agents interleave reasoning, tool use, and communication (Yao et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024), producing trajectories in which an error and its visible consequences may be separated by many steps. Failure attribution identifies the responsible agent and the decisive error step (Zhang et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2026), providing specific evidence for debugging and subsequent improvement. The difficulty lies in tracing how errors arise and propagate through execution to identify the deci-sive error behind downstream symptoms. Suppose an agent misreads a retrieved value, another agent uses it in a calculation, and the final answer repeats the incorrect result. The final answer reveals the failure, but the earlier interpretation may be the decisive error. Empirical studies likewise examine failure onset and propagation in agent trajectories (Zhao et al., 2026). Attribution must therefore ex-amine what information a step received, how it used that information, and what happened afterward. This becomes harder when relevant evidence is distributed across a long trace (Liu et al., 2024). Existing attribution frameworks use global judgments, sequential checks, or binary search (Zhang et al., 2025), as well as constraint validation, multi-agent investigation, and hierarchical diagno-sis (Barke et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2026). Our focus is to connect suspicious events with the execution that made them consequential. A local deviation must be examined together with its upstream sources and the later actions that adopted, amplified, or corrected its effects. Recon-structing these relations brings distributed evidence together to explain how the execution failed. 1arXiv:2610.01256v1 [cs.AI] 1 Oct 2026 We introduce DeFA , a framework for dependency-guided failure attribution from recorded trajec-tories. DeFA first combines protocol relations and semantic dependencies into an event dependency graph spanning the trajectory. Building on this structure, it identifies events that may violate task re-quirements and traces their sources and subsequent effects to construct a failure propagation graph. It then combines trajectory content and propagation relations to identify the decisive error step, re-sponsible agent, and error category. For long trajectories, DeFA uses segmented analysis, combining the current segment’s detailed content with summaries of the other segments so that local diagnosis can use both concrete step evidence and global execution context. Against seven baselines, DeFA leads in responsible-agent and exact step accuracy on Who&When with all three backbones, improving exact step by 7.6–9.8 percentage points. On 300 Pro text tra-jectories, it also achieves the highest responsible-agent and exact step accuracy with all three back-bones, as well as the highest failure-mode accuracy among taxonomy-aligned methods. On 100 multimodal trajectories, it improves exact step accuracy by 6.0–7.0 points across both backbones. Ablations show that segmentation, the event dependency graph, and the failure propagation graph each contribute to attribution accuracy. Removing propagation relations lowers exact step accu-racy even when the adjudication set is held fixed, supporting their role in identifying the decisive error. Downstream experiments integrate DeFA’s diagnostic feedback into Trace2Skill, improving test accuracy over the native pipeline by 6–15 percentage points across human-written and model-generated initial skills and demonstrating its utility for agent improvement on subsequent tasks. Our contributions are: • We introduce a failure attribution framework that combines protocol relations and seman-tic dependencies into an event dependency graph to identify suspicious events, trace their contribution to task failure, and locate the decisive error and responsible agent. • We develop a failure propagation graph that connects suspicious events with their up-stream sources and downstream effects, tracing how errors are introduced, transmitted, and adopted to support decisive-error attribution. • We demonstrate gains in precise attribution across text and multimodal evaluations, and show that DeFA’s diagnostic feedback improves downstream skill evolution by 6–15 per-centage points over native Trace2Skill. 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Execution trajectory evaluation and analysis. Agent evaluation includes response judg-ing (Zheng et al., 2023), intermediate work and competency assessment through Agent-as-a-Judge and AgentDiagnose (Zhuge et al., 2025; Ou et al., 2025), and reasoning correctness and error local-ization through process supervision and ProcessBench (Lightman et al., 2024; Zheng et al., 2025). MAST characterizes design, coordination, and verification failures (Cemri et al., 2025); ToolEmu evaluates risk in emulated tool environments (Ruan et al., 2024); and Zhao et al. (2026) study coding-agent failure development and recovery. 

Failure diagnosis and attribution frameworks. Who&When formalizes responsible-agent and decisive-step attribution with global, sequential, and binary-search judges (Zhang et al., 2025). TRAIL supplies a taxonomy and human-annotated diagnostic traces (Deshpande et al., 2025); Who&When Pro uses controlled error injection across modalities (Liu et al., 2026). AgentRx supplies validated constraint violations to an attribution judge (Barke et al., 2026), Ver-ifyMAS tests taxonomy-derived hypotheses (Qiao et al., 2026), and DeepDebug combines global reading, focused investigation, and cross-examination (Zhu et al., 2026). ErrorProbe detects lo-cal anomalies using a failure taxonomy and traces backward from observed symptoms to focus the investigation. Specialized agents then propose, investigate, and verify error hypotheses using executable evidence (Li et al., 2026). CockpitHAT performs hierarchical diagnosis for embodied multi-agent cockpits, integrating dialogue and other execution evidence with a panel of analysts. Its consensus procedure weighs analyst confidence and emphasizes failures with greater safety con-sequences (Wang et al., 2026). EDGE studies attribution of multiple error categories within a tra-jectory, combining a two-stage LLM judge for detection with counterfactual rollout analysis for interpreting errors and potential repairs (Hou et al., 2026). 2Additional executions provide another source of evidence: AgenTracer constructs training super-vision through counterfactual replay and fault injection (Zhang et al., 2026), FAMAS estimates suspiciousness from variation across repeated executions (Ge et al., 2026), and REFLECT and CausalFlow assess responsibility through patched replay or step-level interventions (Lin et al., 2026; Bonagiri et al., 2026). 

Diagnostic feedback for agent improvement. Self-Refine iteratively revises outputs using feed-back (Madaan et al., 2023); Reflexion carries verbal feedback into later trials (Shinn et al., 2023). Trace2Skill consolidates trajectory lessons into reusable skills (Ni et al., 2026), including for Spread-sheetBench tasks (Ma et al., 2024). We evaluate DeFA’s diagnostic feedback within this skill evolu-tion process.

Figure 1: Overview of DeFA’s dependency-guided failure attribution pipeline. 3.2 EVENT DEPENDENCY GRAPH CONSTRUCTION 

The event dependency graph represents each step as a node in V = {1, . . . , T } and combines pro-tocol relations with semantic dependencies. It records how steps pass and use instructions, results, and state. Protocol relations capture interactions defined by the agent framework’s conventions, such as task delegation and its execution, or tool requests and their results. Semantic dependencies capture how later steps refer to or use earlier content, such as adopting a previous conclusion or using a retrieved value in a calculation. 3Protocol relation recovery. DeFA first compresses oversized step content into a normalized tra-jectory ˜τ , preserving step identities and actors under instructions to retain facts, provenance, and action status. An LLM then induces protocol rules from message roles, formats, and interaction patterns. Each rule specifies source and target matching conditions, a pairing procedure, and the resulting relation. A deterministic interpreter scans ˜τ to form the protocol edge set Ep: each edge 

(i, j ) links a source step to a matched later step, such as a tool request to its result or a delegation to its execution. The interpreter supports next-match, all-match, FIFO, and LIFO pairing to accom-modate different interaction patterns. These edges guide segmentation and complement semantic dependencies. 

Segmentation and context. DeFA forms M consecutive segments S = ( S1, . . . , S M ) under step and content budgets, adjusting boundaries using Ep to preserve related interactions where possible. For multiple segments, it generates a narrative summary uj of each. The analysis view Vj combines the full normalized content of Sj with the other summaries:

An LLM adjudicator J selects the decisive error from Vf using the task, failure propagation graph, and trajectory evidence view Z. For a single segment, Z is the complete normalized trajectory; otherwise, it contains all segment summaries in execution order and the full normalized content of each step in Vf .After identifying why the task failed, the adjudicator uses step content and propagation relations to compare plausible errors and their effects on the outcome. For each plausible root cause, it assesses whether a correct alternative was feasible at that point and whether substituting it, with later execution continuing from the corrected state, would turn failure into success. When several steps qualify, it compares how directly each explains the failure and the supporting evidence. It selects ˆt and classifies that step’s own error under Y:

Illustrative attribution. In an illustrative Who&When example, WebSurfer retrieves a film-ranking table at step 12, Assistant uses it to compare two rankings at step 16, and step 19 outputs the resulting count. The failure propagation graph connects these steps as 12 → 16 → 19 . Comparing the retrieved table with Assistant’s interpretation reveals that the table contains the needed entry, but Assistant omits it from the comparison. The missing film is Demon Slayer, whose inclusion changes the answer from five shared films to the correct count of six. DeFA attributes the failure to step 16, matching the benchmark annotation. The connected evidence identifies where the available information was used incorrectly. Specifically, the retrieved domestic ranking places Demon Slayer tenth and Wonder Woman 1984 immediately below it. Assistant includes the latter in the domestic top ten, creating the mismatch used in the overlap calculation. 

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

4.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmarks. We evaluate on Who&When (Zhang et al., 2025) and Who&When Pro (Liu et al., 2026). Who&When contains 184 annotated failure trajectories from algorithm-generated and hand-crafted multi-agent systems; we pool both subsets for evaluation. From Pro, we select 300 text trajectories spanning eight single- and multi-agent frameworks, stratified by framework and failure-mode group with additional coverage constraints on trajectory length and error position. All methods use the same evaluation trajectories. 

Baselines and backbones. We compare DeFA with seven baselines: All-at-Once, Step-by-Step, and Binary Search from Who&When (Zhang et al., 2025); AgentRx (Barke et al., 2026); DeepDe-bug, the diagnostic component of AgentDebugX (Zhu et al., 2026); ErrorProbe (Li et al., 2026); and CockpitHAT (Wang et al., 2026). We evaluate every method with GLM-5.2, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, and Qwen3.8-Max on both text benchmarks. For each backbone, all methods use the same model for every LLM invocation, with temperature set to 0 and greedy decoding. 

Metrics and scoring. We evaluate attribution along three dimensions: responsible agent, error step, and failure mode. Agent measures responsible-agent accuracy on trajectories from multi-agent frameworks. It uses all 184 Who&When trajectories and, following Pro’s official Who evaluation protocol, 89 Pro text trajectories, excluding Smolagents, ALFAgent, and MathChat. Step measures exact error-step localization accuracy; Step ±1 and Step ±3 allow deviations of up to one and three steps, respectively. These metrics use all 184 Who&When and all 300 Pro text trajectories. Mode

## 5 CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS 

We introduced DeFA, which traces agent failures through step dependencies. It combines proto-col relations and semantic dependencies into an event dependency graph spanning the trajectory to capture how steps use earlier information. DeFA then identifies events that may violate task require-ments and traces their sources and effects to construct a failure propagation graph. Final attribution uses this connected evidence to compare how steps introduce, transmit, or adopt errors and identify the decisive error and responsible agent. Experiments on Who&When and Who&When Pro demonstrate precise attribution gains across backbones, trajectory lengths, and text and multimodal inputs. Ablations support segmentation, the event dependency graph, and the failure propagation graph. DeFA’s diagnostic feedback also improves test accuracy after Trace2Skill skill evolution by an average of 9.0 percentage points over the native pipeline, showing that the diagnoses can guide skill improvements for subsequent tasks. 

Limitations. DeFA’s semantic dependency recovery and failure propagation analysis depend on the model’s trajectory interpretation; missing or misidentified relations can affect attribution, es-pecially in long executions with dispersed evidence. Downstream utility has been evaluated for spreadsheet skill evolution; effectiveness in other domains and forms of agent improvement remains to be assessed.
