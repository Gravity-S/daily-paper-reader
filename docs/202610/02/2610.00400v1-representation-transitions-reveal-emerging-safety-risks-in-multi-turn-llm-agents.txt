# Abstract 

Multi-turn attacks on agentic systems can compose individually permissible ac-tions into harmful outcomes, challenging defenses that assess actions or states in isolation. We show that such attacks leave a detectable signature in the agent’s internal representations: harmful behavior emerges as an accumulated represen-tation transition across context updates, whose triggering context can be identified from the same signal. We further find that naive aggregation is confounded by benign representation drift, as a contrastive safety direction need not assign zero to benign transitions. We address this by denoising the direction, anchoring benign traffic at zero and removing its leading variation directions, with no runtime cost. These findings motivate DART, a runtime framework that detects and attributes representation shifts and intervenes with targeted reminders. Across six models and two multi-turn benchmarks, DART reduces attack success from 84% to 25% 

on MT-AgentRisk, catching every attack at a mean false-alarm rate of 12% , and from 97% to 52% on ASEval, at costs in benign non-refusal of 8% and 0% 

respectively. On MT-AgentRisk, it outperforms ToolShield, the state-of-the-art multi-turn defense, on all six models: under the same protocol, ToolShield reaches only 55% . Denoising is critical: on ASEval, the undenoised monitor catches only 

7% –40% of attacks, while the denoised monitor catches 60% –85% . The same monitor covers single-turn indirect injection without modification and adds only 

0.14 –0.56 s overhead per monitored step without requiring an auxiliary model, making it a lightweight complement to computation-heavy speculative defenses. 

# 1 Introduction 

Multi-turn attacks pose a fundamental challenge to runtime safety for agentic LLM systems: harmful behavior can emerge from the composition of individually permissible steps. An attack may first induce an agent to retrieve or transform information and only later use that information for a harmful action, such as exfiltrating emails or redirecting a payment (Debenedetti et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026b). Thus, the risk may not be evident in any individual action or execution state, but only in how the agent’s behavior evolves across turns. Existing runtime defenses primarily monitor individual states or actions. I/O guardrails classify newly received context or generated outputs (Inan et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2024; Team, 2025d), while action-level enforcement and monitoring approaches assess proposed tool calls or their associated safety risks (Wang et al., 2026a;b; Xiang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025). These approaches can identify unsafe states or actions, but have limited effectiveness against risks assembled across permissible 1arXiv:2610.00400v1 [cs.LG] 30 Sep 2026 Preprint. Under review.

Recent work aims to address the issue by incorporating temporal information in different ways. ToolShield (Li et al., 2026b) augments the agent with tool-specific safety knowledge obtained before execution, while Speculative Safety Honeypot (SSH) (Wang et al., 2026c) uses auxiliary LLMs to predict future behaviors and construct a speculative trajectory tree. Both extend beyond single-step detection, but rely on precomputed knowledge or additional-model prediction rather than directly observing how the agent’s behavior changes as new context arrives. Inspired by Representation Engineering (Zou et al., 2023), we ask whether the emergence of unsafe behavior can be observed from how an agent’s internal representation changes across turns. We reveal that harmful multi-turn behavior induces representation transitions toward a safety-relevant direction, and the transition identifies both whether the agent is shifting toward unsafe behavior and which context update coincides with the shift. However, directly aggregating these transitions is confounded by benign representation drift, whose small per-turn contributions can accumulate and overwhelm a sparse attack. We therefore construct a denoised safety direction that anchors benign traffic at zero and projects out its dominant directions of variation. This makes the aggregate transition a robust signal of emerging unsafe behavior, with no additional runtime computation. These findings motivate DART ( Detect, Attribute, and Remind over representation Transitions; Figure 1), an end-to-end runtime safety framework against multi-turn agent attacks. DART projects each context-induced representation transition onto the denoised safety direction, aggregates the scores along the trajectory, and intervenes when the aggregate exceeds a calibrated threshold. DART identifies the triggering context, and an attributed reminder provides safety guidance while preserving the context and allowing execution to continue. We evaluate DART online across six models on two agentic multi-turn benchmarks, without looka-head. On MT-AgentRisk (Li et al., 2026b), DART catches every harmful trajectory and reduces attack success from 84% to 25% at an 8% utility cost, outperforming ToolShield’s 55% ; action-level enforcement fails because each step is individually permissible. On ASEval (Ma et al., 2026), where sparse attacks are diluted within benign trajectories, DART reduces attack success from 97% to 52% 

with no utility loss or false alarms. Denoising exposes this sparse signal, raising catch from 7% –

40% to 60% –85% . Complementary to speculative simulation (Wang et al., 2026c), DART reuses the agent’s hidden states, adding only 0.14 –0.56 s per step versus 6.7 s for simulation. 2Preprint. Under review. 

# 2 Preliminaries 

Agents and representation transitions. We consider an LLM agent that interacts with users, tools, memory, retrieved documents, and its environment to complete a task. During execution, the agent receives a sequence of context segments 

x = ⟨c0, . . . , c n⟩,

where each ci denotes a newly received piece of context—a user turn, tool result, retrieved document, memory item, or environmental observation— together with whatever the agent itself generated since the previous segment (its reasoning and its tool call). Bundling the agent’s own generation into the segment that follows it makes ⟨c0, . . . , c n⟩ an ordered partition of the entire token stream, with no tokens left between consecutive segments; this is what makes the per-segment scores telescope exactly in §4. The agent’s generation is never scored as a segment of its own, and every monitored quantity is read at a segment boundary. These contexts induce a sequence of internal representations

δi = hi+1 − hi.

Following common practice, we use the hidden state of the last token at a fixed layer ℓ as the representation of the agent’s current context (Barbero et al., 2024). Because hi+1 and hi are last-token states of sequences of different length , δi carries position- and length-dependent artifacts—rotary position encoding, and the depth- and length-dependent growth of the state’s norm—alongside the semantic contribution of ci. These artifacts are not incidental: §3 shows that they are exactly what a naive running sum accumulates, which is why the denoising step there is necessary rather than cosmetic. Our central question is whether the emergence of unsafe behavior can be detected from these transitions. We seek a scalar signal that (1) accumulates evidence across individually permissible steps, (2) identifies the context responsible for an emerging shift, and (3) supports runtime intervention without unnecessarily disrupting benign task execution. 

Agent misbehavior and its defense. LLM agents can be driven toward unsafe actions by runtime context. Indirect prompt injection places malicious instructions in tool results, retrieved pages, or memory (Abdelnabi et al., 2023; Perez & Ribeiro, 2022), a threat studied extensively in tool-agent benchmarks (Zhan et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2025; Ruan et al., 2024; Debenedetti et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026b). Agents may also misuse tools without an injection, performing unsafe or unauthorized actions across a task (Zhang et al., 2024; Li et al., 2026b; Yuan et al., 2024; Andriushchenko et al., 2025). Existing defenses include guardrail models that classify inputs and outputs (Inan et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2024; Team, 2025d), runtime enforcement and monitoring approaches that assess actions or their associated safety risks (Wang et al., 2026a;b; Xiang et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025), and monitors that inspect reasoning traces or generated tokens (Zhang et al., 2025; Korbak et al., 2025). These approaches largely judge individual states—messages, actions, or tokens—and may miss risk that emerges gradually across otherwise permissible steps. This motivates DART to monitor the representational transition induced by each context segment, detecting and attributing behavioral shifts as they form. 

Internal monitors over trajectories. Existing internal monitors either introduce an additional model or operate within a single context window. TRACES (Li et al., 2026a) learns prefix-level risk states from a trained observer LLM, whereas Rennervate (Zhong et al., 2026) uses token-level attention features to detect and sanitize prompt injections within one context. DART instead reuses the acting agent’s forward pass and monitors representational transitions across context updates, targeting trajectory-level harm arising from sparse injections or individually permissible turns. 3Preprint. Under review. 

Representation engineering. Linear activation directions can decode safety-relevant properties and steer model behavior (Azaria & Mitchell, 2023; Zou et al., 2023; Rimsky et al., 2024; Li et al., 2023; Turner et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024), including refusal (Arditi et al., 2024). However, a predictive direction need not provide a reliable causal control mechanism (Belinkov, 2022; Hewitt & Liang, 2019; Elazar et al., 2021). This read–steer distinction motivates DART to use representation transitions for detection and attribution, while mitigating risk through a targeted reminder grounded in the attributed context.

Reminder as enforcement. For the attributed segment c⋆, DART constructs a reminder ρ that identifies the contributing content and instructs the agent how to interpret it. For retrieved documents or tool outputs, for example, the content should be treated as untrusted data rather than instructions. 

ρ(c⋆) = “The requests in this conversation are assembling toward an unsafe outcome; this step in particular contributes to it: {c⋆}. Do not carry out that step or the wider task, and say you cannot help.” The context becomes

where ⊕ denotes context concatenation. The agent regenerates before its next action, after which monitoring resumes. We use a reminder because it adds rather than removes context, making the same intervention applicable to both agent-external content and user turns. We do not halt execution, as doing so would sacrifice agent autonomy and prevent completion of benign tasks under attack. 

# 5 Experiments 

5.1 Setup 

Models and benchmarks. We evaluate six locally hosted 8-bit models through MLX (Team, 2025b): Qwen3-8B, -14B, and -32B; Qwen3-30B-A3B ( ∼3B active parameters) Team (2025c); Llama-3.1-8B Llama Team (2024); and Mistral-Small-24B Team (2025a). We learn one safety direction per model because hidden dimensions and depths differ. We use two agentic multi-turn benchmarks with contrasting signal density k/T . MT-AgentRisk (Li et al., 2026b) decomposes harmful tasks into individually permissible user turns for a tool-using agent (filesystem, terminal, SQL database, and web browser), with each harmful conversation paired with a benign one using the same tools; every turn advances the attack ( k/T = 1 ). ASEval (Ma et al., 2026) instead injects instructions into otherwise benign tool-using trajectories, each paired with its benign source trajectory. A run averages 6.7 monitored segments—user turns and tool responses—of which 1.8 are injected ( k/T = 0 .26 ). 

Metrics. We report detection as catch , the fraction of harmful trajectories flagged, and realized FPR on benign trajectories; AUROC is used only for layer selection and in §3. For mitigation, ASR is the fraction of harmful trajectories whose final response does not refuse, and Utility is the fraction of benign trajectories whose final response does not refuse. These residual failures arise from ineffective intervention rather than missed detection, motivating stronger mitigation strategies for multi-turn agent attacks in the future. 5.3 Comparison with Existing Defenses Step-wise defenses do not naturally capture decomposition attacks, where harm emerges across individually permissible actions. AgentSpec (Wang et al., 2026a), for example, checks individual tool calls against hand-written rules. Since each call can satisfy its rule in isolation, it does not fire on these attacks (Appendix B). This highlights a limitation of action-level enforcement: it cannot directly capture safety properties that emerge only across a trajectory. ToolShield (Li et al., 2026b), released with MT-AgentRisk, is a closer baseline. It explores tools offline, distills per-tool safety experiences, and places them in the agent context as fixed guidelines. Like our attributed reminder, it intervenes through context rather than model modification or exe-cution blocking. Unlike DART, however, its guidance is fixed before execution and continuously applied, so it cannot identify when or which newly acquired context drives a trajectory toward harm. We reproduce its published experiences and prompt format with the same system prompt and deterministic decoding as DART. On MT-AgentRisk, ToolShield reduces average ASR from 84% to 55% , whereas DART reduces it to 25% , outperforming ToolShield on all six models (one-sided sign test over models, p = 0 .016 ). ToolShield’s utility is slightly higher ( 91% against 85% ), consistent with its guidance being always-on and never trajectory-gated. # 6 Conclusion 

We presented DART, a runtime defense that detects emerging multi-turn safety risks from represen-tation transitions, attributes them to the contributing context, and intervenes with targeted reminders. Across six models and two agentic benchmarks, DART substantially reduces attack success while preserving benign utility, including attacks composed entirely of individually permissible steps. Denoising is critical for separating sparse harmful shifts from accumulated benign drift, enabling reliable trajectory-level monitoring at low runtime cost. By reusing the agent’s hidden states, DART provides a lightweight safety layer that can operate independently or complement heavier defenses such as speculative simulation.

When denoising is needed. Two diagnostics decide whether a direction is healthy, and they differ in what they cost to obtain. The first, the benign drift E[r | benign ], needs only benign calibration traffic : it can be read before deployment, without any attack and without labels. The second, the share of harmful peaks at t=0 , needs labelled harmful trajectories and is therefore available only where a red-team calibration set exists—which, in our protocol, it does, because the same 30+30 

fitting split that supplies the contrast also supplies these traces. We are explicit about this because the two are sometimes run together: the drift alone is the deployable screen, the peak timing is the confirmation, and a purely unsupervised deployment gets the former only. Where the drift is the only diagnostic available, it should be treated as necessary but not sufficient (see the Llama-3.1-8B case below). The drift is large and negative on ASEval, where denoising is what makes the aggregate usable; −2.48 on MT-AgentRisk Qwen3-32B, with 17% of peaks at the opening segment, where it cuts false alarms (§5.4); −0.32 on MT-AgentRisk Qwen3-8B, where it changes nothing; and positive (+2 .6, +5 .0) on ActorBreaker, whose plain direction already satisfies the condition. The drift alone is not sufficient, however: with the ASEval contrast held fixed, the plain direction on Llama-3.1-8B has benign drift of only −0.72 yet peaks on the opening segment in 60% of harmful runs. We therefore report the share of peaks at t=0 alongside the drift wherever harmful traces are available, and treat a direction as healthy only when both are near zero; with benign traffic alone, a near-zero drift is a necessary condition that the Llama case shows is not sufficient.

# B Rule-based baseline: AgentSpec 

Baseline: rule-based enforcement (AgentSpec). A natural alternative to a learned monitor is a hand-written action policy. AgentSpec (Wang et al., 2026a) intercepts each tool call and blocks it when a state-changing action targets an entity the user’s task and environment never authorized (a per-suite whitelist), or performs an unrequested self-modification. In-domain and single-turn it is very strong : on slack it blocks every attack that emits a violating action, driving ASR to 0.00 for all six models (Table 7)—on the weak-gate models it thereby beats the drift monitor, whose trajectory-level recall is lower. However, it exhibits limitations along three axes that the representational signal avoids. (i) No precision knob: a fixed rule blocks whenever it fires, and a whitelist learned from one model’s clean behavior mis-fires on another’s—on Llama and Mistral it over-blocks 50 –90% 

of benign slack runs, collapsing utility to 0.40 and 0.10 , where the monitor’s τ holds utility at its no-intervention level. (ii) No cross-domain transfer: its two per-suite artifacts—the guarded tool set and the whitelist—do not carry over. Authored on one suite and applied to another, catch collapses to ≈ 0 (Table 6), because a rule for slack’s send_channel_message is blind to workspace’s send_email ; the very held-out slack that needs a slack-authored rule is detected by our direction fit leave-slack-out at 0.96 . (iii) No decomposition coverage: judging actions , it never fires on MT—whose steps emit no violating action—leaving ASR at 0.80 –1.00 while the drift monitor closes it to 0.00 (Table 7). AgentSpec is thus the stronger specialist —one suite, single-turn, overt action—and DART the generalist : comparable where the gate is precise, and uniquely robust to model-, domain-, and attack-shape transfer.

# F Ablations: aggregator, direction, and steering 

Three ablations support the design; we summarise them here and give the detail in the subsections that follow. (i) Transition vs. state: scoring the transition δi beats the accumulated state hi on average (0.925 vs. 0.884 leave-one-suite-out) and stabilizes the fragile folds, justifying it as the default. 

(ii) What the direction encodes: controlled probes show it fires on any injected imperative—a benign-goal injection in the same wrapper is flagged 98% of the time—and is not a length detector, so it is primarily structural , with a weaker but real harm-correlated component. (iii) Reader, and deliberately not an actuator: a one-line reminder dominates every soft-suffix steering variant, and a random -direction suffix matches steering along u, so the suffix’s effect is token perturbation rather than displacement along the direction. Direct residual-stream steering does do causal work—at a hand-tuned mid layer under strong over-steering it reaches 0.68 clean flip, above the reminder’s 

0.52 —but it is confined to that window, adds two uncalibrated hyper-parameters beside a conformally calibrated threshold, discards the attribution, and leaves no auditable record. Why we act on the context rather than on the model. Efficacy is not why DART declines to steer, and we are explicit that on the injected-run trade-off steering can win. The reason is architectural. An edit to the residual stream is blind to which segment fired: it discards the attribution the monitor has just computed, and it leaves no trace in the transcript, so nothing records that enforcement occurred, on what evidence, or to what effect. A quoted span (the deployed reminder, §4) or a withheld segment (the read-level fallback of Appendix F.4) is inspectable, reviewable, and reversible; a steering edit is none of these, and it cannot supply the provenance on which either enforcement lever depends. Steering also adds two uncalibrated hyper-parameters—layer and α—beside a threshold that carries a conformal false-alarm guarantee. This is consistent with what u is: difference-of-means maximizes 

correlation with the injected/clean label (§F.2: largely structural), which yields an excellent readout with no guarantee that displacing along it reconstructs correct behavior (Belinkov, 2022; Elazar et al., 2021), even where—as here—it demonstrably moves the action. What to do when quoting backfires. Table 17 puts the reminder beside two levers that do not depend on the model reading a quotation correctly. Halting forces a refusal on a flag. Read-level withholding replaces the attributed segment with a placeholder noting that a read was withheld, re-anchors the accumulator at the post-removal state, and lets the agent continue; it needs read-level attribution, which is measured at 1.00 on these runs, but no sub-segment localisation. Three findings. (i) The reminder is capability-gated, not broken : it takes ASR to 0.00 –0.10 on four of six models and to 0.00 on both InjecAgent families for the 14B, but rises above the no-intervention baseline on Qwen3-8B ( 0.23 → 0.29 on AgentDojo, 0.33 → 0.87 on InjecAgent ds) and Llama-3.1-8B ( 0.07 → 0.38 ). (ii) Withholding is uniformly reliable : ASR ≤ 0.09 on every model and both benchmarks, including every cell where the reminder inverts. (iii) It is not free : the reminder preserves more task completion than withholding on five of six models, and halting—which is the most reliable of all at ASR 0.00 —is also by far the most expensive, leaving 0.00 –0.24 completion on the flagged runs against the reminder’s 0.37 –0.71 .This gives a deployment rule we can state without tuning: use the attributed reminder where the model is strong enough to read a quotation as a negative example, and fall back to read-level withholding where it is not. Which regime a model is in is measurable before deployment, on the same red-team calibration set that fits the direction—issue the reminder on the calibration attacks and check whether ASR goes down. We report the two levers side by side rather than folding the choice into DART itself, because the gate is a property of the model rather than of the monitor. 

# G Monitoring overhead 

What is measured. Per monitored segment, DART performs one forward pass over the context to read hi+1 at the layer ℓ⋆, and one dot product against u. Figure 6 reports the wall-clock cost of both against the cost of the agent step they sit beside, on one Apple M3 Ultra ( 96 GB unified memory) under MLX with 8-bit weights, taking the median over MT-AgentRisk conversations. The agent step is fixed at 256 generated tokens so the denominator is stable across models; the monitor’s cost is the cost of a prefill over the current context, which grows with the conversation, so the reported figure is the median over all monitored steps of all conversations rather than a single-length measurement. Contexts in this setting run to a few thousand tokens by the final turn. 

KV-cache and why this is an upper bound. We measure the monitor as a separate prefill: it re-reads the context through the same weights, with its own cache, and does not reuse the agent loop’s KV-cache. This is the pessimistic implementation, and we report it because it is the one that requires no change to the serving stack. Memory. The monitor adds no resident model: it reuses the agent’s own weights, so the only additional state is the reading direction u (one vector of the model’s hidden width: 4096 floats on Qwen3-8B, 5120 on Qwen3-32B, i.e. tens of kilobytes) and the transient activations of the extra prefill, which are released immediately. Peak memory over the agent’s own requirement was within measurement noise in our runs. This is the cost line on which DART differs most sharply from speculative simulation (§5.3), which must hold a second model resident and run it several times per step. 26
